7.5  The Rationals Inside the Cuts, and a Complete Ordered Field

This section is part of the optional Chapter 7, and nothing outside the chapter depends on it. Section 7.4 finished the arithmetic: by Theorem 7.4.7 the cuts form an ordered field RD, with zero D0 and one D1. Three questions are left before RD can be called a model of the real numbers. Do the rational cuts Dq form a faithful copy of Q, with the arithmetic of Q? Is the new field complete? And does the cut σ of Example (The cut of the gap), built to sit at the gap of Q, really square to D2? This section answers all three with yes. On the Cauchy road, Section 5.4 asked the first question about the map κ (Theorem 5.4.1), and Sections 5.5 and 5.6 together answered the second; here the second answer is already in hand, from Section 7.2.

7.5.1. A copy of the rationals

Every ordered field F contains a copy of Q: by Theorem 4.2.2 there is exactly one map ιF:Q→F that sends 1 to 1F and respects sums and products, and it is strictly increasing. For F=RD we have a candidate in hand, the map q↦Dq of Definition 7.1.2. It sends 1 to D1, the one of the field. The theorem below checks the rest: sums and positive products by computing with sets, negatives and the other signs by the field rules; and the uniqueness clause then says the candidate is ιRD.

Theorem 7.5.1 (The Rationals Inside the Cuts)

The map Q→RD, q↦Dq, is injective, and for all p,q∈Q:

  • (a) p<q if and only if Dp<Dq;
  • (b) Dp+Dq=Dp+q;
  • (c) −Dq=D−q;
  • (d) DpDq=Dpq;
  • (e) D1 is the one of the ordered field RD of Theorem 7.4.7.

Consequently Dq=ιRD(q) for every q∈Q, where ιRD is the map of Theorem 4.2.2.

Idea. The identity (b), and (d) for positive p,q, are equalities of sets of rationals, so each has two halves. The ⊆ halves are closure: if r<p and s<q, then r+s<p+q. The ⊇ halves must split a given rational x<p+q as a sum of something below p and something below q, and the obvious split x=p+(x−p) fails, because p∉Dp. So we take a little off both summands: half the gap p+q−x from each. For products we do the same with a factor instead of a summand. Signs other than “both positive” are reduced to that case, the chapter’s named move of reducing to the positive case (Section 7.4); since RD is already known to be a field, the ring rules for signs do the reduction.

Proof. Throughout, RD is an ordered field (Theorem 7.4.7), so the rules of Proposition 2.3.5 hold in it as well as in Q.

(a) If p<q, then p∈Dq, so Dp⊊Dq by Proposition 7.1.2 (a) applied to the cut Dq; that is, Dp<Dq (Definition 7.2.1). Conversely, if Dp⊊Dq, then Proposition 7.1.2 (a), applied to the cut Dq and the rational p, gives p∈Dq, that is, p<q.

Injectivity. If p≠q, then p<q or q<p by trichotomy (O1) in Q, and (a) gives Dp≠Dq in either case.

(b) (⊆) An element of Dp+Dq is r+s with r<p and s<q (Definition 7.3.1), and r+s<p+q by Proposition 2.3.5 (b). (⊇) Let x∈Q with x<p+q, and put δ:=p+q−x2>0. Then p−δ<p and q−δ<q, so p−δ∈Dp and q−δ∈Dq, and (p−δ)+(q−δ)=p+q−2δ=x. Hence x∈Dp+Dq.

(c) By (b), Dq+D−q=Dq+(−q)=D0. By Theorem 7.3.3, also Dq+(−Dq)=D0. The negative of an element of the commutative ring RD is unique (Proposition 2.3.2 (a)), so −Dq=D−q.

(d), both positive. Let p>0 and q>0. By (a), Dp>D0 and Dq>D0, so the product is that of Definition 7.4.1: DpDq={x∈Q:x≤rs for some r∈Dp, s∈Dq with r>0, s>0}. (⊆) If 0<r<p and 0<s<q, then rs<ps<pq by Proposition 2.3.5 (c), used twice. So x≤rs gives x<pq.

(⊇) Let x<pq. If x≤0, take r:=p/2 and s:=q/2: they are positive, lie in Dp and Dq, and x≤0<rs. If 0<x<pq, put λ:=12(1+xpq),r:=λp,s:=xλp. Since 0<x/(pq)<1, the midpoint λ satisfies x/(pq)<λ<1. Hence 0<r<p; and s>0, with s<q because this is equivalent, after multiplying by λp>0, to x<λpq, that is, to x/(pq)<λ. So r∈Dp, s∈Dq, both are positive, and rs=x. Hence x∈DpDq.

(d), the other signs. If p=0 or q=0, then one factor is D0, so DpDq=D0 by Definition 7.4.2, and Dpq=D0. For negative entries, (c) gives Dp=−D−p for every p, since −(−α)=α (Proposition 2.3.2 (a)). If p<0<q, then by the sign rule (−a)b=−(ab) of Proposition 2.3.2 (c) in RD (in the cut model it is part of Lemma 7.4.4) and the positive case, DpDq=(−D−p)Dq=−(D−pDq)=−D−pq=Dpq, the last step by (c). The case q<0<p follows by commutativity of the product. If p<0 and q<0, then the rule (−a)(−b)=ab of Proposition 2.3.2 (c) and the positive case give DpDq=(−D−p)(−D−q)=D−pD−q=D(−p)(−q)=Dpq.

(e) This is part of Theorem 7.4.7.

The consequence. By (e), (b) and (d), the map q↦Dq sends 1 to the one of RD and respects sums and products. By the uniqueness clause of Theorem 4.2.2, applied to the ordered field F=RD, it is ιRD. This proves the theorem.

So the rationals live inside RD as the rational cuts, with their order and arithmetic unchanged. From now on, a computation with rational cuts may be done in Q and carried over: D1/2+D1/3=D5/6, D−2D3=D−6, D2−1=D1/2. The last one holds because D2D1/2=D1 by (d), and in a field the inverse is the only element with that property (Lemma 4.1.1 (a)).

Quick check. In the (⊇) half of (b), why not write x=p+(x−p)? It has x−p<q.

Solution. Because p∉Dp: the cut Dp consists of the rationals strictly below p. The split must take something from p as well, and δ>0 is that something. The flawed proof of Exercise C2 of Section 7.3 falls into the same trap, with 0∉D0.

On the Cauchy road the same theorem was Theorem 5.4.1, and its proof was a computation with constant sequences. There the sum of two constant sequences is the constant sequence of the sum, termwise. Here the sum of two rational cuts is not obviously a rational cut, and the ⊇ half needs the room that δ provides.

Proposition 7.5.2 (A Cut Is the Union of the Rational Cuts Below It) Optional: nothing later depends on this.

For every cut α, α=⋃{Dq:q∈α}=sup{Dq:q∈α}.

Proof. (⊇) If q∈α and r<q, then r∈α by (C2) of Definition 7.1.1; so Dq⊆α. (⊆) If p∈α, then (C3) gives p′∈α with p′>p, and p∈Dp′. This proves the first equality. The set {Dq:q∈α} is non-empty, since α≠∅, and bounded above by α, by the ⊇ half; so by Theorem 7.2.2 its supremum is its union.

The proposition is the cut model’s way of saying that every real number is approached from below by rationals. Nothing later in the chapter uses it.

7.5.2. Between two cuts there is a rational cut

In Q between two rationals lies a third, their midpoint. In an Archimedean field, Theorem 4.2.5 puts a rational between any two elements. For RD we prove the corresponding statement directly from the cut conditions. We do not yet know that RD is Archimedean, and the direct proof is shorter anyway.

Theorem 7.5.3 (The Rational Cuts Are Dense)

Let α and β be cuts with α<β. Then there is q∈Q with α<Dq<β.

Try it first (ten minutes): given cuts α<β, which rational would you put between them, and why is one point of β∖α not quite enough?

Idea. Since α⊊β, there is a rational p in β but not in α, and the natural guess is q=p. By Proposition 7.1.2, p∉α gives α⊆Dp (part (b)), and p∈β gives Dp⊊β (part (a)). So α≤Dp<β. The first inequality need not be strict: for α=D1, β=D2 and p=1, the guess gives Dp=α. The cure is to move up once more inside β: condition (C3) of β gives p′∈β above p, and Dp′ is strictly above Dp, so strictly above α, and still strictly below β.

Proof. Since α⊊β (Definition 7.2.1), there is p∈β with p∉α. By (C3) of Definition 7.1.1 for β, there is p′∈β with p′>p. Then:

  • α⊆Dp, by Proposition 7.1.2 (b), since p∉α;
  • Dp⊊Dp′, by 1 (a), since p<p′;
  • Dp′⊊β, by Proposition 7.1.2 (a), since p′∈β.

Hence α⊊Dp′⊊β, that is, α<Dp′<β. Take q:=p′.

Each hypothesis has a job. “α<β” supplies p, and the strictness is needed: for α=β there is nothing strictly between. Condition (C3), used for β, supplies the second step up; without it, D1 and the set {r∈Q:r≤1} would be two “cuts”, the first strictly inside the second, with no Dq strictly between them: D1⊊Dq forces q>1, and then Dq contains (1+q)/2>1, which the second set lacks.

Warning. “Dense” does not mean that every cut is rational. The rational cuts are dense, and still σ of Example (The cut of the gap) is not one of them, as Proposition 7.5.5 below proves. Density says only that between two cuts there is a rational cut, never that a cut is one.

Quick check. Name a rational q with σ<Dq<D3/2, and check both inequalities.

Solution. Take q=29/20. Then q>0 and q2=841/400>800/400=2, so q∉σ, and Proposition 7.1.2 (b) gives σ⊆Dq. The inclusion is strict: 10/7>0 and (10/7)2=100/49>98/49=2, so 10/7∉σ, while 10/7<29/20 (200/140<203/140), so 10/7∈Dq. Hence σ<Dq. And 29/20<30/20=3/2, so Dq<D3/2 by 1 (a). The rational q=10/7 works the same way, with 17/12 as the witness of strictness: (17/12)2=289/144>2 and 17/12<10/7 (119/84<120/84).

7.5.3. Archimedes in the cuts

An ordered field is Archimedean (Definition 4.2.2) if no element lies above every n⋅1F, n∈N. In RD, n⋅1=ιRD(n)=Dn, by Theorem 4.2.2 and 1. A cut is bounded inside Q by any rational it misses, and Q is Archimedean, so the property comes almost for free.

Proposition 7.5.4 (The Cuts Are Archimedean) Optional: nothing later depends on this.

For every cut α there is n∈N with α<Dn. Hence RD is an Archimedean ordered field.

Proof. By (C1) of Definition 7.1.1 there is b∈Q with b∉α, and Proposition 7.1.2 (b) gives α⊆Db. By Theorem 2.5.3 there is n∈N with b<n, so Db<Dn by 1 (a). Hence α<Dn=n⋅1RD, which is Definition 4.2.2 for RD.

7.5.4. The square root of two

The cut σ:={p∈Q:p≤0 or p2<2} of Example (The cut of the gap) was introduced as “the rationals below 2”. Now that cuts can be multiplied, that description becomes a theorem: σσ=D2. The proof is the chapter’s multiplicative form of walk up by ε, Lemma 7.4.2: step up by a factor μ>1 until you leave σ.

Proposition 7.5.5 (The Cut of the Square Root of Two)

The cut σ of Example (The cut of the gap) satisfies σ>D0 and σσ=D2. Consequently σ≠Dq for every q∈Q.

Idea. The ⊆ half is a bound: two positive elements of σ have squares below 2, so their product has square below 4. The ⊇ half must write a given x<2 as a product rs of positive elements of σ. Small x are easy: x≤1⋅1. For 1≤x<2 we want r∈σ with r2>x, since then s:=x/r is smaller than r and so lies in σ. Such an r is found by walking up by a factor: if r∈σ and μr∉σ, then r2≥2/μ2, and this exceeds x as soon as μ2<2/x. So we need a rational μ>1 with μ2<2/x, written down without square roots.

Proof. σ>D0. Every p<0 has p≤0, so D0⊆σ; and 0∈σ∖D0. Hence D0⊊σ. So σσ is the product of Definition 7.4.1.

(⊆) Let x∈σσ, so x≤rs with r,s∈σ, r>0, s>0. Since r>0 and r∈σ, r2<2; likewise s2<2. By Proposition 2.3.5 (c), used twice, (rs)2=r2s2<2s2<4=22. Since rs≥0 and 2≥0, Proposition 2.3.5 (g) gives rs<2. So x<2 and x∈D2.

(⊇), small x. Let x<1. Then 1∈σ, since 12=1<2, and x≤1⋅1 with r=s=1 positive. So x∈σσ. This covers every x≤0 as well.

(⊇), 1≤x<2. Put t:=2x−1,μ:=1+t3. Since 1≤x<2, we have 1<2/x≤2, so 0<t≤1 and μ>1. Moreover t2/9<t/3, because this is equivalent to t<3. Hence μ2=1+2t3+t29<1+2t3+t3=1+t=2x. Since σ>D0 and μ>1, Lemma 7.4.2 gives r∈σ with r>0 and μr∉σ. As μr>0 and μr∉σ, the definition of σ gives (μr)2≥2, so r2≥2μ2>x, the second inequality because μ2<2/x. Put s:=x/r. Then s>0, and s<r because x<r2. Since r∈σ, (C2) of Definition 7.1.1 gives s∈σ. Finally rs=x, so x∈σσ.

So σσ=D2.

The consequence. Suppose σ=Dq for some q∈Q. Then D0<Dq, so q>0 by 1 (a). By 1 (d), Dq2=DqDq=σσ=D2, and by injectivity q2=2. This contradicts Theorem 2.6.1. Hence σ≠Dq for every q∈Q.

The constant μ depends on x only, and it is chosen before r: the lemma is applied to the fixed factor μ, and it returns r. For x=3/2, for instance, t=1/3 and μ=10/9, with μ2=100/81<108/81=4/3=2/x. Exercise C1 of Section 7.1 proved σ≠Dq directly from the definition; the proof above gets it as a by-product of σσ=D2 and needs no step below a rational q with q2>2.

Quick check. Why does the case 1≤x<2 need Lemma 7.4.2, while the case x<1 does not? Where in the (⊇) half is the hypothesis x<2 used?

Solution. For x<1 the fixed element 1∈σ already gives x≤1⋅1. For x≥1 no fixed pair works for all x: we need r∈σ with r2>x, and how close to the gap r must be depends on how close x is to 2. The hypothesis x<2 is used to get 2/x>1, so t>0 and μ>1, which the lemma requires.

7.5.5. A complete ordered field

The last question has the shortest answer. Completeness in the sense of Definition 5.6.1 asks that every non-empty subset bounded above have a supremum for the order ≤ of the field. In RD, that order is inclusion (Definition 7.2.1), and Theorem 7.2.2 found the supremum: the union.

Theorem 7.5.6 (The Cuts Form a Complete Ordered Field)

RD, with the sum of Definition 7.3.1, the product of Definition 7.4.2 and the order of Definition 7.2.1, is a complete ordered field (Definition 5.6.1).

Proof. By Theorem 7.4.7, RD is an ordered field. Its relation ≤ (“< or =”, Definition 2.3.2) is “⊊ or =”, that is, ⊆, the order of Definition 7.2.1. Let A⊆RD be non-empty and bounded above for this order. By Theorem 7.2.2, ⋃A is a cut and is the supremum of A in (RD,≤). So every non-empty subset bounded above has a supremum, and RD is complete.

That is the whole proof, and it is the point of the chapter’s comparison. On the Cauchy road, Section 5.5 proved that every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges, and Section 5.6 then needed bisection and the Archimedean property to reach suprema (Theorem 5.6.2). On the Dedekind road the supremum is a union, and all the work went into the arithmetic of Sections 7.3 and 7.4.

With this theorem, Theorem 6.1.2 gives a second proof of Proposition 7.5.4: every complete ordered field is Archimedean. By Theorem 6.3.1, RD is therefore a copy of the field R of Chapter 5, by exactly one order-preserving field isomorphism. Section 7.6 writes that isomorphism down.

7.5.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Which result shows that the map q↦Dq is the map ιRD of Theorem 4.2.2, and which property of ιRD does that argument use?
  • (b) True or false, with a reason from the cut conditions: every cut is a union of rational cuts.
  • (c) In the proof of Theorem 7.5.3, the rational p∈β∖α was not used directly. Give cuts α<β and a rational p∈β∖α with Dp=α.
  • (d) True or false: if α is a cut and α≠Dq for every q∈Q, then αα≠Dr for every r∈Q.

Solution.

  • (a)
    1. Parts (b), (d) and (e) say that q↦Dq respects sums and products and sends 1 to the one of RD; the uniqueness clause of Theorem 4.2.2 says only one map Q→RD does that, namely ιRD.

  • (b) True. Let α be a cut. For q∈α, (C2) gives Dq⊆α, so the union of the Dq with q∈α is contained in α. Conversely, for q∈α, (C3) gives q′∈α with q′>q, and then q∈Dq′. So α is the union of the rational cuts Dq, q∈α. (This is the optional proposition on unions of rational cuts in this section; the argument above does not need it.)

  • (c) α=D1, β=D2, p=1: then 1∈D2, 1∉D1, and Dp=D1=α. So Dp is not strictly above α, and the proof moves up to p′∈β with p′>p.

  • (d) False. σ is not rational (Proposition 7.5.5), and σσ=D2 is.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)

Let p,q∈Q with p<0<q. Prove that DpDq=Dpq directly from the case distinction of Definition 7.4.2, using the positive case of 1 (d) and part (c), but not the ring rules for signs (Proposition 2.3.2 (c), Lemma 7.4.4).

Solution. By 1 (a), Dp<D0<Dq. So neither factor is D0, the signs differ, and Definition 7.4.2 gives DpDq=−(|Dp||Dq|). Since Dq≥D0, |Dq|=Dq. Since Dp<D0, |Dp|=−Dp, and −Dp=D−p by 1 (c). Now −p>0 and q>0, so the positive case of (d) gives D−pDq=D(−p)q=D−pq. Hence DpDq=−D−pq=Dpq, the last step by (c) again.

Exercise (B2)

Let ρ:={p∈Q:p≤0 or p2<3}. Exercise B2 of Section 7.1 shows that ρ is a cut (there the set is written with p<0; since 02<3, it is the same set). Prove that ρ>D0 and ρρ=D3.

Hint: follow Proposition 7.5.5, and check that the same μ still works when 2/x is replaced by 3/x.

Solution. ρ>D0. D0⊆ρ, since p<0 gives p≤0, and 0∈ρ∖D0.

(⊆) If x≤rs with r,s∈ρ positive, then r2<3 and s2<3, so (rs)2=r2s2<3s2<9=32 by Proposition 2.3.5 (c), and rs<3 by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), since rs,3≥0. So x<3.

(⊇) Let x<3. If x<1, then x≤1⋅1 with 1∈ρ. Let 1≤x<3, and put t:=3/x−1 and μ:=1+t/3. Then 1<3/x≤3, so 0<t≤2 and μ>1. Since t<3, t2/9<t/3, and μ2=1+2t3+t29<1+t=3x. By Lemma 7.4.2 (ρ>D0, μ>1) there is r∈ρ, r>0, with μr∉ρ. Then (μr)2≥3, so r2≥3/μ2>x. Put s:=x/r: then 0<s<r, so s∈ρ by (C2), and rs=x. So x∈ρρ.

Hence ρρ=D3.

The only change from σ is the range of t: now t can be as large as 2, and the inequality t2/9<t/3 still holds, because it needs only t<3.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: An irrational cut in every gap)

Prove that between any two cuts α<β lies a cut that is not rational: there is a cut γ with α<γ<β and γ≠Dr for every r∈Q.

Solution (Hint 1). Build an irrational cut between D0 and D1 from σ, then move it with rational coefficients.

Solution (Hint 2). By Theorem 7.5.3, used twice, there are rationals q,q′ with α<Dq<Dq′<β.

Solution. Two rationals. By Theorem 7.5.3 there is q∈Q with α<Dq<β; applied again to Dq<β, it gives q′ with Dq<Dq′<β. By 1 (a), q<q′.

An irrational cut between D0 and D1. Since 12<2, 1∈σ, so D1<σ (Proposition 7.1.2 (a)). Since 3/2>0 and (3/2)2=9/4>2, 3/2∉σ, so σ⊆D3/2 (Proposition 7.1.2 (b)), and D3/2<D2 (1 (a)). Hence D1<σ<D2. Adding −D1=D−1 (translation in the ordered field RD, Theorem 7.4.7, and 1 (b), (c)) gives D0<σ−D1<D1.

The cut. Put γ:=Dq+Dq′−q(σ−D1). Since q′>q, Dq′−q>D0 by 1 (a), and multiplying by it preserves strict inequalities (Proposition 2.3.5 (c) in RD), so D0<Dq′−q(σ−D1)<Dq′−qD1=Dq′−q. Adding Dq gives Dq<γ<Dq+Dq′−q=Dq′. Hence α<Dq<γ<Dq′<β.

γ is not rational. Suppose γ=Dr. In the field RD, Dq′−q≠D0 has the inverse D1/(q′−q) (by (d), Dq′−qD1/(q′−q)=D1). Solving for σ, σ=D1+D1/(q′−q)(Dr−Dq)=D1+(r−q)/(q′−q), by 1 (b), (c), (d). This contradicts Proposition 7.5.5. So γ is not rational.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: two rationals q<q′ with α<Dq<Dq′<β, from density used twice. - 3: a non-rational cut strictly between D0 and D1 (or between any two rational cuts), with both inequalities proved. - 3: the affine image lies strictly between Dq and Dq′, using the order rules of the ordered field RD with a positive factor. - 2: the image is not rational, because rational cuts are closed under the field operations (1). Fatal errors (0 for the step): treating σ as a number “2” with decimal digits; claiming σ−D1<D1 without a rational bound such as 3/2∉σ.

Exercise (C2: A cube root of two)

Let τ:={p∈Q:p≤0 or p3<2}. We show that τ is a cut with τ>D0 and that τττ=D2. Everything below is proved in full except the last case, which is left to you.

Monotone cubing. For rationals 0≤a<b, b3−a3=(b−a)(b2+ab+a2)>0, since b−a>0, b2>0 (Proposition 2.3.5 (e)), ab≥0, a2≥0, and a product of positives is positive (Proposition 2.3.5 (c)). Only non-negative a,b are needed, because of the clause “p≤0 or”.

The ⊇ half, the case 1≤x<2. Let 1≤x<2. Find r∈τ, r>0, with r3>x, and then write x as a product of three positive elements of τ.

Name the move, then complete it.

Solution. The move is walk up by ε in its multiplicative form, Lemma 7.4.2: step up by a fixed factor μ>1 until you leave τ, and choose μ so small that the last step inside is already beyond what we need.
