1.4  Square Roots, Quadratic Equations, and the Missing Order

Logic and Proof, Section 7.1 needed, in one of its checks, the fact that every complex number has a complex square root, and took it there as a school fact about polar form. This section proves it, with no angles at all: a square root of a complex number is built from two square roots of non-negative reals, which Number Systems: From N to R, Chapter 6 provides (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4). The quadratic formula follows, for complex coefficients. Then we see what C has lost on the way from R: it has no order compatible with its arithmetic, and the rule xy=xy has no complex version.

1.4.1. Square roots from real square roots

The question is: given z=a+bi, find w=x+yi with w2=z. Since w2=(x2−y2)+2xyi, comparing real and imaginary parts turns the question into two real equations, x2−y2=a,2xy=b. The answer is an explicit formula in a, b and |z|.

Proposition 1.4.1 (Square Roots of Complex Numbers)

Let z=a+bi∈C with a,b∈R and z≠0, and let r:=|z|. Put s:=1 if b≥0 and s:=−1 if b<0, and x:=r+a2,y:=sr−a2,w:=x+yi. Then:

  • (a) w2=z;
  • (b) for u∈C, u2=z if and only if u=w or u=−w;
  • (c) w≠−w.

For z=0: u2=0 if and only if u=0.

In words: every non-zero complex number has exactly two square roots, w and −w, and the formula finds one of them; 0 has exactly one.

Try it first (ten minutes): find w with w2=3+4i by writing w=x+yi. What do you do with the two equations you get?

Idea. For 3+4i the two equations are x2−y2=3 and 2xy=4. The natural attempt eliminates y=2/x and arrives at x4−3x2−4=0, a quartic; it can be solved, but it hides what is going on, and in general it needs the quadratic formula before we have it. The missing piece is a third equation, and it comes from the modulus. If w2=z, then |w|2=|w2|=|z| by Theorem 1.2.2 (d), that is, x2+y2=r. Now the first equation and the third give x2 and y2 by adding and subtracting: x2=(r+a)/2, y2=(r−a)/2. That fixes x and y up to sign, and the second equation 2xy=b says which signs go together: xy has the sign of b. For 3+4i: r=5, x2=4, y2=1, xy>0, so w=±(2+i).

The proof runs this forward: define x and y by the formulas, check w2=z, and get “only ±w” from the field axioms.

Before reading on: why does the sign of b decide whether x and y have the same sign?

The proof uses one small rule about real square roots: for p,q∈R with p,q≥0, pq=pq. Both sides are non-negative (a product of non-negative reals is non-negative), and the square of the left side is (p)2(q)2=pq, by commutativity; so the left side is the non-negative number whose square is pq, which is the right side by the uniqueness in Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4. The rule is about non-negative reals only; the last part of this section shows that it has no complex version.

Proof. The roots exist. By Lemma 1.3.1, |a|=|Rez|≤r, so −r≤a≤r (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 4.1.3 (d)). Hence r+a≥0 and r−a≥0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (a), (b)). Halving keeps this: for p≥0, either p>0 and then p/2>0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 4.1.2 (b), with ε=p), or p=0 and then p/2=0⋅(1/2)=0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (b)). So (r+a)/2≥0 and (r−a)/2≥0. So the two square roots in the definition of x and y exist (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4).

  • (a) Since s2=1, x2−y2=r+a2−s2r−a2=r+a2−r−a2=a. For the imaginary part, the rule for real square roots gives 2xy=2sr+a2r−a2=2sr2−a24=sr2−a2=sb2=s|b|=b. Here the third equality is 2t/4=4t/4=t, by the same rule and 4=2; the fourth uses r2=a2+b2 (Definition 1.2.1); the fifth holds because |b|≥0 and |b|2=b2 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 4.1.3 (a), (b)), with uniqueness in Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4; and the last because s|b|=b by the definitions of s and of |b| (Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 4.1.1), in each of the two cases b≥0 and b<0. Hence w2=(x2−y2)+2xyi=a+bi=z, by the multiplication rule of Definition 1.1.1.

  • (b) Let u∈C. Since C is a commutative ring, (u−w)(u+w)=u2−w2 (Proposition 1.1.2, Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (c)). If u2=z, then u2−w2=z−z=0 by (a), so (u−w)(u+w)=0. Since C is a field (Theorem 1.2.4), it has no zero divisors (Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1 (d)), so u−w=0 or u+w=0, that is, u=w or u=−w. Conversely w2=z by (a), and (−w)2=w2=z by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (c).

  • (c) First w≠0, since w2=z≠0 and 02=0. Suppose w=−w. Then 2w=w+w=0. But 2=1+1≠0 in C: it is the real number 2, which is positive in R (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (h)), and R⊆C by the injective embedding of Proposition 1.1.1. So 2w=0 with 2≠0 and w≠0 contradicts the absence of zero divisors. Hence w≠−w.

Finally, for z=0: 02=0, and if u2=u⋅u=0, then u=0 by Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1 (d). This proves the proposition.

The answer to the prompt: x is non-negative by construction, and y is s times a non-negative number, so xy is s times a non-negative number, and 2xy must equal b. If b>0, then x and y must both be non-zero with the same sign; if b<0, opposite signs. When b=0 the choice of s does not matter, because then one of x,y is 0.

The proposition proves what Logic and Proof, Section 7.1 assumed, and it is stronger: it counts the roots. It uses only the field C, its modulus, and square roots of non-negative reals; no angle, cosine or sine appears. That matters later: Chapter 5 proves the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra using square roots in exactly this form.

Example (Square Roots of 5−12i)

Find all w∈C with w2=5−12i.

Solution. Write w=x+yi with x,y∈R. Comparing real and imaginary parts in (x2−y2)+2xyi=5−12i gives x2−y2=5andxy=−6. Taking moduli, by Theorem 1.2.2 (d), x2+y2=|w|2=|5−12i|=25+144=13. Adding and subtracting, x2=9 and y2=4, so x=±3 and y=±2. Since xy=−6<0, x and y have opposite signs. Hence w=3−2i or w=−3+2i. Check: (3−2i)2=9−12i+4i2=5−12i, and (−3+2i)2=(3−2i)2.

The formula of Proposition 1.4.1 gives the same root: a=5, b=−12<0, so s=−1; r=13, x=18/2=3 and y=−8/2=−2. By part (b) there are no other roots.

Quick check. Find the square roots of −4 and of 2i.

Solution. For −4: a=−4, b=0, r=4, so x=0=0 and y=8/2=2; the roots are ±2i, and indeed (2i)2=−4. For 2i: a=0, b=2, r=2, so x=1=1 and y=1; the roots are ±(1+i), and (1+i)2=1+2i−1=2i.

1.4.2. The quadratic formula

With square roots in hand, a quadratic equation with complex coefficients is solved exactly as in school: complete the square. The only difference is that we never need to ask whether the discriminant is negative.

Corollary 1.4.2 (Quadratic Formula over the Complex Numbers)

Let a,b,c∈C with a≠0, and let d∈C be a square root of b2−4ac (one exists by Proposition 1.4.1). Then for z∈C, az2+bz+c=0⟺z=−b+d2a  or  z=−b−d2a. The two solutions coincide if and only if b2−4ac=0.

Here a,b,c are complex numbers, in a departure from the chapter’s habit of reserving these letters for reals; the statement is the school formula, and keeps its letters. The choice of d does not matter: the other square root is −d (Proposition 1.4.1 (b)), which swaps the two solutions.

Idea. Complete the square, as in school: after multiplying by 4a, the equation says that 2az+b is a square root of b2−4ac. The square roots of b2−4ac are exactly d and −d, and solving the two linear equations 2az+b=±d gives the formula.

Proof. Multiply by 4a and complete the square. By the ring laws (Proposition 1.1.2) and Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (d), 4a(az2+bz+c)=4a2z2+4abz+4ac=(2az+b)2−(b2−4ac). Now 4a≠0, since 4≠0 in C (as for 2 in the proof of Proposition 1.4.1 (c)) and a≠0, and C has no zero divisors (Theorem 1.2.4, Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1 (d)). So az2+bz+c=0 if and only if (2az+b)2=d2, that is, (2az+b−d)(2az+b+d)=0, as in the proof of Proposition 1.4.1 (b). By the absence of zero divisors again, this holds if and only if 2az+b=d or 2az+b=−d. Since 2a≠0, these say z=(−b+d)/(2a) or z=(−b−d)/(2a).

The two solutions coincide if and only if d=−d, that is, 2d=0, that is, d=0 (as 2≠0). And d=0 if and only if d2=0 (no zero divisors), that is, b2−4ac=0. This proves the corollary.

Example. Solve z2−(3+i)z+(2+2i)=0. Here a=1, and the discriminant is (3+i)2−4(2+2i)=(8+6i)−(8+8i)=−2i. The formula of Proposition 1.4.1, applied to −2i (real part 0, imaginary part −2<0, modulus 2), gives x=2/2=1 and y=−2/2=−1, so d=1−i; indeed (1−i)2=1−2i−1=−2i. The solutions are z=(3+i)+(1−i)2=2andz=(3+i)−(1−i)2=1+i. Check: their sum is 3+i and their product is 2+2i, the negative of the middle coefficient and the constant term, as they should be for (z−2)(z−(1+i)).

1.4.3. No order on the complex numbers

In R, and in every ordered field, a non-zero square is positive (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e)). In C, i is non-zero and its square is −1. If C carried an order of the kind R has, −1 would have to be positive, and so would 1. That is already the proof.

Proposition 1.4.3 (The Complex Numbers Cannot Be Ordered)

There is no relation < on C with which (C,+,⋅,<) is an ordered field in the sense of Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 2.3.2.

Proof. Suppose < is a relation on C with which C is an ordered field. Since i=(0,1)≠(0,0)=0, Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e) gives i2>0, that is, −1>0 (Proposition 1.1.1: i⋅i=−1). Since 1≠0 in the field C, the same part (e) gives 1>0. Adding, by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (b), 0=1+(−1)>0+0=0. But 0<0 is impossible: (O1), with both elements equal to 0, says that exactly one of 0<0 and 0=0 holds, and 0=0 does. So no such relation exists.

The proof never asks whether i>0 or i<0: part (e) covers both at once. It also never assumes that the hypothetical order agrees with the order of R on real numbers. It concludes 1>0 and −1>0 in whatever order is supposed, and that is the contradiction.

Remark. Number Systems: From N to R, Chapter 4 showed, in an exercise (Number Systems: From N to R, Exercise (C1: A field that cannot be ordered)), that a field containing a square root of −1 cannot be ordered, and named the complex numbers as such a field; the proposition proves that fact for C on its own. Compare Number Systems: From N to R, Example (A field with two elements that cannot be ordered) and Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 3.4.3 (e): there the obstacle to an order is n⋅1=0 for some n≥1. That obstacle is absent here, since n⋅1 in C is ι(n⋅1), the image of the real number n⋅1>0 of R (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (h)), which is non-zero as ι is injective. (The identity n⋅1=ι(n⋅1) is proved by Logic and Proof, Axiom 4.2.1 on n, as for powers in Section 1.1: 0⋅1=0=ι(0), and if n⋅1=ι(n⋅1), then (n+1)⋅1=ι(n⋅1)+ι(1)=ι((n+1)⋅1) by Proposition 1.1.1.) The obstacle in C is i2=−1 instead.

Warning. An inequality z<w between complex numbers means nothing: by Proposition 1.4.3 no order on C is compatible with its arithmetic. Apart from arguments that suppose an order on C in order to reach a contradiction (the proof above and Exercises B2 and C2), every inequality in this chapter compares real numbers in the order of R: moduli, real parts, imaginary parts, or quantities proved to be real, as 2Re(zw―) was in Section 1.3. Writing i>0 leads, by the proof above, to 0>0; so does writing i<0.

Quick check. Is the following a proof that C cannot be ordered? “Every real square is ≥0, and i2=−1<0. So i is not a square of anything, a contradiction.”

Solution. No. The argument proves nothing about orders on C. “−1<0” is a statement in R’s order, and a hypothetical order on C need not agree with it on the reals; the argument never even introduces the hypothetical order. And “i is not a square” is false: i=(±(1+i)/2)2 by Proposition 1.4.1. The correct proof works inside the supposed order, where i2>0 because i≠0.

1.4.4. The square-root rule does not survive

In R, xy=xy for x,y≥0: that is the rule for real square roots used above. A non-zero complex number has two square roots and no preferred one, and no way of preferring one keeps the rule alive.

Warning. No choice of “the” square root z of each complex z can satisfy zw=zw for all z,w∈C. Whatever is chosen for −1, it is i or −i (Proposition 1.4.1 (b)), and in both cases −1⋅−1=(±i)2=−1. But (−1)(−1)=1, and the rule itself forces 1=1: since 1 is a square root of 1, 1=1⋅1=1⋅1=1. So the rule would give −1=1, which is false in C (the real parts −1<1 differ). So in this volume the symbol x is applied only to non-negative reals; for any other z, say “a square root of z” and solve w2=z.

1.4.5. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) True or false: if z,w∈C and z2=w2, then z=w. Justify your answer, and state what is true.
  • (b) Run the formula of Proposition 1.4.1 on z=−4: compute a,b,r,s,x,y and both square roots.
  • (c) Where in the proof of Proposition 1.4.1 is r≥|a| used?
  • (d) State the idea of the proof of Proposition 1.4.1 in two sentences.

Solution.

  • (a) False: z=1, w=−1 have z2=w2=1 and z≠w. What is true: z2=w2 implies z=w or z=−w, since (z−w)(z+w)=z2−w2=0 and C has no zero divisors (this is the argument of Proposition 1.4.1 (b)).

  • (b) a=−4, b=0, r=|−4|=4, s=1 (as b≥0), x=(4−4)/2=0, y=(4+4)/2=2. So w=2i, and the square roots are ±2i.

  • (c) To take the square roots at all: r≥|a| gives r+a≥0 and r−a≥0, so (r+a)/2 and (r−a)/2 exist.

  • (d) Comparing parts gives x2−y2=a and 2xy=b; taking moduli gives a third equation x2+y2=r, and the first and third give x2 and y2 by adding and subtracting. The signs are then fixed by 2xy=b, and the absence of zero divisors shows that ±w are the only roots.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1)
  • (a) Find all w∈C with w2=−3+4i.
  • (b) Hence solve z2+2z+4−4i=0.

Solution.

  • (a) Write w=x+yi. Comparing parts, x2−y2=−3 and xy=2. Taking moduli (Theorem 1.2.2 (d)), x2+y2=|−3+4i|=9+16=5. Adding and subtracting, x2=1 and y2=4. Since xy=2>0, x and y have the same sign, so w=1+2i or w=−1−2i, and by Proposition 1.4.1 (b) there are no others. Check: (1+2i)2=1+4i−4=−3+4i.

  • (b) By Corollary 1.4.2 with a=1, b=2, c=4−4i: the discriminant is 4−4(4−4i)=−12+16i=4(−3+4i), with square root d=2(1+2i)=2+4i by (a), since (2(1+2i))2=4(1+2i)2. So z=−2+(2+4i)2=2iorz=−2−(2+4i)2=−2−2i. Equivalently, completing the square, z2+2z+4−4i=(z+1)2−(−3+4i), so z+1=±(1+2i). Check: (2i)2+2(2i)+4−4i=−4+4i+4−4i=0.

Exercise (B2: Put the proof in order)

The nine lines below include the seven lines of a proof of Proposition 1.4.3, shuffled, and two lines that do not belong. Write the seven lines in an order that makes a proof, and say what is wrong with each of the other two.

  • (i) i2=−1, so −1>0.
  • (ii) Since i≠0, we have i>0.
  • (iii) Suppose < is a relation on C that makes C an ordered field.
  • (iv) 0<0 is impossible by (O1), so no such relation exists.
  • (v) So i2>0, because non-zero squares are positive in an ordered field.
  • (vi) 1≠0, so 1=12>0.
  • (vii) i2=−1<0, because −1<0 in R.
  • (viii) i≠0, since i=(0,1)≠(0,0).
  • (ix) Adding, 0=1+(−1)>0+0=0.

Solution. An admissible order is (iii), (viii), (v), (i), (vi), (ix), (iv). The constraints are what each line uses: (v), (vi), (i), (ix) and (iv) work in the supposed order, so they come after (iii); (v) also uses (viii); (i) uses (v); (ix) uses (i) and (vi); (iv) uses (ix). Line (viii) is a fact about C and uses no order, so it may stand anywhere before (v), even before (iii); line (vi) may stand anywhere after (iii) and before (ix). For instance (iii), (vi), (viii), (v), (i), (ix), (iv) and (viii), (iii), (v), (i), (vi), (ix), (iv) are also admissible. The admissible orders are exactly those that respect these constraints.

Line (ii) drops a case: from i≠0, trichotomy (O1) gives i>0 or i<0, and the line keeps only the first. That is (N7) of Logic and Proof, Example (What is not allowed), a missing case. Line (v) needs neither case, which is why the proof does not split.

Line (vii) cites a reason that is not available: the supposed order on C is arbitrary, and nothing says that it agrees with the order of R on the real numbers. The proposition is about every relation on C, and (vii) quietly replaces “every” by “one that extends R’s order”. (The inequality −1<0 does hold in any ordered field, by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (a) applied to 1>0, so the line could be repaired by citing that; as written its reason is (N9) of Logic and Proof, Example (What is not allowed), a fact that is not available.)

Exercise (B3)

Let a,b,c∈R with a≠0 and b2−4ac<0. Prove that the equation az2+bz+c=0 has exactly the two solutions z=−b+i4ac−b22aandz=−b−i4ac−b22a in C, and that they are conjugate to each other.

Solution. Since 4ac−b2>0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (a)), q:=4ac−b2 exists, q>0, and d:=iq satisfies d2=i2q2=−(4ac−b2)=b2−4ac. So Corollary 1.4.2 with this d gives exactly the two solutions displayed, and they are distinct since b2−4ac≠0. Since a,b,q are real and 2a≠0, the two solutions are −b2a+q2aiand−b2a−q2ai, with the same real part and opposite imaginary parts: each is the conjugate of the other (Definition 1.2.1). For instance z2−2z+5=0 has b2−4ac=−16, q=4, and the solutions 1±2i.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Find the error)

Here is an argument.

Claim. For z,w∈C, if z2=w2, then z=w or z=−w.

Proof. If w=0, then z2=0, so z=0=w, since C has no zero divisors. If w≠0, put u:=z/w, which exists because C is a field; then u2=z2/w2=1. Taking square roots, u=u2=1=1. In particular, u=1 or u=−1. Multiplying by w, z=w or z=−w.

Is the claim true? Does the argument prove it? If not, name the line that fails and the reason, give a counterexample to that line, and repair the argument.

Solution. The claim is true: it is the argument of Proposition 1.4.1 (b), and it also follows directly from (z−w)(z+w)=z2−w2=0 and the absence of zero divisors in C.

The argument does not prove it. The failing line is “u=u2=1=1”. It uses the rule “u=u2 whenever u2 is a non-negative real”, which is neither proved nor true: it is a general “fact” of the kind (N9) of Logic and Proof, Example (What is not allowed) forbids. The symbol x is defined only on non-negative reals, and u is a complex number; even for real u, u2=|u|, not u. Counterexample to the step: u=−1 has u2=1, but u2=1=1≠u.

The next line, “u=1 or u=−1”, is a valid weakening of u=1 (a disjunction follows from either part), so it is not itself an error; it only papers over the loss of the root −1 one line earlier. The rest of the argument (the case w=0, the division, and multiplying back by w) is correct.

Repair: replace the two middle lines by “Then (u−1)(u+1)=u2−1=0, and C has no zero divisors, so u=1 or u=−1.”

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: says the claim is true, with a reason. - 3: names the line u=u2=1=1 as the failing one, and its illegal rule u=u2. - 2: a counterexample to that step (u=−1, or any u with u2=1, u≠1). - 3: a correct repair, by factoring u2−1 and using no zero divisors (or by Proposition 1.4.1 (b) applied to z=1). Fatal errors (0 for the step): declaring the claim false; naming the “In particular” line instead of the x line as the failing one, since it is a valid weakening.

Exercise (C2: A second proof that there is no order)
  • (a) Use Proposition 1.4.1 to give a second proof of Proposition 1.4.3 in which the element i is never mentioned.
  • (b) Say why the argument in (a) does not need the special element i.
  • (c) Is the converse of the principle behind (a) true? That is: if a commutative ring with 1≠0 admits no order, must −1 be a square in it? Test the ring Z/3Z.

Solution (Hint 1). In an ordered field, which elements can be squares? For (c), list the squares of the three classes.

Solution.

  • (a) Suppose < makes C an ordered field. Every square is then ≥0: a non-zero square is positive by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e), and 02=0. First, −1≠0, since otherwise 1=−(−1)=−0=0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a)), against 1≠0 in the field C (Theorem 1.2.4). By Proposition 1.4.1 (a), applied to the non-zero number −1, some d∈C has d2=−1. So −1=d2≥0, and as −1≠0, −1>0. By part (e) again, 1>0. Adding (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (b)), 0=1+(−1)>0, which (O1) forbids. This is the argument of Number Systems: From N to R, Exercise (C1: A field that cannot be ordered), with the square root of −1 supplied by a formula.

  • (b) The argument uses only that −1 is a square in C, and that is a property of the field, not of any named element. It does not matter that the formula of Proposition 1.4.1, applied to −1, happens to produce i (a=−1, b=0, r=1: x=0, y=1). The same argument shows that no field in which −1 is a square can be ordered; in particular no field in which every element is a square.

  • (c) No. The ring Z/3Z is a commutative ring with [1]≠[0] (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 3.4.3 (a), (c)), and no relation makes it an ordered commutative ring (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 3.4.3 (e)). Its three elements are [0],[1],[2] (part (b) there), with squares [0]2=[0], [1]2=[1], [2]2=[4]=[1]. So the squares are [0] and [1], while −[1]=[2]. Hence −1 is not a square in Z/3Z, and yet the ring cannot be ordered: the obstacle there is 3⋅[1]=[0], not a square root of −1.
