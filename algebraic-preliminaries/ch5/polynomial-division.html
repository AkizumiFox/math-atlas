5.1  Division with Remainder

Number Systems: From N to R, Section 3.1 divided −17 by 5 and kept the remainder 3: a quotient times the divisor, plus something smaller than the divisor. Chapter 4 built the ring F[x] of polynomials over a field and gave every non-zero polynomial a degree. For polynomials, “smaller” will mean “of smaller degree”. For instance, in Q[x], x4+2x3−x+3=(x2+x)(x2+x−1)+3, and the leftover 3 has degree 0, below the degree 2 of x2+x−1. Three questions are open. Why can one always go on until the degree drops below that of the divisor? Why is the answer unique? And where does it matter that the coefficients lie in a field? This section answers all three, and then uses the answer to connect a root of a polynomial with a factor of degree 1.

Throughout the chapter, F is a field (Definition 2.1.1), and F[x] is the ring of polynomials over F (Definition 4.2.1), a commutative ring by Theorem 4.2.4 (a). A field is an integral domain (Example (Which Rings Are Integral Domains)), so the results of Chapter 4 stated for integral domains apply to F[x]. We use the degree deg⁡p and the leading coefficient lc(p) of Definition 4.3.1, with deg⁡0=−∞, and the conventions −∞<n and −∞+n=−∞ for n∈N that come with it. A constant a∈F is identified with the polynomial ax0 (Theorem 4.2.4 (b)), and (ap)k=apk for a constant a (Theorem 4.2.4 (d)). Numerals in F are multiples of 1: 2:=1+1, 3:=2+1, and so on, so that 2=0 in F2 and 3=0 in F3 (Definition 2.2.1).

5.1.1. Divisibility over a field

In Z, “a divides b” asked whether b is some integer times a. The same question makes sense in any commutative ring, and in F[x] it is the question this chapter is built on. The one new feature is that the answer can depend on the field.

A polynomial g divides f when f is g times some polynomial over the same field.

Definition 5.1.1 (Divisibility of Polynomials)

Let F be a field and f,g∈F[x].

  • (a) g divides f over F, written g∣f, if there exists h∈F[x] with f=gh. Then g is a divisor (or factor) of f, and f is a multiple of g. We write g∤f when g does not divide f.
  • (b) f and g are associates if f=cg for some non-zero constant c∈F.

In words: (a) asks for a cofactor h, and the cofactor must have its coefficients in F; (b) says that two polynomials are associates when they differ by a non-zero constant factor. When two fields are in play, “g∣f in F[x]” names the field.

Four instances, chosen so that the same symbols give different answers:

  • x−1∣x2−1 in Q[x], since x2−1=(x−1)(x+1).
  • 2x−2∣x2−1 in Q[x], since x2−1=(2x−2)(12x+12). The polynomials x−1 and 2x−2=2(x−1) are associates, and the cofactor 12x+12 needs the inverse of 2.
  • x+1∣x2+1 in F2[x], since (x+1)2=x2+2x+1=x2+1 there: 2=0 in F2.
  • x+1∤x2+1 in Q[x]. Suppose x2+1=(x+1)h with h∈Q[x]. Evaluating at −1 respects sums and products (Theorem 4.4.1), so (−1)2+1=(−1+1)⋅h(−1), that is, 2=0⋅h(−1)=0 in Q, which is false.

Non-example by minimal change. The last two lines use the same symbols, x+1 and x2+1. Only the field changed, from F2 to Q, and with it the value of 2: the evaluation argument gives 2=0, which is true in F2 and false in Q. So “g∣f” is a statement about g, f and F, which is why the definition says “over F”.

Degenerate cases. Every g divides 0, since 0=g⋅0. The zero polynomial divides only 0. And a non-zero constant c divides every f, since f=c(c−1f); this is (F8), inverses of non-zero elements, at work in general (the cofactor of 2x−2 above was one instance), and it means that non-zero constants are as invisible to divisibility as ±1 were in Z.

Quick check. In F3[x], does x+1 divide x2+1? Evaluate at −1=2.

Solution. No. If x2+1=(x+1)h, evaluating at 2 (Theorem 4.4.1) gives 22+1=(2+1)h(2), that is, 5=0⋅h(2)=0. In F3, 5=2≠0. So x+1∤x2+1 in F3[x].

The rules below are the ones the chapter uses. Parts (a)–(d) are identities in the commutative ring F[x], plus the inverse of a non-zero constant (F8) for the last clause of (a) and one degree count; parts (e) and (f) say exactly what “invisible” means.

Proposition 5.1.1 (Rules for Divisibility of Polynomials)

Let F be a field and f,g,h∈F[x].

  • (a) g∣0. 0∣f if and only if f=0. Every non-zero constant divides every polynomial.
  • (b) If f∣g and g∣h, then f∣h.
  • (c) If h∣f and h∣g, then h∣af+bg for all a,b∈F[x].
  • (d) If g∣f and f≠0, then deg⁡g≤deg⁡f.
  • (e) The units of F[x] (Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 3.5.1) are exactly the non-zero constants.
  • (f) f∣g and g∣f if and only if f and g are associates (this includes f=g=0). Every non-zero f has exactly one monic associate, namely lc(f)−1f. Two monic polynomials that divide each other are equal.

Idea. Parts (a)–(c) are rearrangements of equations f=gh by the ring laws, except that the last clause of (a) also uses c−1, from (F8). Part (d) is the degree of a product: a non-zero multiple of g has degree deg⁡g plus something in N. Part (e) is Chapter 4’s description of the units of R[x], read with R=F. For (f), mutual divisibility gives f=f⋅uv, and canceling f makes u a unit, which (e) identifies as a non-zero constant.

Proof. We use the ring laws of F[x] (Theorem 4.2.4 (a)) and Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 without further mention.

  • (a) 0=g⋅0, so g∣0. If 0∣f, then f=0⋅k=0 for some k; conversely 0∣0 by the first claim. If c∈F is a non-zero constant, then c−1 exists by (F8) of Definition 2.1.1, and f=(cc−1)f=c(c−1f), so c∣f.

  • (b) Let g=fu and h=gv. Then h=(fu)v=f(uv), so f∣h.

  • (c) Let f=hu and g=hv. Then af+bg=a(hu)+b(hv)=h(au+bv), so h∣af+bg.

  • (d) Let f=gu with u∈F[x]. Since f≠0, neither g nor u is 0. The field F has no zero divisors (Theorem 2.1.1 (f)), so Theorem 4.3.2 (b) gives deg⁡f=deg⁡g+deg⁡u. Here deg⁡u∈N, so deg⁡g≤deg⁡f.

  • (e) By Proposition 4.3.4, applied to the integral domain F (Example (Which Rings Are Integral Domains)), the units of F[x] are the constants that are units of F. In a field every non-zero a is a unit, by (F8), and 0 is not, since 0⋅v=0≠1 for every v (Theorem 2.1.1 (d) and (F7) of Definition 2.1.1). So the units of F[x] are the non-zero constants.

  • (f) (⇐) Let f=cg with c≠0 a constant. Then g∣f, and g=c−1f, so f∣g.

(⇒) Let f∣g and g∣f. If f=0, then 0∣g, so g=0 by (a), and f=1⋅g. Otherwise write g=fu and f=gv. Then f=fuv, so f(1−uv)=0. Since f≠0, Corollary 4.3.3 (a) gives 1−uv=0, that is, uv=1. So v is a unit of F[x], hence a non-zero constant by (e), and f=vg: f and g are associates.

The monic associate. Let f≠0 and a:=lc(f)≠0. The polynomial a−1f has the coefficients a−1fk (Theorem 4.2.4 (d)). These vanish exactly where the fk do, since a−1≠0 (as a⋅0=0≠1=aa−1, by Theorem 2.1.1 (d), (F7), (F8)) and F has no zero divisors, so deg⁡(a−1f)=deg⁡f and lc(a−1f)=a−1a=1. If also cf is monic for a constant c≠0, the same computation gives lc(cf)=ca=1, so c=a−1 (uniqueness of inverses, Theorem 2.1.1 (c)). So a−1f is the only monic associate of f.

Monic and mutually dividing. Let f,g be monic with f∣g and g∣f. By what we proved, f=cg with a non-zero constant c, and comparing leading coefficients, 1=lc(f)=clc(g)=c. So f=g. This proves the proposition.

Parts (e) and (f) are the polynomial form of a fact about Z: in Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 3.1.1 (f) and (g), the units of Z were ±1, and two positive integers that divide each other are equal. In F[x] the units are the non-zero constants, and the role of “positive” is played by “monic”: among the associates of a non-zero polynomial, exactly one is monic. So whenever a polynomial is to be singled out from its associates, it is made monic.

The hypothesis f≠0 in (d) cannot be dropped: x5∣0, and deg⁡x5=5>−∞=deg⁡0.

Quick check. In Q[x], list the associates of 3x+6 that have integer coefficients and leading coefficient between −3 and 3. Which one is monic?

Solution. The associates are c(3x+6) with c∈Q, c≠0; the leading coefficient is 3c. Leading coefficients ±1,±2,±3 give ±(x+2), ±(2x+4), ±(3x+6), all with integer coefficients. The monic one is x+2=13(3x+6).

5.1.2. Division with remainder

Now the theorem. The tool is the one Number Systems: From N to R, Section 3.1 used for integers, with degree in place of size: among all f−hg, take one of least degree, and show that its degree is below deg⁡g.

Theorem 5.1.2 (Division with Remainder in F[x])

Let F be a field, and let f,g∈F[x] with g≠0.

  • (a) There are unique q,r∈F[x] with f=qg+randdeg⁡r<deg⁡g.
  • (b) g∣f if and only if r=0.

The polynomials q and r are the quotient and the remainder of f on division by g.

Since deg⁡0=−∞<deg⁡g, the remainder r=0 is allowed.

Idea. Look at all polynomials f−hg, h∈F[x]. If 0 is among them, f is a multiple of g and we are done. Otherwise their degrees are natural numbers, and well-ordering gives one, r, of least degree n. If n≥deg⁡g, we can kill the leading term of r by subtracting a constant times a power of x times g: the constant is lc(r)lc(g)−1, and that inverse is where the field is needed. The result is again of the form f−hg and has smaller degree, against the choice of r. Uniqueness: two answers give (q−q′)g=r′−r, and a non-zero multiple of g cannot have degree below deg⁡g. We call this move compare degrees.

Proof.

  • (a) Existence. Let m:=deg⁡g∈N and b:=lc(g)≠0, and let S:={f−hg:h∈F[x]}. If 0∈S, say 0=f−qg, then r:=0 and this q satisfy f=qg+r and deg⁡r=−∞<m.

    Suppose 0∉S. Then every element of S has a degree in N, and D:={deg⁡s:s∈S}⊆N is non-empty, since f=f−0⋅g∈S. By the well-ordering of N (Sets, Relations, and Cardinality, Theorem 2.1.3; on N=ω it is Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 1.6.2), D has a least element n. Choose q∈F[x] with r:=f−qg of degree n, and let a:=lc(r)≠0.

    Suppose n≥m. Let t:=(ab−1)xn−m, a monomial with n−m∈N and non-zero coefficient ab−1: b−1≠0, since b⋅0=0≠1=bb−1 (Theorem 2.1.1 (d), (F7), (F8)), so ab−1≠0 by Theorem 2.1.1 (f). Then deg⁡t=n−m and lc(t)=ab−1, so by Theorem 4.3.2 (b), tg has degree (n−m)+m=n and leading coefficient ab−1b=a. Let r1:=r−tg=f−(q+t)g, an element of S. By Theorem 4.3.1 (with deg⁡(−p)=deg⁡p for every p, since (−p)k=−pk), deg⁡r1≤max(deg⁡r,deg⁡tg)=n, and the coefficient of xn in r1 is a−a=0. So deg⁡r1<n (or r1=0). But r1≠0, since 0∉S, and then deg⁡r1∈D is smaller than the least element n of D. This contradiction shows n<m. So f=qg+r with deg⁡r<deg⁡g.

    Uniqueness. Let f=qg+r=q′g+r′ with deg⁡r<m and deg⁡r′<m. Then (q−q′)g=r′−r, and deg⁡(r′−r)≤max(deg⁡r′,deg⁡r)<m by Theorem 4.3.1 (using deg⁡(−r)=deg⁡r as above). If q≠q′, then Theorem 4.3.2 (b) gives deg⁡((q−q′)g)=deg⁡(q−q′)+m≥m, a contradiction. So q=q′, hence r′−r=0⋅g=0 and r=r′.

  • (b) (⇐) If r=0, then f=qg, so g∣f. (⇒) If f=hg, then f=hg+0 with deg⁡0<deg⁡g is a division with remainder; by the uniqueness in (a), r=0. This proves the theorem.

The field is used in two places. The first is the inverse b−1 of the leading coefficient of g, which builds the monomial t. The second is the degree of a product, in deg⁡(tg)=n and in the uniqueness: Theorem 4.3.2 (b) needs the product of the leading coefficients to be non-zero, and a field has no zero divisors. The hypothesis g≠0 is needed as well: for g=0 no r has deg⁡r<−∞.

The existence proof is also a procedure. Each pass replaces r by r−tg, which removes the leading term of r, and the degree drops by at least one. Starting from f, at most deg⁡f−deg⁡g+1 passes are needed when deg⁡f≥deg⁡g, and none when deg⁡f<deg⁡g: then q=0 and r=f. This is the long division of school algebra, now with a proof that it stops and that its answer is the only one. Chapter 4 did not divide polynomials with remainder, so this is where the volume first does it. Its integer twin is Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 3.1.2, whose least-element proof the one above follows.

Example (Long division in two fields)

  • (i) In Q[x], divide f=2x4−3x3+x2+4x−1 by g=2x2−x+3.

  • (ii) In F3[x], divide f=x4+x+2 by g=2x2+1.

Solution.

  • (i) Here lc(g)=2, with inverse 12. Each pass subtracts tg, where t is the leading term of the current remainder divided by 2x2: f−x2g=−2x3−2x2+4x−1,(−2x3−2x2+4x−1)−(−x)g=−3x2+7x−1,(−3x2+7x−1)−(−32)g=112x+72. The last line has degree 1<2, so we stop. Adding up the t’s, 2x4−3x3+x2+4x−1=(x2−x−32)(2x2−x+3)+112x+72. Check: (x2−x−32)(2x2−x+3)=2x4−3x3+x2−32x−92, and adding 112x+72 gives f.

  • (ii) In F3, 2⋅2=4=1, so lc(g)−1=2−1=2. The first t is 1⋅2⋅x2=2x2, and 2x2⋅g=4x4+2x2=x4+2x2. The second t is 2, and 2g=4x2+2=x2+2: f−2x2g=−2x2+x+2=x2+x+2,(x2+x+2)−2g=x. So x4+x+2=(2x2+2)(2x2+1)+x in F3[x]. Check: (2x2+2)(2x2+1)=4x4+6x2+2=x4+2 in F3, and adding x gives f.

Quick check. Find two different pairs (q,r) with x2=q(x2+1)+r in Q[x] and deg⁡r≤2. Which condition in part (a) of the theorem does the second one fail?

Solution. (q,r)=(1,−1): 1⋅(x2+1)−1=x2, with deg⁡(−1)=0<2. And (q,r)=(0,x2): 0⋅(x2+1)+x2=x2, with deg⁡r=2. The second fails deg⁡r<deg⁡g=2. Uniqueness holds only among remainders of degree strictly below that of the divisor; (1,−1) is the quotient and remainder.

Warning. Division with remainder needs the leading coefficient of the divisor to be invertible. Take the polynomials in Q[x] all of whose coefficients lie in Z, and try to divide x2 by 2x among them: x2=q⋅2x+r with deg⁡r<1. Comparing the coefficients of x2 gives 1=2q1 (Lemma 4.2.2 (a)), and no integer q1 satisfies it, since 1 is odd (Number Systems: From N to R, Corollary 3.1.3 (a)). In Q[x] there is no problem: x2=12x⋅2x+0. Exercise C1 shows that integer coefficients are kept when the divisor is monic.

5.1.3. Roots and factors of degree one

Division by a polynomial of degree 1 has a remainder of degree at most 0, that is, a constant. Which constant? Evaluate: the divisor x−c vanishes at c, so the quotient term disappears and the remainder is read off. We call this move evaluate at a root.

Theorem 5.1.3 (Remainder Theorem and Factor Theorem)

Let F be a field, f∈F[x] and c∈F.

  • (a) The remainder of f on division by x−c is the constant f(c).
  • (b) c is a root of f if and only if x−c∣f.
  • (c) f has a factor of degree 1 in F[x] if and only if f has a root in F.

Idea. Divide by x−c and evaluate at c: the term with the quotient vanishes, and what is left is the remainder. Part (b) is then the divisibility test of Theorem 5.1.2 (b), and for (c) a factor ax+b of degree 1 is made monic, which turns it into some x−c.

Proof.

  • (a) The polynomial x−c is non-zero, of degree 1 and monic. By Theorem 5.1.2 (a), f=q⋅(x−c)+r with deg⁡r<1, so r is a constant (possibly 0). Evaluation at c respects sums and products, sends x to c and a constant to itself (Theorem 4.4.1), so f(c)=q(c)⋅(c−c)+r=q(c)⋅0+r=r.

  • (b) By (a), f=q⋅(x−c)+f(c) is the division of f by x−c. By Theorem 5.1.2 (b), x−c∣f if and only if this remainder is 0, that is, f(c)=0.

  • (c) (⇐) If f(c)=0, then x−c∣f by (b), and x−c has degree 1. (⇒) Let ax+b∣f with a,b∈F, a≠0. Then ax+b=a(x−(−a−1b)), so x−(−a−1b)∣ax+b, and by Proposition 5.1.1 (b), x−(−a−1b)∣f. By (b), −a−1b is a root of f.

Part (b) is the factor theorem, and it turns a question about values (is f(c)=0?) into a question about divisibility. Part (c) turns the search for factors of degree 1 into a search for roots.

Example (Remainders without dividing)

  • (a) In Q[x], find the remainder of f=x5−3x3+2x+7 on division by x−2.

  • (b) In Q[x], find the remainder of x5 on division by x2−x.

Solution.

  • (a) By Theorem 5.1.3 (a), the remainder is f(2)=32−24+4+7=19.

  • (b) The divisor x2−x has degree 2, so the remainder has degree at most 1: x5=q⋅(x2−x)+ax+b with a,b∈Q (Theorem 5.1.2 (a)). The divisor vanishes at 0 and at 1. Evaluating at both (Theorem 4.4.1), 0=q(0)⋅0+b,1=q(1)⋅0+a+b, so b=0 and a=1. The remainder is x. Check: x5−x=(x2−x)(x3+x2+x+1).

Quick check. Without dividing, decide whether x+1 divides x7+1 in Q[x], and whether x−1 does.

Solution. x+1=x−(−1), and (−1)7+1=−1+1=0, so x+1∣x7+1 by Theorem 5.1.3 (b). For x−1, the remainder is 17+1=2≠0 in Q, so x−1∤x7+1. (Over F2, 2=0 and both divide.)

Remark (Looking ahead). Section 5.5 builds, for any finite list of distinct points of F, the polynomial of least degree that takes prescribed values there. Exercise C2 below is the case of two points: the remainder of f on division by (x−a)(x−b).

5.1.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) State Theorem 5.1.2 with every hypothesis.
  • (b) Name the two places in its proof where “F is a field” is used.
  • (c) Let f∈F[x], and let c∈F be a non-zero constant. What are the quotient and the remainder of f on division by c?
  • (d) True or false, with a reason: for f,g∈Q[x], if f∣g and g∣f, then f=g. The same question in F2[x].

Solution.

  • (a) Let F be a field and f,g∈F[x] with g≠0. Then there are unique q,r∈F[x] with f=qg+r and deg⁡r<deg⁡g; and g∣f if and only if r=0.

  • (b) The inverse lc(g)−1, which builds the monomial t=lc(r)lc(g)−1xn−m; and the absence of zero divisors in F, through Theorem 4.3.2 (b), which gives deg⁡(tg)=n and, in the uniqueness, deg⁡((q−q′)g)≥deg⁡g.

  • (c) q=c−1f and r=0: indeed f=(c−1f)c+0, and deg⁡0=−∞<0=deg⁡c. By uniqueness this is the division.

  • (d) In Q[x]: false. x∣2x and 2x∣x (x=12⋅2x), but x≠2x. By Proposition 5.1.1 (f), mutual divisibility only makes f and g associates. In F2[x]: true. By (f), f=cg with c a non-zero constant, and the only non-zero element of F2 is 1; so f=g.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Two long divisions)
  • (a) In Q[x], divide x4−3x3+2x−5 by 3x2−1.
  • (b) In F5[x], divide x4+2x3+3x+1 by 2x2+3, and decide whether 2x2+3 divides x4+2x3+3x+1 in F5[x].

Solution.

  • (a) lc(3x2−1)−1=13. The passes subtract 13x2, −x and 19 times the divisor: (x4−3x3+2x−5)−13x2(3x2−1)=−3x3+13x2+2x−5,(−3x3+13x2+2x−5)+x(3x2−1)=13x2+x−5,(13x2+x−5)−19(3x2−1)=x−449. So the quotient is 13x2−x+19 and the remainder is x−449, of degree 1<2. Check: (3x2−1)(13x2−x+19)=x4−3x3+x−19, and adding x−449 gives x4−3x3+2x−5.

  • (b) In F5, 2⋅3=6=1, so 2−1=3. The passes subtract 3x2, x and 3 times the divisor, with 3x2(2x2+3)=x4+4x2, x(2x2+3)=2x3+3x and 3(2x2+3)=x2+4: (x4+2x3+3x+1)−(x4+4x2)=2x3+x2+3x+1,(2x3+x2+3x+1)−(2x3+3x)=x2+1,(x2+1)−(x2+4)=2, using −4=1 and −3=2 in F5. So the quotient is 3x2+x+3 and the remainder is the constant 2. Since 2≠0 in F5, Theorem 5.1.2 (b) gives 2x2+3∤x4+2x3+3x+1 in F5[x].

Exercise (B2: A factor of a difference of powers)

Let F be a field, c∈F and n∈Z>0. Deduce from Lemma 1.1.4 that xn−cn=(x−c)∑j=0n−1cjxn−1−j, and hence that x−c∣xn−cn. Then give a second proof of the divisibility with Theorem 5.1.3.

Solution. F[x] is a commutative ring (Theorem 4.2.4 (a)), so Lemma 1.1.4 applies in it. Take z=c and w=x: cn−xn=(c−x)∑j=0n−1cjxn−1−j. Negate both sides: −(cn−xn)=xn−cn and −((c−x)s)=(x−c)s for every s∈F[x] (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a), (c)). This is the displayed identity, and it shows x−c∣xn−cn. Taking z=c, w=x rather than z=x, w=c gives the sum in the stated order, with no re-indexing.

Second proof. By Theorem 4.2.4 (c), xn is the monomial 1xn, whose value at c is 1⋅cn=cn (Definition 4.4.1, the monomial example after it). So the value of xn−cn at c is cn−cn=0 (Theorem 4.4.1, for the sum and the constant −cn), so c is a root and x−c∣xn−cn by Theorem 5.1.3 (b). This proof gives the divisibility at once but not the quotient.

Exercise (B3: One remainder over every field)

Let F be any field. Show that the remainder of x100 on division by x2−1 in F[x] is 1. Then explain why, over Q, evaluating at the roots 1 and −1 also finds it, and why that method alone does not settle the question over F2.

Solution. By Lemma 1.1.4 in F[x] with z=x2, w=1 and n=50, since (x2)k=x2k and 1k=1 (Lemma 4.1.1 (c)), (x2−1)∑j=049x2j=x100−1. So x100=(∑j=049x2j)(x2−1)+1, and deg⁡1=0<2=deg⁡(x2−1). By the uniqueness in Theorem 5.1.2 (a), the remainder is 1, over every field.

Over Q by evaluation. Write x100=q(x2−1)+ax+b. Evaluating at 1 and −1 gives 1=a+b and 1=−a+b; subtracting, 2a=0, so a=0 since 2≠0 in Q; then b=1.

Over F2. There −1=1, so the two roots coincide and evaluation gives the single equation 1=a+b. It does not determine a and b (both (0,1) and (1,0) satisfy it), so the uniqueness argument above is needed.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Monic divisors keep integer coefficients)

Call p∈Q[x] an integer polynomial if every coefficient of p lies in Z.

  • (a) Let f,g be integer polynomials with g monic. Prove that the quotient and the remainder of f on division by g in Q[x] are integer polynomials.
  • (b) Prove the same if lc(g)=−1.
  • (c) Explain why the least-degree argument of Theorem 5.1.2 still works when S is replaced by {f−hg:h an integer polynomial}, for g monic.

Solution (Hint 1). Rerun the existence proof of Theorem 5.1.2, but only with integer polynomials h.

Solution (Hint 2). Let SZ:={f−hg:h an integer polynomial}. If 0∉SZ, take r=f−qg∈SZ of least degree n (Sets, Relations, and Cardinality, Theorem 2.1.3) and suppose n≥deg⁡g.

Solution. Sums, negatives and products of integer polynomials are integer polynomials: their coefficients are sums of products of coefficients (Definition 4.2.1), and Z is closed under +, −, ⋅.

  • (c) and (a). Let m:=deg⁡g and SZ:={f−hg:h an integer polynomial}⊆Q[x], whose elements are integer polynomials. Run the existence proof with SZ in place of S. If 0∈SZ, then f=qg with q an integer polynomial and r=0. Otherwise take r=f−qg∈SZ of least degree n (Sets, Relations, and Cardinality, Theorem 2.1.3), and suppose n≥m. With a:=lc(r)∈Z and b=1, the monomial t=axn−m is an integer polynomial, so r−tg=f−(q+t)g∈SZ, and it has degree <n or is 0, exactly as in the proof. Both contradict the choice of r. So deg⁡r<m, and f=qg+r with q,r integer polynomials. This is a division with remainder in Q[x], so by uniqueness (Theorem 5.1.2 (a)) these q, r are the quotient and remainder. The argument works because the step r↦r−tg never leaves SZ: it needs b−1∈Z, and b=1 gives b−1=1. That answers (c).

  • (d) If lc(g)=−1, then b−1=−1∈Z, and the same proof runs with t=−axn−m. (Alternatively, divide f by the monic −g: f=q(−g)+r=(−q)g+r.)

Exercise (C2: The remainder modulo a quadratic with known roots)

Let F be a field, f∈F[x] and a,b∈F.

  • (a) If a≠b, prove that the remainder of f on division by (x−a)(x−b) is f(a)(a−b)−1(x−b)+f(b)(b−a)−1(x−a).
  • (b) If a=b, write f=(x−a)h+f(a) (Theorem 5.1.3 (a)) and prove that the remainder of f on division by (x−a)2 is h(a)(x−a)+f(a).
  • (c) Hence find the remainder of x100 on division by x2−3x+2 in Q[x].

Solution (Hint 1). The remainder has degree at most 1. Which values does it take at a and at b?

Solution (Hint 2). Write f=q(x−a)(x−b)+r with deg⁡r≤1, and let s be the displayed polynomial.

Solution.

  • (a) The divisor (x−a)(x−b) is monic of degree 2 (Theorem 4.3.2 (b)), so Theorem 5.1.2 (a) gives f=q(x−a)(x−b)+r with deg⁡r≤1. Evaluating at a and at b (Theorem 4.4.1) kills the first term: r(a)=f(a) and r(b)=f(b). Let s be the displayed polynomial; it has degree at most 1, and s(a)=f(a)⋅1+f(b)⋅0=f(a), s(b)=f(b). So r−s has degree at most 1 and the two roots a≠b. If r−s≠0, then x−a∣r−s (Theorem 5.1.3 (b)), say r−s=(x−a)k, and deg⁡k≤0 by Theorem 4.3.2 (b), so k is a constant; evaluating at b gives 0=(b−a)k, so k=0 because b−a≠0, and then r−s=0 after all. Hence r=s.

  • (b) Divide h by x−a: h=(x−a)h2+h(a) (Theorem 5.1.3 (a)). Then f=(x−a)2h2+h(a)(x−a)+f(a), and h(a)(x−a)+f(a) has degree at most 1<2. By uniqueness, it is the remainder.

  • (c) x2−3x+2=(x−1)(x−2). By (a) with a=1, b=2, f(1)=1 and f(2)=2100, the remainder is 1⋅(−1)−1(x−2)+2100⋅1−1(x−1)=(2100−1)x+(2−2100).
