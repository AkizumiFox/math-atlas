Chapter 1

Chapter Review

Summary

Finite sums obey the rules of Lemma 1.1.3 in every commutative ring, and a sum whose terms are differences telescopes (Theorem 1.1.7). The binomial theorem holds in every commutative ring with coefficients defined by Pascal’s rule (Theorem 1.2.3). In the real numbers it bounds a power from below by one or two of its terms (Corollary 1.2.4). Bernoulli’s inequality becomes strict (Lemma 1.3.1) and extends to products, and the arithmetic mean bounds the geometric mean (Theorem 1.3.3). With these, geometric sequences, n/rn and 1/n! tend to zero. Every non-negative real has a unique non-negative n-th root (Lemma 1.5.1). The powers am/n of a positive real are well defined and obey the laws of powers (Proposition 1.5.5).

What is open: powers with irrational exponents, which Chapter 8 defines through the exponential function, and the limit of the compound-interest sequence, which Analysis on the Real Line, Chapter 4 identifies.

Proof methods in this chapter

  • Telescoping (Example (Method: telescoping)). When a sum has terms bk+1−bk, the middle cancels and only the ends survive. Introduced in Section 1.1, where it sums reciprocals and squares and proves the difference of powers. Its “verification form” checks a guessed closed form instead of finding it.
  • Bound a product by a power (Example (Method: bound a product by a power)). Replace the factors by their arithmetic mean, which is easy to compute, and let the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality turn the product into a power. Introduced in Section 1.3.
  • Keep the dominant term (Example (Method: keep the dominant term)). In a quotient of sums, keep the term that grows fastest and absorb the others into it from some index on. The quotient is then bounded by a quotient of single powers. Introduced in Section 1.4.
  • Bound a power from below (Example (Method: bound a power from below)). Write a base above one as 1+h and keep one term of the expansion, by Bernoulli or by the binomial theorem. This makes rn at least a multiple of a power of n. Introduced in Section 1.4, with exercises B1 and C2.
  • Recalled from earlier volumes. Proving a limit from the definition (F3 Example (Method: proving a limit from the definition)); induction on the last term (F2 Example (Method: finding P(n) inside P(n+1))); well-definedness (F2 Example (Method: checking that a definition on classes is well defined)). Constructing a point as a supremum, from Number Systems: From N to R, Chapter 6 (F3 Proposition 6.1.4), is run again in Section 1.5.

Terms

  • arithmetic mean, geometric mean: (a1+⋯+an)/n and a1⋯ann, compared in Theorem 1.3.3.
  • binomial coefficient: the natural number (nk) given by Pascal’s rule, never by a quotient of factorials (Definition 1.2.1).
  • integer power: a−n:=(a−1)n for a≠0 in a field (Definition 1.5.1).
  • natural multiple: n⋅a, the sum of n copies of a, with its laws (Lemma 1.1.2).
  • n-th root: the unique b≥0 with bn=a, written an (Lemma 1.5.1).
  • rational power: am/n:=(an)m for a>0 (Definition 1.5.2).
  • telescoping sum: a sum of terms bk+1−bk, equal to the difference of its ends (Theorem 1.1.7).

Threads

  • From sums to Cauchy–Schwarz. The rules for finite sums of Section 1.1 are what Chapter 9 uses to prove the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Chapter 4 uses the same sums with terms in a field.
  • Binomial theorem and Fermat. The binomial coefficients are defined without division because Chapter 4 needs them in Fp, where p! is zero. There (x+y)p=xp+yp.
  • Rational to real powers. Section 1.5 stops at rational exponents. Chapter 8 defines ax for every real x from the exponential function. Analysis on the Real Line proves the facts about the exponential that Chapter 8 borrows.
