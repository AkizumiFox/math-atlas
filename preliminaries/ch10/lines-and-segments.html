Lines and Segments

In the plane a line is the set of solutions of one equation ax+by=c. In R3 one equation gives too much. The set {x∈R3:x1+x2+x3=1} contains (1,0,0), (0,1,0) and (0,0,1), and these three points lie on no common line, as we check below. A line is thinner: a point moving along it is fixed by a single number. So this section describes lines by one parameter, not by equations. It then cuts out the part of a line between two points, the segment, and shows that “between” can be said with distances alone.

10.1.1 Lines

Chapter 9 gave every vector v of Rn its multiples tv, and the multiples of a non-zero v form the line through the origin in the direction of v (Section 9.6). A line that misses the origin is the same picture moved: start at a point p and add the multiples of v.

For example, start at (1,0,0) and add the multiples t(−1,1,0): the points are (1−t,t,0) for t∈R. The numbers t=0 and t=1 give (1,0,0) and (0,1,0), and every one of these points has third coordinate 0.

A line is a starting point plus all multiples of one non-zero direction.

Definition 10.1.1 (Lines in Rn)

Let p,v∈Rn with v≠0. We write p+Rv:={p+tv:t∈R}. A subset L⊆Rn is a line if L=p+Rv for some p∈Rn and some v≠0. The map t↦p+tv from R to L is a parametrization of L, with base point p and direction v; the number t is the parameter of the point p+tv.

In words: a line is any set of this form, and the pair (p,v) is a way of naming it, not part of it. The condition v≠0 is the one that bites. Without it, p+R0={p}, a single point. We write Rv for 0+Rv, the line through 0 with direction v. The sums and multiples are those of Definition 9.2.1. So the points (1−t,t,0) form the line (1,0,0)+R(−1,1,0).

Quick check. Decide each, using only the definition.

  • (a) Is a one-point set {p}⊆Rn a line?
  • (b) Is R itself a line in R1=R?
  • (c) Is (3,5,7) on the line (1,1,1)+R(1,2,3)?
  • (d) Can one point of p+Rv have two different parameters?

Solution. Part (d) first. If p+tv=p+uv, then (t−u)v=0, so |t−u|‖v‖=‖(t−u)v‖=0 by Proposition 9.4.1 (b). Since v≠0, ‖v‖>0 (Proposition 9.4.1 (a)), and so |t−u|=0, that is, t=u. So no: distinct parameters give distinct points.

  • (a) No. By (d), the points p+0v and p+1v of a line are different, so a line has at least two points. (b) Yes: R=0+R⋅1, with direction 1≠0. (c) Yes: (1,1,1)+2(1,2,3)=(3,5,7), so its parameter is t=2.

So for a fixed parametrization each point of the line has exactly one parameter. We use this without further comment.

A line has many names. (1,0,0)+R(−1,1,0) is also (0,1,0)+R(2,−2,0): the base point may be any point of the line, and the direction may be scaled. The next result says that this is all the freedom there is.

Proposition 10.1.1 (When Two Parametrizations Give the Same Line)

Let p,p′∈Rn and v,v′∈Rn∖{0}. Then p+Rv=p′+Rv′ if and only if p′∈p+Rv and v′=λv for some real λ≠0.

Idea. For (⇒), two points of the second line, p′ and p′+v′, must lie on the first; subtracting their parametrizations exposes v′ as a multiple of v. For (⇐), rewrite a point of each line in the other’s parametrization.

Proof. (⇐) Let p′=p+sv and v′=λv with λ≠0. For every t∈R, p′+tv′=p+(s+tλ)v∈p+Rv. Conversely, for every t, p+tv=p′+t−sλv′∈p′+Rv′, since the right side is p+sv+(t−s)v; the division is allowed because λ≠0. The two inclusions give equality.

(⇒) The points p′ and p′+v′ (parameters 0 and 1) lie on p′+Rv′=p+Rv. So p′=p+sv and p′+v′=p+uv for some s,u∈R; in particular p′∈p+Rv. Subtracting, v′=(u−s)v. If u−s were 0, then v′=0v=0, against the hypothesis v′≠0. So λ:=u−s is non-zero, as claimed.

Uses: Proposition 9.2.1 (e), (h) (regrouping multiples), (i) (0v=0).

The direction of a line is fixed only up to a non-zero factor, and the base point can be any of its points. Through two different points there is exactly one line.

Proposition 10.1.2 (Two Points Determine a Line)

Let p,q∈Rn with p≠q. Then p+R(q−p) is a line containing p and q, and it is the only line containing both.

Proof. Since p≠q, the direction q−p is non-zero, so p+R(q−p) is a line; its points with parameters 0 and 1 are p and q. Let r+Rv be any line containing p and q, say p=r+sv and q=r+uv. Then q−p=(u−s)v, and u≠s because q≠p. So p∈r+Rv and q−p=λv with λ:=u−s≠0, and Proposition 10.1.1 gives r+Rv=p+R(q−p).

Uses: Proposition 9.2.1 (vector algebra).

Definition 10.1.2 (Collinear Points)

Points of Rn are collinear if one line contains them all.

By Proposition 10.1.2, any two different points are collinear, and a single point p lies on the line p+Rv for any v≠0. Three need not be. The only line through (1,0,0) and (0,1,0) is (1,0,0)+R(−1,1,0), whose points all have third coordinate 0; so (0,0,1) is not on it, and the three points of the opening are not collinear. In particular the solution set of x1+x2+x3=1, which contains all three, is not a line.

10.1.2 Meeting points

Exercise B2 proves that two lines in the plane whose directions are not multiples of each other meet in exactly one point. In space this fails. Two lines of R3 are skew if they have no common point and neither direction is a multiple of the other. (If v′=λv with v′≠0, then λ≠0 and v=λ−1v′, so “neither” is the same as “the first is not a multiple of the second”. By Proposition 10.1.1, any other parametrizations of the two lines have directions λv and μv′ with λ,μ≠0. So whether one direction is a multiple of the other does not depend on which parametrizations are used.)

Example (Two Lines in Space)

Let L1=(1,0,1)+R(1,1,0),L2=(0,2,0)+R(1,0,1),L3=(2,1,1)+R(0,1,1). Do L1 and L2 meet? Do L1 and L3?

Idea (Scratch work). A common point of L1 and L2 is a point that has some parameter on L1 and some parameter on L2. Nothing says the two parameters are equal: the point with parameter 1 on one line may have parameter 5 on the other. So we need two letters, s for L1 and t for L2.

We know We want
x∈L1: x=(1+s,s,1) for some s is there such an x?
x∈L2: x=(t,2,t) for some t find s,t with (1+s,s,1)=(t,2,t)
equal triples: one equation per coordinate 1+s=t, s=2, 1=t: solve, then check all three

We solve two of the equations and test the third.

Solution. The points of L1 are (1+s,s,1) and those of L2 are (t,2,t). They are equal if and only if 1+s=t,s=2,1=t. The second and third equations force s=2 and t=1, and then the first reads 3=1, which is false. So L1∩L2=∅. The directions are not multiples: (1,1,0)=λ(1,0,1) would need λ=1 from the first coordinate and λ=0 from the third. So L1 and L2 are skew.

The points of L3 are (2,1+u,1+u). Equality with (1+s,s,1) means 1+s=2,s=1+u,1=1+u. The third equation gives u=0, the second then gives s=1, and the first holds: 1+1=2. So L1 and L3 meet, at (1+1,1,1)=(2,1,1), the base point of L3.

Keep the pair L1,L2 in mind. Two lines in space can fail to meet although their directions are not multiples of each other. Exercise B2 shows that this cannot happen in the plane. The method used twice in the example is worth stating on its own.

Example (Method: parametrize and solve)

How it goes. Give each line or segment its own parameter, with a different letter for each. Write “the point lies on both” as one equation per coordinate. Solve some of the equations, then check every equation with the values found. For a segment, also check that the parameter lies in [0,1].

When to use it. Deciding whether a point lies on a line, a segment or a ray. Finding where two lines meet, or showing that they do not. Later, finding where a line meets a sphere or a hyperplane.

Common mistakes. Using one letter for both parameters looks only for a point reached “at the same time” on both lines, and so misses most meeting points. Solving two equations and forgetting to test the rest can produce a meeting point that does not exist.

10.1.3 Segments

The part of a line between two of its points is used constantly in analysis: a function is examined along it, or a set is asked to contain it. Starting at p and moving towards q, the points p+t(q−p) with t from 0 to 1 run from p to q. For p=(0,0) and q=(2,4) these points are (2t,4t): the numbers t=0, 12, 1 give (0,0), (1,2), (2,4).

A segment is the part of the line from one point to another.

Definition 10.1.3 (Segment)

Let p,q∈Rn. The segment from p to q is [p,q]:={(1−t)p+tq:t∈[0,1]}. Its midpoint is 12(p+q), the point with t=12.

Three remarks. First, (1−t)p+tq=p+t(q−p), so for p≠q the segment is the part of the line p+R(q−p) with parameters in [0,1]. Second, [p,q]=[q,p]: the point with t in the first is the point with 1−t in the second, and t∈[0,1] if and only if 1−t∈[0,1]. Third, [p,p]={p}, since (1−t)p+tp=p. This one-point segment is allowed, and it is not a line. The midpoint is the point of Chapter 9 (Example (Midpoints and Parallelograms)), equally displaced from both ends.

Segments and intervals. The bracket [p,q] also names a closed interval of R. For n=1 the two readings agree when p≤q. Every (1−t)p+tq=p+t(q−p) with t∈[0,1] lies between p and q. For p<q, every y with p≤y≤q has this form, with t:=(y−p)/(q−p). For p>q, the segment [p,q] is the interval [q,p], while the interval [p,q] is empty. In this chapter [p,q] is a segment whenever p and q have been declared as points of Rn.

A segment contains, with any two of its points, the segment between them. This is the first instance of the property that Section 10.3 names.

Lemma 10.1.3 (A Segment Contains Its Subsegments)

Let p,q∈Rn. If x,y∈[p,q], then [x,y]⊆[p,q].

Idea. Write x and y with their parameters on [p,q]. A point of [x,y] then has a parameter on [p,q] that is an average of the two, and an average of two numbers in [0,1] stays in [0,1].

Proof. Let x=p+s(q−p) and y=p+u(q−p) with s,u∈[0,1], and let t∈[0,1]. Then (1−t)x+ty=p+((1−t)s+tu)(q−p). The numbers 1−t, t, s, u are all ≥0, and s,u≤1. Multiplying 0≤s≤1 by 1−t≥0 keeps both inequalities, so 0≤(1−t)s≤(1−t)⋅1. In the same way 0≤tu≤t⋅1. Adding the two gives 0≤(1−t)s+tu≤(1−t)+t=1. So (1−t)x+ty∈[p,q].

Uses: Proposition 9.2.1 (the displayed identity); F3 Proposition 4.1.2 (d) (multiply by a number ≥0); F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (b) (add inequalities).

Quick check. Is (2,0,1) on the segment [(1,−1,2),(3,0,0)]?

Solution. No. A point of the segment is (1,−1,2)+t(2,1,−2) with t∈[0,1]. For (2,0,1) this asks for (1,1,−1)=t(2,1,−2). The first coordinate forces t=12, the second forces t=1. No t works, so the point is not even on the line through the two ends.

10.1.4 Between by distances

For any three points the triangle inequality gives ‖p−q‖≤‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖: the detour through x is never shorter. Take p=(0,0) and q=(4,0). The point x=(1,0) gives 1+3=4, no loss. The point x=(1,1) gives 2+10>4. A picture suggests that the detour costs nothing exactly when x lies on the segment [p,q]. Chapter 9 found exactly when the triangle inequality is an equality: ‖u+w‖=‖u‖+‖w‖ holds if and only if u=0 or w=cu for some real c≥0 (Theorem 9.5.3 (a)). That is all we need.

Theorem 10.1.4 (Points Between Two Points)

Let p,q,x∈Rn. Then x∈[p,q]⟺‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖=‖p−q‖. Moreover, if x=(1−t)p+tq with t∈[0,1], then ‖p−x‖=t‖p−q‖,‖x−q‖=(1−t)‖p−q‖.

Idea (Scratch work). The direction (⇒) is a computation: the two pieces of the segment are multiples of q−p by t and 1−t. For (⇐) we have an equality and want a parameter. Name the two displacements, u:=x−p and w:=q−x, so that u+w=q−p, and plan with a table.

We know We want
‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖=‖p−q‖ x=p+t(q−p) for some t∈[0,1]
‖u‖+‖w‖=‖u+w‖, as ‖−v‖=‖v‖ the same
u=0, or w=cu with c≥0 (Chapter 9) case u=0: x=p, t=0
w=cu, so q−p=(1+c)u u=11+c(q−p): t=11+c∈(0,1]

The step from ‖u‖+‖w‖=‖u+w‖ to “u=0 or w=cu with c≥0” is the equality case of the triangle inequality. It turns one equation between numbers into a relation between vectors. The rest is solving for u.

Proof. (⇒) and the moreover clause. Let x=(1−t)p+tq with t∈[0,1]. Then x−p=t(q−p) and q−x=(1−t)(q−p). Since t≥0 and 1−t≥0, the rule ‖cv‖=|c|‖v‖ of Proposition 9.4.1 (b) gives ‖p−x‖=‖x−p‖=t‖q−p‖,‖x−q‖=(1−t)‖q−p‖. Here ‖p−x‖=‖x−p‖ and ‖q−x‖=‖x−q‖ are the same rule with c=−1. Also ‖q−p‖=‖p−q‖. This is the moreover clause, and adding the two equations gives ‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖=(t+1−t)‖p−q‖=‖p−q‖.

(⇐) Let u:=x−p and w:=q−x, so that u+w=q−p. Since ‖−v‖=‖v‖ for every v, the hypothesis reads ‖u‖+‖w‖=‖u+w‖. By Theorem 9.5.3 (a), u=0 or w=cu for some real c≥0.

Case u=0. Then x=p=(1−0)p+0q∈[p,q].

Case w=cu with c≥0. Then q−p=u+cu=(1+c)u. Since 1+c≥1>0, the number t:=1/(1+c) exists and t>0. Taking reciprocals of the positive numbers in 1≤1+c reverses the inequality, so t≤1. Multiplying by t gives u=t(q−p). So x=p+u=p+t(q−p)=(1−t)p+tq with t∈[0,1]. This proves the theorem.

Uses: Proposition 9.2.1 (vector algebra); Proposition 9.4.1 (b) (‖cv‖=|c|‖v‖); F3 Definition 4.1.1 (|t|=t for t≥0); F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (b), (e) (1+c≥1>0); F3 Proposition 3.2.2 (a) (1/(1+c)>0); F3 Proposition 4.1.2 (c) (reciprocals reverse ≤).

Where is c≥0 used? It gives 1+c≥1. So t=1/(1+c) exists and is positive, and taking reciprocals gives t≤1. A multiplier c<0 with c≠−1 gives t<0 or t>1, a point of the line outside the segment, and the equality case rules such multipliers out.

The right side of the theorem mentions only distances, no sums of vectors and no parameters. That is the form of “between” that survives when Rn is replaced by any set with a distance, as in Analysis on the Real Line, Chapter 3. It is also why a map that preserves distances sends segments to segments, a fact to be proved in Affine and Projective Geometry.

Example (Method: use the equality case)

How it goes. A known inequality A≤B is given, or proved, to hold with A=B. Recall its exact equality condition. Typically it reads “one vector is a non-negative multiple of the other, or is zero”. Write that condition with a named multiplier. Then solve for what you want, keeping every case of the condition.

When to use it. A sum of distances equals a distance. Cauchy–Schwarz holds with equality. A question asks for all extreme cases of an inequality.

Common mistakes. Dropping the sign condition loses the point: c≥0 is exactly what separates [p,q] from the rest of the line through p and q. Forgetting the case u=0 leaves a gap, because when u=0 and w≠0 no multiplier exists.

The moreover clause of the theorem also tells us where on the segment a point sits: its distance from p fixes it.

Corollary 10.1.5 (A Point Is Pinned by Its Distances to the Ends)

Let p,q∈Rn and let s,r≥0 with s+r=‖p−q‖. Then exactly one x∈Rn satisfies ‖p−x‖=s and ‖x−q‖=r. If p≠q, it is x=p+s‖p−q‖(q−p); if p=q, it is p.

Proof. Let p≠q, so ‖p−q‖>0 (Proposition 9.4.1 (a)), and put t:=s/‖p−q‖. From 0≤s≤s+r=‖p−q‖, dividing by the positive number ‖p−q‖ gives 0≤t≤1. So the point x:=p+t(q−p)=(1−t)p+tq lies on [p,q]. By the moreover clause of Theorem 10.1.4, ‖p−x‖=t‖p−q‖=s and ‖x−q‖=(1−t)‖p−q‖=‖p−q‖−s=r.

Now let y be any point with ‖p−y‖=s and ‖y−q‖=r. Then ‖p−y‖+‖y−q‖=‖p−q‖, so y∈[p,q] by Theorem 10.1.4, say y=(1−t′)p+t′q with t′∈[0,1]. The moreover clause gives s=t′‖p−q‖, so t′=t and y=x. The case p=q, where the formula would divide by 0, is treated next.

Uses: Proposition 9.4.1 (a); F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (b), (c) and F3 Proposition 3.2.2 (a) (divide by a positive number); F3 Lemma 4.1.1 (cancel a non-zero factor).

↺Second readingThe case p = q

If p=q, then s+r=0 with s,r≥0. If s>0, then s+r>0 by F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (b), which is impossible; so s=0, and then r=0. A point with ‖p−x‖=0 is p itself (Proposition 9.4.1 (a)), and p does have both distances equal to 0. So p is the only such point. The formula of the main case cannot be used here: it divides by ‖p−q‖=0.

Quick check. Show that the midpoint of [p,q] is the only point of Rn at distance 12‖p−q‖ from both p and q.

Solution. Take s=r=12‖p−q‖. Both are ≥0 and s+r=‖p−q‖, so by Corollary 10.1.5 exactly one point has these two distances. The midpoint, with t=12, has them, by the moreover clause of Theorem 10.1.4. So it is that one point.

Being the midpoint takes two equations, one for each distance. Keeping only “equally far from both ends” loses the point.

Warning. Equal distances do not make a midpoint. Let p=(−1,0), q=(1,0) and x=(0,1). Then ‖p−x‖=‖(−1,−1)‖=2 and ‖x−q‖=‖(−1,1)‖=2, so x is equally far from p and q. But x∉[p,q]: every point (1−t)p+tq has second coordinate 0. The sum 22 is not ‖p−q‖=2. Section 10.2 finds all points equally far from p and q.

10.1.5 Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Parametrize the line through (1,−1,2) and (3,0,0). Is (5,1,−2) on it?
  • (b) Find the midpoint of the segment between these two points.
  • (c) Show that (1,2), (3,5) and (7,11) are collinear, and find one of them that lies on the segment between the other two.
  • (d) In the proof of Theorem 10.1.4, direction (⇐), where is c≥0 used? Take p=0, q=(1,0) and x=(2,0), a point of the line through p and q. Which hypothesis of the theorem fails, and what does the equality case of the triangle inequality say about u=x−p and w=q−x?

Solution.

  • (a) By Proposition 10.1.2, the line is (1,−1,2)+R(2,1,−2), since (3,0,0)−(1,−1,2)=(2,1,−2). For t=2, (1,−1,2)+2(2,1,−2)=(5,1,−2), so yes, with parameter 2.

  • (b) 12((1,−1,2)+(3,0,0))=12(4,−1,2)=(2,−12,1).

  • (c) Put p=(1,2) and v=(2,3). Then (3,5)=p+1v and (7,11)=p+3v, since (6,9)=3(2,3). So all three lie on p+Rv, with parameters 0, 1, 3. The middle one is between the others: (3,5)=(1,2)+13((7,11)−(1,2)), so (3,5)∈[(1,2),(7,11)] with t=13.

  • (d) It is used twice. It gives 1+c≥1>0, so t=1/(1+c) exists and t>0. Taking reciprocals in 1≤1+c gives t≤1. Positivity alone would not do: with c=−12, 1+c>0 but t=2>1.

For x=(2,0): ‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖=2+1=3≠1=‖p−q‖, so the hypothesis of (⇐) fails. Here u=(2,0) and w=(−1,0)=−12u. Since u≠0, the only c with w=cu is −12<0. By the equality case of Theorem 9.5.3 (a), equality fails, in agreement with 1<3. Indeed q−p=12u, so x=p+2(q−p) lies on the line beyond q.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1, used later)

Let p,v∈Rn with v≠0, and let a<b be real numbers. Prove that p+tv∈[p+av,p+bv] if and only if a≤t≤b.

Solution. (⇐) Let a≤t≤b and s:=(t−a)/(b−a), allowed since b−a>0. From 0≤t−a≤b−a, dividing by b−a>0 gives s∈[0,1]. Then (1−s)(p+av)+s(p+bv)=p+(a+s(b−a))v=p+tv, so p+tv∈[p+av,p+bv].

(⇒) Let p+tv=(1−s)(p+av)+s(p+bv) with s∈[0,1]. The right side is p+(a+s(b−a))v. A point of p+Rv has only one parameter (p+tv=p+uv forces t=u), so t=a+s(b−a). Since 0≤s(b−a)≤b−a (F3 Proposition 4.1.2 (d)), adding a gives a≤t≤b.

Exercise (B2, used later)

Let v,v′∈R2 be non-zero.

  • (a) Prove that v′ is a multiple of v if and only if v1v2′−v2v1′=0.
  • (b) Suppose v′ is not a multiple of v. Prove that for all p,p′∈R2 the lines p+Rv and p′+Rv′ meet in exactly one point.

Solution.

  • (a) (⇒) If v′=λv, then v1v2′−v2v1′=v1λv2−v2λv1=0.

    (⇐) Let v1v2′=v2v1′. If v1≠0, put λ:=v1′/v1. Then λv1=v1′ and λv2=v1′v2/v1=v1v2′/v1=v2′, so v′=λv. If v1=0, then v2≠0 because v≠0, and the hypothesis reads 0=v2v1′, so v1′=0 (no zero divisors, F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (f)). Put λ:=v2′/v2: then λv1=0=v1′ and λv2=v2′.

  • (b) By Example (Method: parametrize and solve), with s on the first line and t on the second. Let d:=p′−p. Then p+sv=p′+tv′ if and only if sv1−tv1′=d1,sv2−tv2′=d2. Let D:=v1v2′−v2v1′, which is non-zero by (a). Multiplying the first equation by v2′, the second by v1′, and subtracting eliminates t; multiplying by v2 and v1 and subtracting eliminates s. Any solution therefore satisfies sD=d1v2′−d2v1′,tD=d1v2−d2v1. Since D≠0, there is at most one pair (s,t), namely s=(d1v2′−d2v1′)/D and t=(d1v2−d2v1)/D. Substituting, sv1−tv1′=d1(v1v2′−v2v1′)/D=d1, and likewise sv2−tv2′=d2; so this pair is a solution. Each line has one parameter per point, so exactly one point is common to both.
Exercise (B3)

Show that the lines R(1,0,0) and (0,1,0)+R(0,0,1) of R3 are skew.

Solution. By Example (Method: parametrize and solve): the points of the lines are (s,0,0) and (0,1,t). Their second coordinates are 0 and 1, so no point is on both. The directions are not multiples: (1,0,0)=λ(0,0,1) fails in the first coordinate, whatever λ is. So the lines are skew.

Exercise (B4: Prove or disprove)

Prove or disprove each statement, for points p,q,x∈Rn and directions v,w≠0.

  • (a) If ‖p−x‖=‖x−q‖=12‖p−q‖, then x=12(p+q).
  • (b) If ‖p−x‖=‖x−q‖, then x∈[p,q].
  • (c) Any three points of R2 are collinear.
  • (d) If p+Rv=p+Rw, then v=w.
  • (e) Two lines with two distinct common points are equal.

Solution.

  • (a) True. By Corollary 10.1.5 with s=r=12‖p−q‖, exactly one point has these two distances. The midpoint has them, by the moreover clause of Theorem 10.1.4.

  • (b) False. Take p=(−1,0), q=(1,0), x=(0,1). Then ‖p−x‖=‖x−q‖=2, but x has second coordinate 1, while every point of [p,q] has second coordinate 0.

  • (c) False. By Proposition 10.1.2, the only line through (0,0) and (1,0) is R(1,0), whose points have second coordinate 0; so (0,1) is not on it.

  • (d) False. p+Rv=p+R(2v) by Proposition 10.1.1 with λ=2, but 2v≠v, since 2v−v=v≠0.

  • (e) True. If both lines contain a≠b, both equal the line a+R(b−a) by Proposition 10.1.2.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Chapter check: one point in the middle)

Prove that of three distinct collinear points of Rn, exactly one lies on the segment between the other two.

Solution. Let the three points lie on the line p+Rv, v≠0. Each has one parameter, and distinct points have distinct parameters, since equal parameters give equal points. Since ≤ is a total order (F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (i)), three distinct reals can be listed in increasing order. So we can name the points xi=p+tiv with t1<t2<t3.

Existence. By Exercise (B1, used later) with a=t1, b=t3 and t=t2, x2∈[x1,x3].

Uniqueness. Suppose x1∈[x2,x3]. By Exercise (B1, used later) with a=t2<b=t3, this gives t2≤t1, against t1<t2. Suppose x3∈[x1,x2]. The same exercise with a=t1<b=t2 gives t3≤t2, against t2<t3. So x2 is the only one of the three between the other two.

What a complete answer needs. (10 points) - 2: puts the three points on one parametrization, with distinct parameters, and says why they are distinct. - 2: orders the parameters. - 2: shows that the point with the middle parameter is between the others (by B1 or directly). - 2: excludes x1, either by B1’s (⇒) (citing it suffices) or by comparing distances with Theorem 10.1.4. - 2: excludes x3 in the same way.

A route through Theorem 10.1.4 (comparing ‖x1−x3‖=(t3−t1)‖v‖ with the sums of the other distances) earns the same points step for step.

Slips that lose the step (0 points for it): using one parameter for a point on two different parametrizations; excluding only one of the outer points.
