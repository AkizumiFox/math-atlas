Chapter 10

Lines, Hyperplanes, and Convex Sets

Chapter 9 gave Rn a distance. This chapter asks how much of the familiar geometry that distance already decides. A great deal: lines and segments, spheres and balls are all sets cut out by distances, and “x lies between p and q” is a statement about three distances and nothing else. One linear equation ⟨a,x⟩=c cuts out a hyperplane, and the distance from a point to it has a formula. A set is convex when it contains the segment between any two of its points. The smallest convex set containing a given set is the set of its convex combinations.

Analysis uses all of this from its first chapters on. The mean value theorem moves along a segment, and a ball is the set of points where an estimate holds. In a convex set, an argument made along one segment works between any two points.

What you need.

  • Vectors, the dot product, the norm and the distance, from Chapter 9, Sections 9.2 to 9.4 (Definition 9.4.1).
  • The triangle inequality and its equality case, from Section 9.5 (Theorem 9.5.3).
  • Projection onto a line and the closest-point theorem, from Section 9.6 (Theorem 9.6.1).
  • The rules of an ordered field and square roots in R, from Number Systems: From N to R (F3 Proposition 6.1.4).
  • Finite sums and induction, from Chapter 1 and Sets and Functions, for convex combinations of many points (F2 Theorem 3.2.1).

Time. About 15 hours if you read the main text and do every A exercise and one B and one C exercise per section; about 20 hours for everything.

Roadmap

  • Lines and Segments. Lines p+Rv, segments [p,q], and the theorem that x lies on [p,q] exactly when ‖p−x‖+‖x−q‖=‖p−q‖ (Theorem 10.1.4).
  • Hyperplanes and Nearest Points. Hyperplanes ⟨a,x⟩=c, the nearest point on a line or a hyperplane with the distance to it (Proposition 10.2.4), and how a line meets a sphere.
  • Convex Sets and Hulls. Convex sets, which include balls (Proposition 10.3.1), convex combinations, and the convex hull of a set as the set of convex combinations of its points.
