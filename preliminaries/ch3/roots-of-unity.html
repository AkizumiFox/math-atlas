Higher Roots, Roots of Unity, and a Look Ahead

Section 3.4 solved z2=c with real square roots alone. Polar form turns the general equation zn=c into a question about lengths and angles: by De Moivre’s formula (Theorem 3.5.5), raising to the n-th power raises the modulus to the n-th power and multiplies the argument by n. The angle part is a linear equation for a real number, with the ambiguity 2πZ of Proposition 3.5.4 attached. The length part is the real equation rn=ρ with ρ>0, and Chapter 1 already showed that it has exactly one positive solution. This section recalls that, solves zn=c, counts the solutions, studies the case c=1, and ends with a look ahead at polynomial equations in general.

3.6.1 Real n-th roots

Chapter 1, Section 1.5, proved that every real number a≥0 has exactly one non-negative n-th root an, by the move construct a point as a supremum (Lemma 1.5.1), and that 0≤s<t gives sn<tn (Lemma 1.5.1 (a)). We use both here, with the difference of powers (Lemma 1.1.8) behind them, for the roots of complex numbers below.

3.6.2 Solving the equation z to the n equals c

Now the main result. Every non-zero complex number has exactly n different n-th roots, and polar form lists them.

Theorem 3.6.1 (n-th Roots of a Complex Number)

Let n∈N with n≥1, and let c∈C with c≠0. Let c=ρeiα be a polar form of c (Proposition 3.5.4), and for k∈Z put zk:=ρnei(α+2πk)/n.

  • (a) For z∈C: zn=c if and only if z=zk for some k∈Z.
  • (b) For k,l∈Z: zk=zl if and only if n∣k−l (F2 Definition 1.1.2).
  • (c) The map {0,1,…,n−1}→{z∈C:zn=c}, k↦zk, is a bijection. So c has exactly n n-th roots (F2 Definition 4.1.2).

In particular every non-zero complex number has an n-th root, and every u∈T has one in T.

Idea. Write the unknown in polar form, z=reiθ. De Moivre turns zn=c into rneinθ=ρeiα, and the uniqueness half of polar form splits that into a real equation for the length, rn=ρ, and an equation for the angle, nθ∈α+2πZ. The first has one solution, r=ρn. The second has the solutions θ=(α+2πk)/n, one for each integer k. Different k can give the same point, and they do exactly when the angles differ by a whole number of turns, that is, when n∣k−l. So the k that matter are the remainders 0,…,n−1.

Before reading on: where is the uniqueness half of the polar form used, and where the uniqueness of the real n-th root?

Proof. Here ρ=|c|>0 by Proposition 3.5.4 (a), so ρn≥0 exists by Lemma 1.5.1 (b), and ρn>0, since 0n=0≠ρ.

  • (a) Let z∈C. If z=0, then zn=0n−1⋅0=0≠c (F3 Proposition 2.3.2 (b)), and z is no zk, since every zk has modulus ρn>0. So let z≠0, and fix a polar form z=reiθ (Proposition 3.5.4 (a)). By Theorem 3.5.5, zn=rneinθ, a polar form with rn>0. By Proposition 3.5.4 (b), zn=c⟺rn=ρ  and  nθ−α∈2πZ. Since r>0, the uniqueness in Lemma 1.5.1 (b) gives rn=ρ⟺r=ρn. And nθ−α=2πk holds for a given k∈Z exactly when θ=(α+2πk)/n.

    (⇒) If zn=c, then r=ρn and θ=(α+2πk)/n for some k∈Z, so z=zk.

    (⇐) If z=zk, use the polar form r:=ρn, θ:=(α+2πk)/n of z in the equivalence above. Both conditions on the right hold, so zn=c.

  • (b) The numbers zk and zl are given in polar form with the same modulus. By Proposition 3.5.4 (b), zk=zl if and only if α+2πkn−α+2πln=2π(k−l)n∈2πZ, that is, if and only if 2π(k−l)/n=2πm for some m∈Z. Multiplying by n/(2π), and back, this says k−l=mn, an equation between integers. So zk=zl if and only if n∣k−l.

  • (c) By (a), each zk is a solution, so the map is into the set of solutions. Injective: let 0≤l<k≤n−1. Then 0<k−l<n. If zk=zl, then n∣k−l by (b), and since n>0 and k−l>0, F3 Proposition 2.5.1 (e) gives n≤k−l, a contradiction. So zk≠zl. Surjective: let zn=c. By (a), z=zk for some k∈Z. Division with remainder (F3 Theorem 2.5.2 (a)) gives k=qn+j with q∈Z and 0≤j≤n−1. Then n∣k−j, so z=zk=zj by (b). Hence the map is a bijection. Composing it with the bijection {1,…,n}→{0,…,n−1}, j↦j−1, shows that the set of solutions has n elements (F2 Definition 4.1.2).

Finally, z0 is an n-th root of c. If |c|=1, then ρ=1 and 1n=1, since 1≥0 and 1n=1; so every zk has modulus 1 and lies in T.

The answer to the prompt: the uniqueness half of the polar form, Proposition 3.5.4 (b), turns the one complex equation zn=c into the two real conditions, and is used again in (b) to decide when two of the zk coincide. The uniqueness of the real root forces the modulus, r=ρn. The ambiguity 2πZ in the angle is what produces n roots instead of one.

Warning. Dividing the angle equation by n too early loses roots. From z3=1, z=eiθ, one might write e3iθ=ei0, “so 3θ=0 and z=1”. But e3iθ=ei0 only gives 3θ∈2πZ (Proposition 3.5.4 (b)), and the angles 2π/3 and 4π/3 give two more cube roots of 1. Only the remainders 0,…,n−1 of k matter, but all of them do.

3.6.3 Roots of unity

The most important case is c=1.

Example (Roots of Unity)

Let n≥1 be an integer. Find all z∈C with zn=1, and draw them for n=6.

Solution. Apply Theorem 3.6.1 with c=1=1⋅ei0 (Theorem 3.5.3 (b)): ρ=1, α=0, and 1n=1. The solutions are zk=e2πik/n,k∈Z. Put ω:=e2πi/n. By Theorem 3.5.5, zk=(e2πi/n)k=ωk for k∈N. By Theorem 3.6.1 (c), there are exactly n solutions, 1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1, the n-th roots of unity. They lie on T, at the arguments 2πk/n, equally spaced around the circle: multiplying by ω turns each one into the next (Theorem 3.5.3 (a)), and ωn−1 back into ωn=zn=z0=1 (Theorem 3.6.1 (b)). For n≥3 they are the corners of a regular polygon with n corners, one of them at 1; for n=2 they are 1 and ω=eiπ=−1 (Example (Special Angles) (i)); for n=1 only 1.

For n=6, ω=eiπ/3=12+32i (Example (Special Angles) (iii)), and ω3=eiπ=−1. Hence ω2=(12+32i)2=14−34+32i=−12+32i, ω4=−ω and ω5=−ω2. The six roots are 1,12+32i,−12+32i,−1,−12−32i,12−32i.

The six roots sum to 0: since ω3=−1, we have ωk+3=−ωk, so the roots cancel in the opposite pairs {1,ω3}, {ω,ω4}, {ω2,ω5}. In the picture, the hexagon is symmetric about its center 0. In general the sum is Proposition 3.6.2 below.

We write μn:={z∈C:zn=1}={1,ω,…,ωn−1} for the set of n-th roots of unity. It is a subset of T, since each element is some zk=e2πik/n (Theorem 3.6.1 (a)). It contains 1, and it is closed under products, since (ζη)n=ζnηn=1 for ζ,η∈μn (Proposition 1.1.1 (c)). It is closed under inverses, since ζ⋅ζn−1=ζn=1 gives ζ−1=ζn−1, a product of elements of μn (for n=1, ζ0=1). Chapter 5 treats μn, like T, as an example of a group.

The roots of unity also describe the roots of any c≠0: the root z0=ρneiα/n of Theorem 3.6.1 satisfies zk=z0ωk for k∈N, by Theorem 3.5.3 (a) and Theorem 3.5.5, since (α+2πk)/n=α/n+2πk/n. So by Theorem 3.6.1 (c) the n-th roots of c are z0,z0ω,…,z0ωn−1: one of them times the n-th roots of unity.

The six roots canceled in pairs. The same pairing works for every even n, since ωn/2=eiπ=−1 (Theorem 3.5.5, Example (Special Angles) (i)) gives ωk+n/2=−ωk; but for odd n there are no opposite pairs: if ζ and −ζ were both n-th roots of unity, then 1=(−ζ)n=(−1)nζn=−1. A different argument is needed. It is Lemma 1.1.8 once more.

Proposition 3.6.2 (Sums of Roots of Unity)

Let n∈N with n≥2, and let ω:=e2πi/n.

  • (a) For m∈N: ωm=1 if and only if n∣m. In particular ω≠1.
  • (b) ∑k=0n−1ωk=0.
  • (c) For m∈N, ∑k=0n−1ωmk={nif n∣m,0otherwise.

Idea. Put ζ:=ωm. The sum in (c) is 1+ζ+⋯+ζn−1, and Lemma 1.1.8 multiplies it by ζ−1 to give ζn−1=0. If ζ≠1 we may cancel, and the sum is 0; if ζ=1 it is n. Part (a) decides which case occurs.

Proof. We use the notation of Example (Roots of Unity): zk=e2πik/n are the roots of c=1 in Theorem 3.6.1, and ωm=zm for m∈N (Theorem 3.5.5).

  • (a) Since 1=z0, Theorem 3.6.1 (b) gives ωm=1⟺zm=z0⟺n∣m. For m=1: if n∣1, then n≤1 by F3 Proposition 2.5.1 (e), against n≥2. So ω≠1.

  • (b) Let ζ:=ωm. By Proposition 1.1.1 (b), ωmk=ζk for each k, and ζn=ωmn=(ωn)m=1m=1, since ωn=zn=z0=1 by Theorem 3.6.1 (b).

Case n∣m. Then ζ=1 by (a), and the sum is ∑k=0n−11=n.

Case n∤m. Then ζ≠1 by (a). By Lemma 1.1.8 with w=1, 0=ζn−1=(ζ−1)∑k=0n−1ζk. Since ζ−1≠0 and C has no zero divisors (Theorem 3.2.4, F3 Lemma 4.1.1 (d)), the sum is 0.

  • (b) This is (c) with m=1, since n∤1 by (a).

Comparing real and imaginary parts in (b), with ωk=cos⁡(2πk/n)+isin⁡(2πk/n) (Theorem 3.5.5), gives two real identities that are awkward to prove any other way: for n≥2, ∑k=0n−1cos⁡2πkn=0and∑k=0n−1sin⁡2πkn=0. Part (c) says more: the sum of the m-th powers of the roots of unity detects whether n divides m.

Quick check. Write the three cube roots of unity in the form a+bi.

Solution. By Example (Roots of Unity) with n=3, they are 1, ω, ω2 with ω=e2πi/3=(eiπ/3)2=−12+32i (Theorem 3.5.5, and the square computed for n=6 above). Since ω∈T and ω⋅ω2=ω3=1, ω2=ω−1=ω―=−12−32i (Proposition 3.5.1 (a)). So the cube roots of unity are 1 and −12±32i. Without angles: z3−1=(z−1)(z2+z+1), and Corollary 3.4.2 applied to z2+z+1=0, with d=3i a square root of 1−4=−3, gives z=(−1±3i)/2.

3.6.4 Polynomial equations, and a look ahead

An n-th root of c solves zn−c=0, a polynomial equation of degree n with only two non-zero coefficients. The general question is whether every equation anzn+an−1zn−1+⋯+a1z+a0=0,a0,…,an∈C, an≠0, n≥1, has a solution in C. Here the left side is a polynomial expression ∑k=0nakzk, a finite sum; Chapter 6 makes polynomials objects in their own right. We built C so that one such equation, z2+1=0, has a solution, and Section 3.4 solved every equation of degree 2. It turns out that nothing more ever needs to be added.

Remark (Looking ahead). Section 7.6 proves that every such equation has a solution in C: the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Its proof uses only the square roots of Section 3.4 and the completeness of R, not polar form and not the borrowed facts about cosine and sine. With division of polynomials, Chapter 7 deduces that every complex polynomial of degree n≥1 is a non-zero constant times a product of n factors of the form z−zj. Nothing in this chapter or in Chapters 4–6 uses the theorem.

One companion fact needs no heavy machinery: when the coefficients are real, the solutions that are not real come in conjugate pairs.

Proposition 3.6.3 (Real Coefficients and Conjugate Roots)

Let n∈N, let a0,…,an∈R, and let z∈C. Then ∑k=0nakzk―=∑k=0nakz―k. Hence, if ∑k=0nakz0k=0 for some z0∈C, then also ∑k=0nakz0―k=0.

Proof. First, zk―=z―k for every k∈N, by F1 Axiom 4.2.1 on k: z0―=1―=1=z―0, since 1 is real (Proposition 3.2.1); and if zk―=z―k, then zk+1―=zkz―=zk―z―=z―kz―=z―k+1, by Theorem 3.2.2 (b). Next, ak―=ak since ak is real (Proposition 3.2.1), so akzk―=akz―k, again by Theorem 3.2.2 (b). Finally, the conjugate of a finite sum is the sum of the conjugates, by induction on the number of terms along the recursion for finite sums, with Theorem 3.2.2 (a) in the step. This proves the identity. If ∑akz0k=0, then ∑akz0―k=0―=0.

A solution z0 that is not real gives a second solution: z0―≠z0, since z0=x+yi with y≠0 has z0―=x−yi, and −y≠y. For example, z2−2z+5=0 has the solution 1+2i, since (1+2i)2−2(1+2i)+5=(−3+4i)−(2+4i)+5=0; so it also has the solution 1−2i. Chapter 7 uses this to factor real polynomials into real factors of degree 1 and 2.

3.6.5 Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Find all z∈C with z4=1.
  • (b) In the proof of Theorem 3.6.1, where is the uniqueness half of Proposition 3.5.4 used?
  • (c) In the same proof, why is the modulus r of a solution forced to be ρn?
  • (d) True or false: z3=1 has exactly three solutions in C, and exactly one of them is real. Justify your answer.
  • (e) State the idea of the proof of Theorem 3.6.1 in two sentences.

Solution.

  • (a) By Example (Roots of Unity) with n=4, the solutions are ωk for k=0,1,2,3, where ω=e2πi/4=eiπ/2=i (Example (Special Angles) (ii)): 1, i, −1, −i.

  • (b) Twice. In (a), to turn rneinθ=ρeiα into the two conditions rn=ρ and nθ−α∈2πZ. In (b), to decide when zk=zl.

  • (c) Because r>0 and rn=ρ, and Lemma 1.5.1 (b) says that ρn is the only non-negative real number with n-th power ρ.

  • (d) True. By Theorem 3.6.1 (c) there are exactly three. By the Check above they are 1 and −12±32i; the last two have imaginary part ±32≠0, so only 1 is real.

  • (e) Polar form and De Moivre split zn=c into a real equation for the modulus, which has exactly one positive solution, and an equation for the argument, which is solved up to the ambiguity 2πZ. That ambiguity, divided by n, produces n different roots, one for each remainder modulo n.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Exit check: n-th roots)

Let n≥1 and c∈C with c≠0, and let c=ρeiα be a polar form. Prove parts (a) and (b) of Theorem 3.6.1: the solutions of zn=c are exactly the numbers zk=ρnei(α+2πk)/n, k∈Z, and zk=zl if and only if n∣k−l. Cite the polar form, De Moivre’s formula and the real n-th root where you use them.

Solution. Here ρ=|c|>0 (Proposition 3.5.4 (a)). The number 0 is not a solution, since 0n=0≠c, and is no zk, since |zk|=ρn>0 (Lemma 1.5.1 (b) gives ρn≥0, and 0n=0≠ρ). For z≠0, fix a polar form z=reiθ with r>0 (Proposition 3.5.4 (a)); then zn=rneinθ (Theorem 3.5.5), a polar form with rn>0, and by the uniqueness of polar form (Proposition 3.5.4 (b)), zn=ρeiα if and only if rn=ρ and nθ−α∈2πZ. The first condition is r=ρn, by existence and uniqueness of the real n-th root (Lemma 1.5.1 (b)); the second is θ=(α+2πk)/n for some k∈Z. So zn=c implies z=zk; conversely, applied to the polar form r:=ρn, θ:=(α+2πk)/n of z=zk, the equivalence gives zn=c. For (b), zk and zl are given in polar form with the same modulus, so the same uniqueness gives zk=zl if and only if 2π(k−l)/n∈2πZ, if and only if k−l=mn for some m∈Z, that is, n∣k−l.

Exercise (C2: Find the error)

Here is an argument.

Claim. For n≥2, the n-th roots of unity sum to 0.

  1. Let ω:=e2πi/n and S:=1+ω+⋯+ωn−1. (2) Then ωS=ω+ω2+⋯+ωn=S, since ωn=1. (3) So (ω−1)S=0. (4) Since ω is an n-th root of unity, ωn=1, so ω≠1. (5) Hence ω−1≠0, and S=0.

Is the claim true? Does the argument prove it? If not, name the first line that fails, say what is wrong with it, give a counterexample to the step, and repair the argument.

Solution. The claim is true: it is Proposition 3.6.2 (b).

The argument does not prove it. Lines (1)–(3) are correct: ωn=1 by Example (Roots of Unity), and (3) is (2) rearranged. Line (5) is correct given (4), since C has no zero divisors. Line (4) fails. “ωn=1, so ω≠1” cites a general “fact”, that an n-th root of unity is not 1, which is false: this is the error (N9) of F1 Example (What is not allowed). Counterexample to the step: 1 itself satisfies 1n=1, and 1=1. Being an n-th root of unity gives no information that separates ω from 1; the information is in the particular choice ω=e2πi/n with n≥2.

Repair. If ω=e2πi/n=1=ei0, then 2π/n∈2πZ by Proposition 3.5.4 (b), so 1/n=m for some m∈Z, that is, 1=mn and n∣1. Since n≥2>0, F3 Proposition 2.5.1 (e) gives n≤1, a contradiction. So ω≠1, and lines (5) and the conclusion stand. (This repair uses (T1)–(T3) of Axiom 3.5.2, through Proposition 3.5.4 (b), and no value of the cosine.)

R. Review

Exercise (R1)

Let σ:C→C satisfy σ(z+w)=σ(z)+σ(w) and σ(zw)=σ(z)σ(w) for all z,w∈C, σ(1)=1, and σ(a)∈R for every a∈R.

  • (a) Prove that either σ(z)=z for every z∈C, or σ(z)=z― for every z∈C.
  • (b) Show that conjugation z↦z― satisfies all the hypotheses on σ.

Solution.

  • (a) Since σ maps R into R, it restricts to a map τ:R→R, τ(a):=σ(a), which preserves sums and products and sends 1 to 1. Since R is a complete ordered field (F3 Theorem 5.5.2), F3 Theorem 6.3.4 gives τ=idR: σ(a)=a for every real a. In particular σ(−1)=−1, so σ(i)2=σ(i⋅i)=σ(−1)=−1=i2. So (σ(i)−i)(σ(i)+i)=0, and σ(i)=i or σ(i)=−i (no zero divisors in C; or Proposition 3.4.1 (b)). For z=a+bi with a,b∈R, σ(z)=σ(a)+σ(b)σ(i)=a+bσ(i). If σ(i)=i, then σ(z)=z for every z; if σ(i)=−i, then σ(z)=a−bi=z― for every z.

  • (b) Conjugation preserves sums and products by Theorem 3.2.2 (a), (b), and a―=a for every real a (Proposition 3.2.1), so in particular 1―=1 and real numbers go to real numbers.

The comparison with Number Systems: From N to R, Chapter 6 (F3 Theorem 6.3.4) is the point: R has only the identity, while C has exactly two such maps. In R the order can be read off from the field operations (the non-negative numbers are exactly the squares, F3 Proposition 6.1.4), and a map preserving + and ⋅ must preserve it. In C there is no order (Proposition 3.4.3), and nothing written with +, ⋅ and real numbers alone tells i from −i.

Exercise (R2)

Let n≥1. Show that [k]↦e2πik/n is a well-defined bijection f:Z/nZ→μn, and that f([k]+[l])=f([k])f([l]) for all k,l∈Z.

Solution. Write zk:=e2πik/n, the roots of c=1 in Theorem 3.6.1 (with ρ=1, α=0, as in Example (Roots of Unity)). The elements of Z/nZ are the classes [k] of Definition 2.3.2, and [k]=[l] if and only if n∣k−l. By Theorem 3.6.1 (b), n∣k−l if and only if zk=zl. If [k]=[l], then n∣k−l, so zk=zl; hence f([k]):=zk does not depend on the representative k, and f is well defined. If zk=zl, then n∣k−l, so [k]=[l]; hence f is injective. Each zk lies in μn by Theorem 3.6.1 (a), and every element of μn is some zk, again by (a); so f is surjective. Finally, [k]+[l]=[k+l] by the addition of Theorem 2.3.1 (b), and by Theorem 3.5.3 (a), f([k]+[l])=zk+l=e2πik/ne2πil/n=f([k])f([l]). As a check on the count, both sets have n elements (Theorem 2.3.3 (b) and Theorem 3.6.1 (c)). In the language of Chapter 5, f is an isomorphism from the additive structure of Z/nZ to the multiplicative structure of μn.

Exercise (R3)

Let F be an ordered field and x,y∈F.

  • (a) Prove that |x+y|≤|x|+|y| by comparing the squares of the two sides.
  • (b) The proof of the same inequality by “trapping” x between −|x| and |x|, and adding the traps for x and y, works in every ordered field. Which step of it cannot be carried out for complex x,y, and what replaces it in the complex triangle inequality?

Solution.

  • (a) Both sides are non-negative. By F3 Proposition 4.1.3 (b), |x|2=x2, |y|2=y2, |x+y|2=(x+y)2 and |x||y|=|xy|. Hence (|x|+|y|)2−|x+y|2=|x|2+2|x||y|+|y|2−x2−2xy−y2=2(|xy|−xy)≥0, since xy≤|xy| (F3 Proposition 4.1.3 (c)). So |x+y|2≤(|x|+|y|)2. If |x+y|>|x|+|y|, then F3 Proposition 2.4.3 (g), applied to these non-negative elements, would give |x+y|2>(|x|+|y|)2; so |x+y|≤|x|+|y|.

  • (b) The trapping proof (F3 Proposition 4.1.3 (e), proved from (c)) uses −|x|≤x≤|x|: it compares x itself with real numbers. For a complex x that is not real such a comparison means nothing, since no order on C is compatible with its arithmetic (Proposition 3.4.3). The squaring proof of (a) is the one that survives: in the complex triangle inequality (Theorem 3.3.2) the cross term 2Re(zw―) is bounded by a comparison between real numbers, Reu≤|u| (Lemma 3.3.1), which replaces xy≤|xy| here.

Exercise (R4)

Find all z∈C with z―=z2.

Solution. The tool: take moduli first. If z―=z2, then |z|=|z―|=|z2|=|z|2 (Theorem 3.2.2 (d)), so |z|(|z|−1)=0 and |z|=0 or |z|=1.

If |z|=0, then z=0 (Theorem 3.2.2 (e)), and z=0 is a solution.

If |z|=1, multiply z―=z2 by z: 1=zz―=z3 (Theorem 3.2.2 (c)). So z is a cube root of unity: z=1 or z=−12±32i (the Check above). Conversely, each cube root of unity ζ lies in T (Exercise B3), so ζ―=ζ−1 (Proposition 3.5.1 (a)), and ζ−1=ζ2 because ζ⋅ζ2=ζ3=1. So each is a solution.

The solutions are 0, 1, −12+32i and −12−32i.

Why the look-alike tools are worse. Comparing real and imaginary parts, with z=x+yi, gives x2−y2=x and 2xy=−y, which can be solved, but only with a case split by hand (y=0 or x=−12). “Divide z―=z2 by z” silently loses the solution z=0. Polar form works, but it is not needed: the moduli settle |z|, and the cube roots of unity can be found without angles, as in the Check.
