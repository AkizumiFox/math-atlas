1.5  Left, Right and Two-Sided Inverses

Section 1.4 sorted functions by two questions. Does f ever send two different inputs to the same output? Does f reach every point of its codomain? The answers were the words injective and surjective (Definition 1.4.3, Definition 1.4.4). This section asks a question that sounds different: can the work of f be undone? It turns out that it asks the same two questions again, one for each side of f. Undoing f after it has acted forces f to be injective, and when X is non-empty injectivity is also enough. Undoing it before, so that f finishes the job, forces f to be surjective. The one new idea in the section is an inverse on one side; an inverse on both sides, written f−1, is simply a map that is both.

Throughout, f:X→Y is a function in the sense of Definition 1.4.1, and composites are formed as in Definition 1.4.2: g∘f is defined when the codomain of f is the domain of g, and (g∘f)(x)=g(f(x)).

1.5.1. Undoing a function

Start with a function we can compute in our heads. Let d:N→N be the doubling map, d(n)=2n. Suppose someone applies d to a secret number and tells us the result, say 14. We recover the secret by halving: it was 7. Halving undoes doubling.

But halving is not quite a function from N to N: 7 has no half in N. If we want a function h:N→N that undoes d, we must give h a value at every odd number as well. Those values do not matter for the undoing, because d never produces an odd number. Any choice will do. So one answer is h(m):={m/2if m is even,1if m is odd, and another is h′(m):=m/2 for even m and h′(m):=m for odd m. Both are functions N→N: every m∈N is even or odd and not both, and an even m is 2k for exactly one k∈N, which is m/2. Both undo d: for every n∈N, the number 2n is even, so h(d(n))=h(2n)=2n2=n, and the same computation holds for h′. In the language of composition, h∘d=idN and h′∘d=idN.

Now try to undo in the other order. Is there a function k:N→N with d∘k=idN, so that d undoes k? Such a k would give d(k(1))=1, that is 2k(1)=1, and 1 is odd, not twice a natural number. So there is no such k. The obstacle is visible: 1 is not in the range of d, so no input to d, however chosen, comes out as 1.

Two things happened here, and they are the whole section in miniature. Undoing d afterwards was possible, in more than one way, and the reason is that d loses no information: different inputs give different outputs. Undoing it beforehand was impossible, and the reason is that d misses a point of its codomain. The two sides behave differently, so they get two names.

1.5.2. Left and right inverses

A left inverse undoes f after it acts; a right inverse is undone by f.

Definition 1.5.1 (Left and Right Inverses)

Let f:X→Y be a function, and let g:Y→X be a function in the opposite direction.

  • (a) g is a left inverse of f if g∘f=idX, that is, g(f(x))=x for every x∈X.
  • (b) g is a right inverse of f if f∘g=idY, that is, f(g(y))=y for every y∈Y.

In words: a left inverse is written on the left of f in the composite, and the composite g∘f does nothing to points of X. A right inverse is written on the right, and f∘g does nothing to points of Y. The two phrasings in each clause say the same thing. Two functions X→X are equal exactly when they agree at every point (Proposition 1.4.1), and g∘f and idX have the same domain and codomain X; so g∘f=idX means (g∘f)(x)=idX(x) for every x∈X, which is g(f(x))=x.

The phrase “g is a left inverse of f” and the phrase “f is a right inverse of g” say the same thing: both say g∘f=idX. Which word applies depends on which function we are talking about.

Check the definition against the doubling map. With d(n)=2n on N, the functions h and h′ above are left inverses of d: the composite h∘d sends every n back to n. And d has no right inverse, because clause (b) fails at the single point y=1. For a right inverse the “for every y∈Y” is the demanding part: one bad y is enough to rule it out.

Example (Left inverses on small sets)

Let X={1,2}, Y={1,2,3}, and e:X→Y, e(1)=1, e(2)=2: the inclusion of X into Y.

  • (a) Show that g:Y→X with g(1)=1, g(2)=2, g(3)=2 is a left inverse of e, and that e is a right inverse of g.
  • (b) Is g a right inverse of e?
  • (c) Let c:X→Y be constant, c(1)=c(2)=3. Does c have a left inverse?

Solution.

  • (a) Clause (a) of Definition 1.5.1 asks for g(e(x))=x at each of the two points of X: g(e(1))=g(1)=1 and g(e(2))=g(2)=2. So g∘e=idX, and g is a left inverse of e. Read with the roles exchanged, the same equation g∘e=idX is clause (b) for g:Y→X and e:X→Y: e is a right inverse of g.

  • (b) No. Clause (b) would need e(g(y))=y for every y∈Y, and at y=3 we get e(g(3))=e(2)=2≠3. The equation e∘g=idY fails at one point, which is enough.

  • (c) No. A left inverse g would need g(c(1))=1 and g(c(2))=2, that is g(3)=1 and g(3)=2. A function has one value at 3, and 1≠2. The information “which input was it” is gone once both inputs land on 3, and nothing applied afterwards can bring it back.

Part (c) is the degenerate case of the example, and it is the one to remember. A left inverse must recover x from f(x), so it cannot exist when two inputs share an output. Part (b) shows the other side: g undoes e, but e does not undo g, because e never produces 3.

Warning. A left inverse need not be a right inverse. For the doubling map d(n)=2n on N and its left inverse h above, h(d(n))=n for every n, but d(h(1))=d(1)=2≠1. The equation h∘d=idN says nothing about d∘h: composition is not commutative (Section 1.4), and here the two composites are different functions.

Quick check. Let s:N→N be the successor map, s(n)=n+1. Does s have a left inverse? Does it have a right inverse?

Solution. It has a left inverse, for instance p(m):=m−1 for m≥1 and p(0):=0. This is a function N→N, since m−1∈N when m≥1, and p(s(n))=p(n+1)=n because n+1≥1. It has no right inverse: a right inverse k:N→N of s would need s(k(0))=0, that is k(0)+1=0, which no k(0)∈N satisfies. The number 0 is not reached by s.

1.5.3. A left inverse exists exactly for injective functions

The examples point to a pattern. The doubling map, the inclusion e, and the successor map are injective, and each has a left inverse. The constant map c is not injective, and it has none. Here is the pattern as a theorem. Its hypothesis X≠∅ looks like a technicality; after the proof we will see that it is not.

We need the range of f from Section 1.4, and from now on we write it as f(X): f(X)={y∈Y:f(x)=y for some x∈X}. Section 1.6 extends this notation to the image of any subset of X.

Theorem 1.5.1 (Left Inverses and Injectivity)

Let f:X→Y be a function.

  • (a) If f has a left inverse, then f is injective.
  • (b) If X≠∅ and f is injective, then f has a left inverse.

Consequently, if X≠∅, then f has a left inverse if and only if f is injective.

Part (a) carries no hypothesis on X, on purpose: the last theorem of this section uses it for every X, the empty set included. The hypothesis X≠∅ sits in (b) only, where it is used. As in Proposition 1.4.2 (b), “X≠∅” is read as “X is non-empty: it has an element”, and the proof uses it in that form. (From an element, X≠∅ follows at once, since the empty set has none (Definition 1.1.4). The passage back, from “X is not the empty set” to “X has an element”, is the classical law “not all fail, so one holds” of Logic and Proof, Chapter 4; reading the hypothesis as “has an element” avoids it.)

Idea. For (a), start from the goal. Injectivity asks: from f(x1)=f(x2), get x1=x2. A left inverse g turns each f(x) back into x, so apply g to both sides.

For (b), build g point by point, the way halving was extended to the odd numbers. ① A point y of the range has a preimage, and injectivity says it has only one; let g(y) be that one. ② A point outside the range has no preimage; send it to a fixed point x0∈X. This is where X≠∅ is needed. ③ Check that ① and ② together define a function, and that g(f(x))=x.

Before reading on: where is X≠∅ used, and what would go wrong at that line if X were empty?

Proof.

  • (a) Let g:Y→X be a left inverse of f. Let x1,x2∈X with f(x1)=f(x2). Since g is a function, equal inputs give equal outputs, so g(f(x1))=g(f(x2)). By Definition 1.5.1 (a), g(f(x))=x for every x∈X, so x1=g(f(x1))=g(f(x2))=x2. Since x1 and x2 were arbitrary, f is injective by Definition 1.4.3.

  • (b) Suppose X≠∅ and f is injective. Since X has an element, fix one and call it x0 [∃E]. Let G be the subset of Y×X given by specification (Definition 1.1.3): G:={(y,x)∈Y×X:(y∈f(X) and f(x)=y) or (y∉f(X) and x=x0)}. We show that for every y∈Y there is exactly one x∈X with (y,x)∈G, by checking the two clauses (Fn1) (existence) and (Fn2) (uniqueness) into which Section 1.4 splits the condition of Definition 1.4.1 (Logic and Proof, Proposition 4.7.2). Then G, with codomain X, is a function g:Y→X. Let y∈Y.

Existence (Fn1). By excluded middle (Logic and Proof, Proposition 2.5.3), y∈f(X) or y∉f(X). Case 1: y∈f(X). Then f(x1)=y for some x1∈X; name it x1 [∃E]. The pair (y,x1) satisfies the first alternative, so (y,x1)∈G. Case 2: y∉f(X). Then (y,x0) satisfies the second alternative, so (y,x0)∈G. In either case some x∈X has (y,x)∈G.

Uniqueness (Fn2). Let x,x′∈X with (y,x)∈G and (y,x′)∈G. Each pair satisfies one of the two alternatives. If both satisfy the first, then f(x)=y=f(x′), so x=x′ because f is injective. If both satisfy the second, then x=x0=x′. If one satisfies the first and the other the second, then y∈f(X) and y∉f(X), a contradiction, so this case cannot occur. Hence x=x′.

So g:Y→X is a function, and g(y)=x means (y,x)∈G. It remains to show g∘f=idX. Let x∈X. Then f(x)∈f(X), with x as the witness [∃I], and f(x)=f(x), so the pair (f(x),x) satisfies the first alternative: (f(x),x)∈G, that is, g(f(x))=x. The functions g∘f and idX have domain and codomain X and agree at every point, so they are equal by Proposition 1.4.1. Hence g is a left inverse of f.

The last sentence of the theorem combines (a) and (b). This proves the theorem.

Look at where each hypothesis did its work. Injectivity was used once, in the uniqueness step: it is what makes “the preimage of y” a single point. The hypothesis X≠∅ was used once, in the first line of (b), to have a point x0 for the second alternative. The case split on y∈f(X) used excluded middle, a classical step: the proof does not tell us, for a given y, which case we are in.

Neither hypothesis of (b) can be dropped.

  • Without injectivity. The constant map c of Example (Left inverses on small sets) (c) sends 1 and 2 to 3, and it has no left inverse. In the proof, the uniqueness step breaks: (3,1) and (3,2) both lie in G, so G is not a function.
  • Without X≠∅. The empty function ∅→{1} is injective: the condition “for all x1,x2∈∅ …” holds vacuously. A left inverse would be a function {1}→∅, and there is none, since the point 1 would need a value in the empty set (Proposition 1.4.2). In the proof, the first line fails: there is no x0 to fix, and the point 1, which lies outside the range, has nowhere to go.

The proof of (b) describes g explicitly in terms of f and x0, so on a small example the construction can be run by hand.

Example (Running the construction)

Let f:{1,2}→{a,b,c} be given by f(1)=a and f(2)=c, where a, b, c are three different objects. Run the proof of Theorem 1.5.1 (b) with x0=1, then with x0=2. How many left inverses does f have?

Solution. First the hypotheses: {1,2}≠∅, and f is injective because its two inputs have the different outputs a and c. The range is f({1,2})={a,c}.

With x0=1: the points a and c of the range go back to their unique preimages, g(a)=1 and g(c)=2. The point b lies outside the range, so g(b)=x0=1. Check: g(f(1))=g(a)=1 and g(f(2))=g(c)=2, so g∘f=id{1,2}.

With x0=2: g′(a)=1, g′(c)=2 as before, and g′(b)=2. The same check shows g′∘f=id{1,2}.

These are all the left inverses. Any left inverse k must satisfy k(a)=k(f(1))=1 and k(c)=k(f(2))=2, so its values on the range are forced. Its value at b is free, and there are two points of {1,2} to choose from. So f has exactly 2 left inverses, and the construction with x0=1 and with x0=2 produces both.

The example shows that a left inverse is not unique in general. The values on the range are forced by the equation g(f(x))=x; the values off the range are free, and they may go to any point of X. (They are free but not always varied: if X has only one point, there is only one place to send them.)

1.5.4. A right inverse forces surjectivity

The doubling map has no right inverse because it misses 1. The same obstacle works for every function.

Proposition 1.5.2 (Right Inverses Give Surjectivity)

Let f:X→Y be a function. If f has a right inverse, then f is surjective.

Proof. Let g:Y→X be a right inverse of f, and let y∈Y. By Definition 1.5.1 (b), f(g(y))=y. So x:=g(y) is an element of X with f(x)=y [∃I]. Since y was arbitrary, f is surjective by Definition 1.4.4.

The proof names its witness: the right inverse is a rule that hands us, for each y, a preimage g(y). No hypothesis on X or Y is needed. If Y=∅, then f is surjective vacuously; and X=∅ as well, since there is no function from a non-empty set to ∅ (Proposition 1.4.2).

Right inverses are not unique either. Here is a small surjection with several.

Example (All right inverses of a small surjection)

Let q:{1,2,3,4}→{0,1} send each number to 0 if it is even and to 1 if it is odd: q(1)=1, q(2)=0, q(3)=1, q(4)=0. Find all right inverses of q.

Solution. A right inverse is a function k:{0,1}→{1,2,3,4} with q(k(0))=0 and q(k(1))=1. The first condition says that k(0) is a preimage of 0, so k(0)∈{2,4}. The second says k(1)∈{1,3}. The two choices are independent, and each combination gives a function {0,1}→{1,2,3,4} satisfying both conditions. So q has exactly 4 right inverses: (k(0),k(1))=(2,1),(2,3),(4,1),(4,3). Each one picks one preimage of each point of the codomain. By Proposition 1.5.2, the fact that one exists shows again that q is surjective.

1.5.5. Does every surjection have a right inverse?

Theorem 1.5.1 went both ways: for X≠∅, a left inverse exists exactly when f is injective. Does Proposition 1.5.2 go both ways too? Suppose f:X→Y is surjective. Must f have a right inverse?

Read what a right inverse must do. For every y∈Y, g(y) must be a preimage of y: an x with f(x)=y. Surjectivity promises that each y has at least one preimage. It does not say which, and there may be many. So the right inverse must choose one preimage for every y, all at once, and assemble the choices into one set of pairs.

Now look at what our rules can do.

  • One y. For a single y, the statement “there is x∈X with f(x)=y” lets us name a preimage by ∃E (Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, Logic and Proof, Section 4.6). That is one choice, made inside one proof.
  • Finitely many y. For a particular finite Y={y1,…,yn}, with n a fixed number, we can apply ∃E n times, once for each point, and write down the n pairs. (To get this for every finite Y at once, with n arbitrary, one inducts on n, Logic and Proof, Axiom 3.2.1.) Example (All right inverses of a small surjection) picked one preimage per point in the same spirit, by listing the preimage sets and choosing from them.
  • Infinitely many y. A proof is a finite object. It cannot contain one ∃E for each of infinitely many points, and no rule of Logic and Proof, Chapter 4 makes infinitely many choices in one step.

Sometimes the difficulty does not arise, because a rule picks the preimage. The squaring map from R onto [0,∞) is surjective, and each y≥0 has the preimages y and −y. The rule “take the non-negative one” defines g(y):=y, and g is a right inverse: (y)2=y. Likewise the projection N×N→N, (m,n)↦m, is surjective, and m↦(m,1) is a right inverse. In each case the specification of Definition 1.1.3 writes g down, because the rule names one preimage for each y.

For a surjection with no such rule, nothing proved so far produces a right inverse. The missing ingredient is a new principle of set formation, which says that a choice of one element from each set of a family can be made. Chapter 3 states it and proves, with it, that every surjection has a right inverse. Until then, we use only the implication proved above: a right inverse gives surjectivity.

1.5.6. Two-sided inverses

The two halves meet in a single fact about composition: when a function has an inverse on each side, the two inverses coincide.

Proposition 1.5.3 (Left and Right Inverses Agree)

Let f:X→Y be a function. If g:Y→X is a left inverse of f and h:Y→X is a right inverse of f, then g=h.

Proof. By Proposition 1.4.3 (associativity and the identity laws), g=g∘idY=g∘(f∘h)=(g∘f)∘h=idX∘h=h, where the first and last equalities are the identity laws, the second uses that h is a right inverse, the third is associativity, and the fourth uses that g is a left inverse. This proves the proposition.

The chain is worth reading twice. The composite g∘f∘h can be simplified from either end: the left pair g∘f collapses to idX, the right pair f∘h collapses to idY. Associativity says both simplifications are of the same function.

Definition 1.5.2 (Inverse Function)

Let f:X→Y be a function. A function g:Y→X is a two-sided inverse, or an inverse, of f if it is both a left inverse and a right inverse of f: g∘f=idXandf∘g=idY. A function with an inverse is invertible. Its inverse is unique: if g and g′ are both inverses of f, then g is a left inverse and g′ a right inverse, so g=g′ by Proposition 1.5.3. We call it the inverse of f and write it f−1:Y→X.

In words: f−1 undoes f on both sides. It sends f(x) back to x, and f sends f−1(y) back to y. The article “the” was earned by Proposition 1.5.3; without it, f−1 could name several functions, as “a left inverse” does.

The definition is symmetric in f and f−1. The two equations that say f−1 is an inverse of f also say that f is an inverse of f−1. So f−1 is invertible, and by uniqueness its inverse is f: (f−1)−1=f. This is the pattern for every identity about inverses: to show that some function is the inverse of f, check the two equations of Definition 1.5.2, and uniqueness does the rest.

Example (Inverses found and checked)

Show that each function is invertible by finding its inverse and checking both composites.

  • (a) a:R→R, a(x)=3x−2.
  • (b) r:(0,∞)→(0,∞), r(x)=1/x.
  • (c) s:R→R, s(x)=x|x|, that is, s(x)=x2 for x≥0 and s(x)=−x2 for x<0.

Solution. Idea. Solve y=f(x) for x to find a candidate, then check both equations of Definition 1.5.2. The solving is scratch work; the two checks are the proof.

  • (a) Solving y=3x−2 gives x=(y+2)/3. Let b(y):=(y+2)/3, a function R→R. For every x∈R, b(a(x))=(3x−2+2)/3=x, and for every y∈R, a(b(y))=3⋅(y+2)/3−2=y. So b=a−1.

  • (b) For x>0, 1/x>0, so r does map (0,∞) to (0,∞). Here r(r(x))=1/(1/x)=x for every x>0. This one equation is both checks at once, with r in the roles of f and g. So r−1=r: the function is its own inverse.

  • (c) Solve by cases on the sign of y, since s(x) has the sign of x. For y≥0 the candidate is y; for y<0 it is −−y. Let t(y):={yif y≥0,−−yif y<0. First check, t(s(x))=x, by cases on x. If x≥0, then s(x)=x2≥0, so t(s(x))=x2=x, the non-negative square root of x2 being x. If x<0, then s(x)=−x2<0, so t(s(x))=−x2=−(−x)=x, since the non-negative square root of x2 is −x.

Second check, s(t(y))=y, by cases on y. If y≥0, then t(y)=y≥0, so s(t(y))=(y)2=y. If y<0, then t(y)=−−y<0, so s(t(y))=−(−y)2=−(−y)=y.

Both checks hold, so t=s−1. Notice that each check needs its own case split: the first splits on the sign of the input x, the second on the sign of y.

Now the theorem that connects inverses with the words of Section 1.4.

Theorem 1.5.4 (Bijections Are the Invertible Functions)

A function f:X→Y is bijective if and only if it has a two-sided inverse.

Idea. (⇐) is already proved in two halves: an inverse is a left inverse, which gives injectivity by Theorem 1.5.1 (a), and a right inverse, which gives surjectivity by Proposition 1.5.2. Neither half asks anything of X.

(⇒) looks like the open question of the last subsection, since we must pick a preimage of every y. But for a bijection there is nothing to pick. Surjectivity gives each y a preimage and injectivity says there is only one. So the set of pairs (y,x) with f(x)=y already has the ∀∃! property of Definition 1.4.1.

Proof. (⇐) Let g be a two-sided inverse of f. Then g is a left inverse of f, so f is injective by Theorem 1.5.1 (a); and g is a right inverse, so f is surjective by Proposition 1.5.2. Hence f is bijective by Definition 1.4.5.

(⇒) Let f be bijective, and let G:={(y,x)∈Y×X:f(x)=y}, a subset of Y×X by specification (Definition 1.1.3). Let y∈Y. Since f is surjective, there is x∈X with f(x)=y, so some x has (y,x)∈G. If (y,x)∈G and (y,x′)∈G, then f(x)=y=f(x′), so x=x′ since f is injective. So G satisfies (Fn1) and (Fn2) (Logic and Proof, Proposition 4.7.2): for every y∈Y there is exactly one x∈X with (y,x)∈G, and G with codomain X is a function g:Y→X (Definition 1.4.1). By construction, g(y)=x holds exactly when f(x)=y.

Now let y∈Y. Then g(y)=x for the x with (y,x)∈G, so f(g(y))=y. And let x∈X. The pair (f(x),x) lies in G, since f(x)=f(x); so g(f(x))=x. By Proposition 1.4.1, f∘g=idY and g∘f=idX. So g is a two-sided inverse of f. This proves the theorem.

Compare the two constructions of this section. The left inverse of Theorem 1.5.1 (b) needed a fixed point x0 and a case split by excluded middle, because some y have no preimage. The inverse of a bijection needs neither. Every y has exactly one preimage, so the definition “g(y) is the preimage of y” names one element for each y without any choosing. This is why no new principle is needed here, while the open question about surjections needs one. The difference between the two is exactly the difference between “exactly one” and “at least one”.

The theorem also covers the degenerate case. A function f:∅→Y is surjective only if Y=∅, since a point of Y would need a preimage in ∅. The empty function ∅→∅ is bijective, and it is its own inverse: both composites are the empty function ∅→∅, which is id∅. The proof of (⇐) runs at X=∅ without change, because it cites only part (a) of Theorem 1.5.1, which has no hypothesis on X.

Remark. The notation f−1 is reserved, as a function, for a bijection f. For a function that is not bijective, “f−1:Y→X” names nothing. Section 1.6 uses the symbol in a second way, f−1(B) for a set B, which makes sense for every function; it shows that for a bijection the two readings give the same set.

Warning. Do not write f−1 for a left inverse. The doubling map d has the two different left inverses h and h′ of the first subsection, so “d−1” would not say which one is meant. And neither is an inverse: d(h(1))=2≠1. The notation is earned by Proposition 1.5.3, which needs an inverse on both sides.

Quick check. Does x↦x3 from R to R have a two-sided inverse? Does x↦x2 from [0,∞) to R?

Solution. The first does: y↦y3, the real cube root, which exists and is unique for every real y (a school fact). Indeed x33=x and (y3)3=y. The second does not. It is injective (Example (Squaring, three ways) (b)), but it is not surjective, since −1 has no preimage; by Theorem 1.5.4 it has no two-sided inverse. (It has left inverses, by Theorem 1.5.1, for instance y↦y for y≥0 and y↦0 for y<0.)

1.5.7. What the section proved

For a function f:X→Y, the section proved four facts and left one question open.

  • A left inverse makes f injective (Theorem 1.5.1 (a)).
  • If X≠∅, an injective f has a left inverse (Theorem 1.5.1 (b)). The empty function ∅→{1} shows that the hypothesis is needed.
  • A right inverse makes f surjective (Proposition 1.5.2).
  • A two-sided inverse exists exactly when f is bijective (Theorem 1.5.4), and it is unique (Proposition 1.5.3).
  • Open: a surjective f has a right inverse. Chapter 3 proves it, with the new principle.

1.5.8. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

These parts are about Theorem 1.5.1 and its proof.

  • (a) At which line of the proof of (b) is X≠∅ used, and for what?
  • (b) The proof shows that the set G is a function by proving two things about the case split into y∈f(X) and y∉f(X). What are they, and which step of the proof uses each?
  • (c) Run the construction on f:{1,2,3}→{1,2,3,4}, f(k)=k+1, with x0=3. Write out the left inverse g and check g∘f=id{1,2,3}.
  • (d) Give the idea of the proof of (b) in two sentences.
  • (e) Let f:{1,2}→{1} be the only function between these sets. Which step of the proof of (b) fails for f, and why?

Solution.

  • (a) In the first line of (b): “fix one and call it x0”. The point x0 is the value g(y) for every y outside the range f(X); without it, the second alternative in the definition of G would have nothing to send such y to.

  • (b) The two cases exhaust every y: by excluded middle, y∈f(X) or y∉f(X); this is used in the existence step. And they do not overlap: a y cannot satisfy both, since y∈f(X) and y∉f(X) contradict each other; this is used in the uniqueness step, to rule out a pair from each alternative. (Uniqueness inside the first case uses injectivity; inside the second, it is automatic.)

  • (c) The hypotheses hold: {1,2,3}≠∅, and f is injective, since k+1=k′+1 gives k=k′. The range is f({1,2,3})={2,3,4}. On the range, g returns the unique preimage: g(2)=1, g(3)=2, g(4)=3. The point 1 is outside the range, so g(1)=x0=3. Check: g(f(1))=g(2)=1, g(f(2))=g(3)=2, g(f(3))=g(4)=3. So g∘f=id{1,2,3}.

  • (d) A point of the range has exactly one preimage, by injectivity, and g sends it there; a point outside the range is sent to a fixed point x0 of the non-empty domain. Then g(f(x)) is the unique preimage of f(x), which is x.

  • (e) The uniqueness step fails. The point y=1 lies in the range and has the two preimages 1 and 2, so (1,1) and (1,2) both lie in G, and G is not a function. The step “f(x)=y=f(x′), so x=x′” needs injectivity, which this f lacks. (By part (a) of the theorem, f has no left inverse at all.)

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Counting one-sided inverses)
  • (a) Find all left inverses of f:{1,2}→{1,2,3}, f(k)=k.
  • (b) Find all right inverses of g:{1,2,3}→{1,2}, g(1)=g(2)=1, g(3)=2.

Solution.

  • (a) A left inverse h:{1,2,3}→{1,2} must satisfy h(f(1))=1 and h(f(2))=2, that is h(1)=1 and h(2)=2. The point 3 is outside the range {1,2}, so h(3) is free: 1 or 2. Each choice satisfies both equations. So there are exactly 2 left inverses: h(3)=1 and h(3)=2, with h(1)=1, h(2)=2 in both.

  • (b) A right inverse k:{1,2}→{1,2,3} must satisfy g(k(1))=1 and g(k(2))=2. The preimages of 1 under g are 1 and 2, so k(1)∈{1,2}; the only preimage of 2 is 3, so k(2)=3. So there are exactly 2 right inverses: k(1)=1 or k(1)=2, with k(2)=3 in both.

Exercise (B2: The inverse of a composite)

Let f:X→Y and g:Y→Z be bijections. Prove that g∘f is invertible and that (g∘f)−1=f−1∘g−1.

Hint: check the two equations of Definition 1.5.2 for the candidate f−1∘g−1.

Solution. The candidate f−1∘g−1:Z→X is defined, since g−1:Z→Y and f−1:Y→X exist by Theorem 1.5.4. We check the two equations of Definition 1.5.2, using Proposition 1.4.3 to regroup and for its identity laws k∘id=k=id∘k: (f−1∘g−1)∘(g∘f)=f−1∘(g−1∘g)∘f=f−1∘idY∘f=f−1∘f=idX,(g∘f)∘(f−1∘g−1)=g∘(f∘f−1)∘g−1=g∘idY∘g−1=g∘g−1=idZ. So f−1∘g−1 is a two-sided inverse of g∘f. By the uniqueness of inverses (Proposition 1.5.3, as in Definition 1.5.2), it is the inverse: (g∘f)−1=f−1∘g−1. Notice the order: to undo “first f, then g”, undo g first.

Exercise (B3: A bijection between an interval and the line)

Let h:(−1,1)→R, h(x)=x/(1−|x|), and k:R→(−1,1), k(y)=y/(1+|y|). Show that h and k are well defined (each denominator is non-zero, and k takes values in (−1,1)), and that k is the inverse of h, by checking both composites by cases on the sign. Hence h is a bijection.

Solution. Well defined. For x∈(−1,1), |x|<1, so 1−|x|>0. For y∈R, 1+|y|≥1>0, and |k(y)|=|y|/(1+|y|)<1 because |y|<1+|y|; so k(y)∈(−1,1).

First check, k(h(x))=x for x∈(−1,1). If 0≤x<1, then h(x)=x/(1−x)≥0, so k(h(x))=x/(1−x)1+x/(1−x)=x/(1−x)1/(1−x)=x. If −1<x<0, then |x|=−x and h(x)=x/(1+x)<0, so |h(x)|=−x/(1+x) and k(h(x))=x/(1+x)1−x/(1+x)=x/(1+x)1/(1+x)=x.

Second check, h(k(y))=y for y∈R. If y≥0, then k(y)=y/(1+y)∈[0,1), so h(k(y))=y/(1+y)1−y/(1+y)=y/(1+y)1/(1+y)=y. If y<0, then k(y)=y/(1−y)∈(−1,0), so |k(y)|=−y/(1−y) and h(k(y))=y/(1−y)1+y/(1−y)=y/(1−y)1/(1−y)=y. Both composites are identities, so k=h−1, and h is a bijection by Theorem 1.5.4.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: One composite equal to the identity)

Let f:X→Y and g:Y→X be functions with g∘f=idX.

  • (a) Prove that f is injective and g is surjective.
  • (b) Show by an example that neither f nor g need be bijective.
  • (c) Prove that if, in addition, f is surjective, then g=f−1.

Solution (Hint 1). Read the equation g∘f=idX twice: once as a statement about f, once as a statement about g.

Solution (Hint 2). For g: let x∈X; you need a point of Y that g sends to x.

Solution.

  • (a) The equation says that g is a left inverse of f, so f is injective by Theorem 1.5.1 (a). The same equation says that f is a right inverse of g, so g is surjective by Proposition 1.5.2. (Directly: for x∈X, the point f(x)∈Y satisfies g(f(x))=x.)

  • (b) Let f:{1}→{1,2}, f(1)=1, and g:{1,2}→{1}, g(1)=g(2)=1. Then g(f(1))=1, so g∘f=id{1}. But f is not surjective (2 has no preimage) and g is not injective (g(1)=g(2) with 1≠2), so neither is bijective.

  • (c) If f is also surjective, it is bijective by (a), so f−1 exists by Theorem 1.5.4, and it is in particular a right inverse of f. Also g is a left inverse of f. By Proposition 1.5.3, g=f−1.

Marking scheme. (10 points)

  • 2: f injective, from the left-inverse equation (citing Theorem 1.5.1 (a) or arguing directly from f(x1)=f(x2)).
  • 2: g surjective, either by citing Proposition 1.5.2 (f is a right inverse of g) or directly, naming the witness f(x) for a given x∈X.
  • 3: an example with explicit sets and both failures checked (a point missed by f, two points identified by g).
  • 3: (c) obtains a right inverse of f (from bijectivity) and applies uniqueness of one-sided inverses, or checks f∘g=idY directly (for y=f(x), f(g(y))=f(g(f(x)))=f(x)=y, using surjectivity to write y=f(x)).

Fatal errors (0 for the step): assuming f∘g=idY in (a) or (c) without proof; in (c), writing f−1 before showing f bijective.

Exercise (C2: When is a left inverse unique?)

Let f:X→Y be injective, with X≠∅.

  • (a) Prove that if f is surjective, or X has exactly one element, then f has exactly one left inverse.
  • (b) Prove that if some y1∈Y is not in f(X), and X has two different elements x0≠x1, then f has at least two different left inverses.
  • (c) Let f:{1,2}→{1,2,3,4}, f(k)=2k. List all left inverses of f, and explain in one sentence why there are 22=4 of them.

Solution (Hint 1). For (a), a left inverse exists by Theorem 1.5.1 (b); only “at most one” needs proof. For (b), run the construction of that proof twice.

Solution (Hint 2). For (a), let g and g′ be left inverses of f. If f is surjective, write a given y∈Y as y=f(x) and compare g(y) with g′(y).

Solution.

  • (a) A left inverse exists by Theorem 1.5.1 (b). For “at most one”, let g and g′ be left inverses of f. Case 1: f is surjective. Then f is bijective (Definition 1.4.5), so it has the inverse f−1 (Theorem 1.5.4), which is in particular a right inverse of f. By Proposition 1.5.3, g=f−1 and g′=f−1, so g=g′. Case 2: X has exactly one element x0. For every y∈Y, both g(y) and g′(y) are elements of X, so g(y)=x0=g′(y); hence g=g′ by Proposition 1.4.1. In either case any two left inverses are equal, so f has exactly one.

  • (b) Run the proof of Theorem 1.5.1 (b) once with the fixed point x0 and once with x1; call the resulting left inverses g0 and g1. The point y1 is outside the range, so the second alternative in the definition of G applies to it: g0(y1)=x0 and g1(y1)=x1. Since x0≠x1, the functions g0 and g1 differ at y1, so g0≠g1.

  • (c) The range is {2,4}. A left inverse g:{1,2,3,4}→{1,2} must satisfy g(f(1))=1 and g(f(2))=2, that is g(2)=1 and g(4)=2. The values g(1) and g(3) are free, each in {1,2}, and every choice gives a left inverse. The four are (g(1),g(2),g(3),g(4))=(1,1,1,2), (1,1,2,2), (2,1,1,2), (2,1,2,2). The values on the range are forced, and each of the two points 1, 3 outside the range may go to either of the two points of the domain, independently: 2⋅2=4 choices. This is the situation of (b): f is not surjective and its domain has two elements.

Marking scheme. (10 points)

  • 1: (a) existence of a left inverse, citing Theorem 1.5.1 (b).
  • 2: (a) surjective case: an inverse exists, and uniqueness of one-sided inverses (Proposition 1.5.3) forces every left inverse to equal it (or a direct argument: for y=f(x), g(y)=x=g′(y)).
  • 2: (a) one-element case: any two functions into a one-element set agree at every point, so they are equal.
  • 3: (b) two left inverses that take the values x0 and x1 at y1 (by the construction or defined directly), each checked to be a left inverse, and shown to differ at y1.
  • 2: (c) the forced values g(2)=1, g(4)=2, the four left inverses, and the count 2⋅2.

Fatal errors (0 for the step): in (a), writing f−1 before showing f bijective; in (b), concluding g0≠g1 without exhibiting a point where they differ.
