    14.11  Minkowski Space

    Sylvester’s law of inertia says that a real symmetric form is nothing but a pair of counts, and that any two forms with the same counts are the same form in disguise. One pair of counts has a claim on our attention that none of the others has: the form of signature (1,3) is the one the physical world appears to run on. This section does the linear algebra of that form. Everything in it is this chapter’s machinery specialized, and the payoff is a picture — a cone dividing space into three regions — together with a classification theorem that is the hyperbolic twin of a circular one we already proved.

    14.11.1. The form of special relativity

    Fix n+=1 and n−=3 in the canonical form of Corollary 14.5.2, and there is only one form to write down.

    Definition 14.11.1 (Minkowski Space)

    Minkowski space R1,3 is the real vector space R4 equipped with the symmetric bilinear form η(x,y):=x1y1−x2y2−x3y3−x4y4, whose quadratic form is q(x)=x12−x22−x32−x42. The first coordinate is the time coordinate and the remaining three are the space coordinates. More generally, for p,m≥0, Rp,m is Rp+m with the form whose matrix in the standard basis is Ip⊕(−Im).

    In words: η is the dot product of R4 with three of its four signs reversed. Its matrix in the standard basis E is [η]EE=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), which is symmetric, so η is a symmetric form (Definition 14.3.1), and it is diagonal, so E is an orthogonal basis for η (Definition 14.4.1). Reading off the signs, the signature of η is (1,3) and its inertia is (1,3,0) (Definition 14.5.1). The form is nondegenerate: η(e1,x)=x1 and η(ej,x)=−xj for j=2,3,4, so a vector paired to zero with everything has all four coordinates zero (Definition 14.1.4).

    Warning. Two sign conventions are in use, and both are called “the” Minkowski form. We take (+,−,−,−), signature (1,3); many sources take (−,+,+,+), signature (3,1), which is our −η. The two are not congruent — congruence preserves the signature (Theorem 14.5.1) — but every statement below transfers by reversing each inequality on q, because q changes sign. The one thing both conventions agree on is the set {q=0}, which is why the picture of this section is convention-free. State your convention before computing, and never mix the two.

    14.11.2. Timelike, spacelike, and null

    Nothing in this section is a new notion. Section 7 studied the vectors on which a form vanishes, and called them isotropic; over R a nondegenerate indefinite form has plenty of them, and the ones belonging to η have names of their own.

    The form sorts nonzero vectors into three classes by the sign of q, and the middle class — where q vanishes — is exactly the isotropy of Section 7.

    Definition 14.11.2 (Timelike, Spacelike, Null)

    Let x∈R1,3 with x≠0. Then x is

    • (a) timelike if q(x)>0;
    • (b) null, or lightlike, if q(x)=0;
    • (c) spacelike if q(x)<0.

    The set {x∈R1,3:q(x)=0} is the light cone. A timelike x is future-pointing if x1>0 and past-pointing if x1<0.

    The word null is a second name for something already defined: a null vector is precisely a nonzero isotropic vector for η in the sense of Definition 14.7.1, and the light cone is the isotropy locus of η together with 0. So “the light cone” is not new geometry; it is the locus Section 7 already forced us to take seriously, drawn for one particular form. Timelike and spacelike are then just names for the two sides that the isotropy locus separates.

    The last clause of the definition is legitimate because a timelike vector cannot have x1=0: if it did, then q(x)=−(x22+x32+x42)≤0. So every timelike vector is future-pointing or past-pointing, and exactly one of the two.

    Example (Reading off the three types)

    Classify e1, e2, e1+e2, and (2,1,1,1) in R1,3, and decide which of them are isotropic.

    Solution. q(e1)=1>0, so e1 is timelike and future-pointing. q(e2)=−1<0, so e2 is spacelike. q(e1+e2)=1−1=0, so e1+e2 is null, hence isotropic. Finally q(2,1,1,1)=4−3=1>0, timelike and future-pointing. Only e1+e2 is isotropic; the other three are anisotropic.

    Section 7’s structure theorem applies verbatim. A nonzero isotropic vector sits in a hyperbolic plane, and here we can point to one: take u=e1+e2 and w=12(e1−e2). Then q(u)=q(w)=0 and η(u,w)=12(1−(−1))=1, so the Gram matrix of (u,w) is (0110) and span(u,w) is a hyperbolic plane (Definition 14.7.3). Splitting it off, Theorem 14.7.5 writes R1,3=H⊥W, with H hyperbolic of signature (1,1) and W anisotropic of signature (0,2), that is, negative definite. Only one plane splits off, because the Witt index of a real form of signature (n+,n−) is min(n+,n−) (Proposition 14.7.7), which here is min(1,3)=1.

    Quick check. Is there a plane in R1,3 on which η vanishes identically?

    Solution. No. Suppose x=(a,u) and y=(b,w) span such a plane, where a,b∈R and u,w∈R3. Then q(x)=q(y)=0 gives a2=‖u‖2 and b2=‖w‖2, while η(x,y)=0 gives ab=u⋅w. Hence |u⋅w|=|a||b|=‖u‖‖w‖, which is the equality case of Theorem 11.1.5, so (u,w) is dependent. If u=0 then a=0 and x=0; otherwise w=λu, so b2=λ2a2 and ab=λa2, which force b=λa and y=λx. Either way (x,y) is dependent, so no plane exists. Structurally this is the statement that the Witt index of R1,3 is min(1,3)=1 (Proposition 14.7.7), since by Definition 14.7.4 no totally isotropic subspace has dimension larger than the index.

    The picture is easiest to draw with one space coordinate instead of three, in R1,1, where the light cone degenerates to the two lines x2=±x1.

    Two features of the picture deserve to be said out loud. The shaded region is not connected: the future-pointing and past-pointing timelike vectors form two separate pieces, and no continuous path of timelike vectors joins them. That last claim is worth one line, since it is the only place in this section where a topological word is used. Suppose γ were a continuous path of timelike vectors from a future-pointing vector to a past-pointing one. Its first coordinate x1 is a continuous real function that is positive at one end and negative at the other, so by the intermediate value theorem — an analysis input, quoted and not proved here, as in Section 6 — it vanishes somewhere along the path. But x1=0 forces q(x)=−x22−x32−x42≤0, so that point of the path is not timelike, a contradiction. And the level set q=1, which plays the role the unit circle plays for the dot product, is unbounded. Both facts come straight from the mixed signs, and both are invisible to anyone whose intuition was trained on positive definite forms.

    Warning. Orthogonality for η is not the perpendicularity you can see. A null vector is η-orthogonal to itself, and the consequence is that Chapter 11’s decomposition V=U⊕U⊥ fails. Take U=span((1,1,0,0)). Since η((1,1,0,0),x)=x1−x2, the η-orthogonal complement of U is the hyperplane {x1=x2}, which contains U. So U+U⊥=U⊥ is three-dimensional, not four, and U∩U⊥=U≠{0}. Nothing has gone wrong; positivity was doing that work in Chapter 11, and it is gone.

    14.11.3. Hyperbolic rotations

    Now the isometries. The group attached to η is the isometry group of its matrix (Definition 14.10.1), which is O(1,3) in the notation of Definition 14.10.2: the matrices P with P⊤[η]EEP=[η]EE. Its elements are the Lorentz transformations. We classify them in dimension 2, where η becomes η2(x,y)=x1y1−x2y2,[η2]EE=diag(1,−1).

    The classification of O(2) in Theorem 11.7.6 rested on one geometric fact: a unit vector of the Euclidean plane is (cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ) for exactly one θ∈[0,2π). Replace the circle by the hyperbola and the same proof runs. We need the corresponding parametrization, and we take it from calculus:

    cosh⁡t=12(et+e−t),sinh⁡t=12(et−e−t),cosh2⁡t−sinh2⁡t=1,cosh⁡t>0,cosh⁡(s+t)=cosh⁡scosh⁡t+sinh⁡ssinh⁡t,sinh⁡(s+t)=sinh⁡scosh⁡t+cosh⁡ssinh⁡t,

    and sinh:R→R is a strictly increasing bijection. Those facts are the only analysis used in this section.

    Definition 14.11.3 (Boost)

    For t∈R, the boost of rapidity t is Bt:=(cosh⁡tsinh⁡tsinh⁡tcosh⁡t)∈M2(R).

    A boost is the hyperbolic analogue of the rotation Rθ of Chapter 11: same shape, hyperbolic functions in place of circular ones, and no minus sign in the corner.

    Theorem 14.11.1 (Classification of the Plane Lorentz Transformations)

    Let P∈O(1,1), that is, P∈M2(R) with P⊤diag(1,−1)P=diag(1,−1). Then there are a unique t∈R and unique signs ε,μ∈{1,−1} with P=(εcosh⁡tμsinh⁡tsinh⁡tεμcosh⁡t), and detP=μ. Writing D=diag(1,−1), this says O(1,1)={Bt,BtD,−Bt,−BtD:t∈R}, a disjoint union of four families. Moreover BsBt=Bs+t for all s,t, so {Bt:t∈R} is a subgroup of O(1,1) isomorphic to (R,+).

    Idea. The congruence P⊤DP=D says exactly that the two columns of P are an orthogonal basis for η2 with q-values 1 and −1. So: parametrize the vectors with q=1, which is the hyperbola; then the second column is forced, because the η2-orthogonal complement of a nonisotropic vector is a line, and on that line only two vectors have q=−1. This is Theorem 11.7.6’s proof with the circle replaced by the hyperbola.

    Proof. Write P=(acbd) with columns p1=(a,b) and p2=(c,d). The (i,j) entry of P⊤DP is η2(pi,pj), so the hypothesis says q(p1)=1,q(p2)=−1,η2(p1,p2)=0.

    The first column. Since sinh is a bijection of R, there is a unique t∈R with b=sinh⁡t. Then a2=1+b2=1+sinh2⁡t=cosh2⁡t, so a=εcosh⁡t for a unique sign ε, unique because cosh⁡t>0.

    The second column. The vector z=(sinh⁡t,εcosh⁡t) satisfies η2(p1,z)=εcosh⁡tsinh⁡t−sinh⁡tεcosh⁡t=0, and z≠0 because εcosh⁡t≠0. The set {y:η2(p1,y)=0} is the null space of the nonzero row vector (a,−b), hence a line, so it equals span(z). Therefore p2=μ′z for some μ′∈R, and q(z)=sinh2⁡t−cosh2⁡t=−1 gives −1=q(p2)=(μ′)2q(z)=−(μ′)2. Hence μ′=μ is a sign, and it is determined by P, since d=μεcosh⁡t and cosh⁡t>0. This is the displayed matrix, and expanding, detP=μ(ε2cosh2⁡t−sinh2⁡t)=μ.

    The four families. For ε=μ=1 the matrix is Bt; for ε=1,μ=−1 it is BtD; for ε=−1,μ=1 it is −B−t; and for ε=μ=−1 it is −B−tD, each by multiplying out and using sinh⁡(−t)=−sinh⁡t, cosh⁡(−t)=cosh⁡t. As t runs over R so does −t, which gives the displayed set equality. The families are disjoint because ε is the sign of the (1,1) entry and μ=detP, and both are determined by P.

    The subgroup. By the addition formulas for cosh and sinh, BsBt=(cosh⁡(s+t)sinh⁡(s+t)sinh⁡(s+t)cosh⁡(s+t))=Bs+t. So t↦Bt is a group homomorphism (R,+)→O(1,1); it is injective because sinh is, and its image is a subgroup by Definition 0.10.2. This proves the theorem.

    The four families are sorted by two signs, and each sign has a reading. The determinant μ says whether the space coordinate is reversed, exactly as in O(2). The sign ε of the (1,1) entry says whether the future cone is sent to the future cone: for Bt the image of e1 is (cosh⁡t,sinh⁡t), with positive first coordinate, while −Bt sends it to (−cosh⁡t,−sinh⁡t). So the identity component consists of the boosts alone, and the other three families are obtained from it by reversing space, time, or both.

    14.11.4. Circular and hyperbolic, side by side

    The comparison is the point of this section, so here it is in one table. In each column, the isometry group of the named form on R2.

    O(2): form I2 O(1,1): form diag(1,−1)
    level set q=1 circle x12+x22=1 hyperbola x12−x22=1, two branches
    canonical element Rθ=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ) Bt=(cosh⁡tsinh⁡tsinh⁡tcosh⁡t)
    parameter θ∈[0,2π), periodic t∈R, unbounded
    addition law RαRβ=Rα+β BsBt=Bs+t
    determinant +1 part SO(2)={Rθ} {Bt}∪{−Bt}
    determinant −1 part reflections Mθ {BtD}∪{−BtD}
    number of pieces 2 4
    entries all of modulus ≤1 unbounded

    The two canonical forms differ in one place only: Rθ carries a minus sign that Bt does not, and that single sign is the difference between cos2+sin2=1 and cosh2−sinh2=1. Everything else in the table follows from it. In particular the last row is the unboundedness that Section 10 recorded when it observed, without proof, that O(p,q) is not compact for p,q≥1 while O(n) is.

    Remark. In the physical reading, tanh⁡t is a velocity measured in units of the speed of light, and the additivity BsBt=Bs+t of rapidities becomes the relativistic law for adding velocities. Exercise (C1) derives the formula from the theorem. The book verifies the algebra and stops there.

    14.11.5. The triangle inequality, reversed

    For a timelike x the number q(x) is positive, so it has a positive square root, and the analogy with a norm is irresistible. Write τ(x):=q(x)(x timelike). The analogy holds, but with every inequality upside down. First the tool.

    Lemma 14.11.2 (Reversed Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality)

    Let x,y∈R1,3 be timelike and both future-pointing. Then η(x,y)≥τ(x)τ(y)>0, with equality if and only if y=λx for some λ>0.

    Idea. Split each vector as a time coordinate plus a space part, x=(a,u). Timelike and future-pointing says a>‖u‖≥0. Then η(x,y)=ab−u⋅w, and Theorem 11.1.5 bounds u⋅w by ‖u‖‖w‖ — in the helpful direction, since it appears with a minus sign. What is left is to compare ab−‖u‖‖w‖ with τ(x)τ(y), and squaring both sides turns the difference into a perfect square.

    Proof. Write x=(a,u) and y=(b,w) with a,b∈R and u,w∈R3, so that q(x)=a2−‖u‖2 and η(x,y)=ab−u⋅w. Timelike gives a2>‖u‖2 and b2>‖w‖2, and future-pointing gives a,b>0; hence a>‖u‖≥0 and b>‖w‖≥0, so ab>‖u‖‖w‖≥0. By Theorem 11.1.5 in R3, η(x,y)=ab−u⋅w≥ab−‖u‖‖w‖>0. Both sides of the inequality to be proved are now known positive, so it suffices to compare squares. Expanding, (ab−‖u‖‖w‖)2−q(x)q(y)=a2‖w‖2+b2‖u‖2−2ab‖u‖‖w‖=(a‖w‖−b‖u‖)2≥0. Therefore ab−‖u‖‖w‖≥q(x)q(y)=τ(x)τ(y), and combining with the previous display gives η(x,y)≥τ(x)τ(y)>0.

    For the equality case, equality forces both steps to be equalities: u⋅w=‖u‖‖w‖ and a‖w‖=b‖u‖. Suppose first u=0. Then a‖w‖=0 and a>0, so w=0, and y=(b,0)=(b/a)x with b/a>0. Suppose instead u≠0. The equality case of Theorem 11.1.5 makes (u,w) dependent, so w=λu with λ∈R, and then u⋅w=λ‖u‖2=‖u‖‖w‖=|λ|‖u‖2 forces λ≥0. Now a‖w‖=b‖u‖ reads aλ‖u‖=b‖u‖, so b=λa and y=λx; and λ>0 because b>0. Conversely, if y=λx with λ>0 then η(x,y)=λq(x) and τ(x)τ(y)=λ2q(x)2=λq(x), so equality holds. This proves the lemma.

    Theorem 14.11.3 (Reversed Triangle Inequality)

    Let x,y∈R1,3 be timelike and both future-pointing. Then x+y is timelike and future-pointing, and τ(x+y)≥τ(x)+τ(y), with equality if and only if y is a positive multiple of x.

    Proof. By Theorem 14.3.5, q(x+y)=q(x)+2η(x,y)+q(y). By Lemma 14.11.2, η(x,y)≥τ(x)τ(y), so q(x+y)≥q(x)+2τ(x)τ(y)+q(y)=(τ(x)+τ(y))2, where the last step uses q=τ2 on timelike vectors. The right-hand side is positive, so x+y is timelike; its first coordinate is a sum of two positive numbers, so it is future-pointing. Both sides of the last display are nonnegative, so taking square roots preserves the inequality and gives τ(x+y)≥τ(x)+τ(y). Equality holds exactly when it holds in Lemma 14.11.2, that is, exactly when y=λx with λ>0. This proves the theorem.

    Remark. Compare Corollary 11.1.6, which gives ‖u+v‖≤‖u‖+‖v‖ in an inner product space, and Theorem 0.6.4, its ancestor in C. Same shape, opposite direction: the reversal is produced by the single sign change in the form, and by nothing else.

    Read as a statement about clocks, the reversed inequality says that a journey made in two straight legs accumulates less elapsed time than the single straight leg between the same endpoints, which is the algebra behind the twin paradox; whether that algebra describes the world is a question for physics, and this book answers only the algebra.

    Quick check. Where exactly would the proof of Theorem 14.11.3 break if x were future-pointing and y past-pointing?

    Solution. At Lemma 14.11.2, whose proof uses a,b>0 to get ab>‖u‖‖w‖≥0. With b<0 we have ab<0, and η(x,y)=ab−u⋅w can be negative, so the chain collapses at its first step. The conclusion genuinely fails: take x=(1,0,0,0) and y=(−1,0,0,0), both timelike with τ=1, whose sum is 0 and is not timelike at all.

    14.11.6. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)
    • (a) State the sign convention used in this section, give the matrix of η in the standard basis, and say what its signature and inertia are.
    • (b) Define timelike, null, and spacelike, and say which earlier notion the word “null” renames.
    • (c) True or false: the sum of two spacelike vectors is spacelike. Justify your answer.
    • (d) Write down detBt and Bt−1, and say which row of the comparison table each answer sits in.
    • (e) True or false: O(1,1) is abelian. Justify your answer.

    Solution.

    • (a) The convention is (+,−,−,−), so [η]EE=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), with signature (1,3) and inertia (1,3,0) (Definition 14.5.1). The opposite convention (−,+,+,+) uses −η and has signature (3,1).

    • (b) For x≠0: timelike means q(x)>0, null means q(x)=0, spacelike means q(x)<0 (Definition 14.11.2). “Null” renames “nonzero isotropic vector” (Definition 14.7.1).

    • (c) False. Take x=(1,2,0,0) and y=(1,−2,0,0), with q(x)=q(y)=1−4=−3<0. Their sum is (2,0,0,0), with q=4>0, which is timelike.

    • (d) detBt=1 by Theorem 14.11.1 with μ=1, and Bt−1=B−t, since BtB−t=B0=I2. The first sits in the “determinant +1 part” row, the second in the “addition law” row, which is what makes t↦Bt a group homomorphism.

    • (e) False. B1 and D=diag(1,−1) both lie in O(1,1), and B1D has (1,2) entry −sinh⁡1 while DB1 has (1,2) entry sinh⁡1; these differ since sinh⁡1≠0.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1)

    Determine which of the following lie in O(1,1). For each one that does, give its rapidity t and the signs ε,μ of Theorem 14.11.1. Justify your answer. A1=(5/34/34/35/3),A2=(2112),A3=(5/3−4/34/3−5/3).

    Solution. Write D=diag(1,−1) and test P⊤DP=D column by column, using q(p1)=1, q(p2)=−1, η2(p1,p2)=0.

    A1: (5/3)2−(4/3)2=(25−16)/9=1; (4/3)2−(5/3)2=−1; (5/3)(4/3)−(4/3)(5/3)=0. So A1∈O(1,1). Here sinh⁡t=4/3 and cosh⁡t=5/3, so ε=μ=1 and A1=Bt with t=log⁡3, since cosh⁡(log⁡3)=12(3+13)=5/3 and sinh⁡(log⁡3)=12(3−13)=4/3.

    A2: 22−12=3≠1, so the first column already fails and A2∉O(1,1). (Indeed A2⊤DA2=diag(3,−3)=3D, so A2 scales the form rather than preserving it.)

    A3: first column as for A1; second column (−4/3)2−(−5/3)2=−1; and (5/3)(−4/3)−(4/3)(−5/3)=0. So A3∈O(1,1), with t=log⁡3, ε=1, and μ=detA3=−25/9+16/9=−1; that is, A3=Blog⁡3D.

    Exercise (B2)
    • (a) Classify x=(3,1,2,2), y=(1,0,1,0), and z=(0,1,1,1) in R1,3 as timelike, null, or spacelike.
    • (b) The vector y is null. Find a w∈R1,3 such that (y,w) is a basis of a hyperbolic plane, that is, q(w)=0 and η(y,w)=1.

    Solution.

    • (a) q(x)=9−1−4−4=0, so x is null. q(y)=1−0−1−0=0, so y is null. q(z)=0−1−1−1=−3<0, so z is spacelike.

    • (b) Try w=(c,0,−c,0), which has q(w)=c2−c2=0 for every c. Then η(y,w)=1⋅c−1⋅(−c)=2c, so c=12 works: w=(12,0,−12,0). The Gram matrix of (y,w) is (0110), so span(y,w) is a hyperbolic plane (Definition 14.7.3).

    Exercise (B3)

    Let x=(5,3,0,0) and y=(13,12,0,0) in R1,3. Compute τ(x), τ(y), η(x,y), and τ(x+y), and check Lemma 14.11.2 and Theorem 14.11.3 on this pair. Is either inequality an equality?

    Solution. q(x)=25−9=16, so x is timelike and future-pointing with τ(x)=4. q(y)=169−144=25, so τ(y)=5. Next η(x,y)=5⋅13−3⋅12=65−36=29, and τ(x)τ(y)=20, so 29≥20 as Lemma 14.11.2 requires. Finally x+y=(18,15,0,0) with q=324−225=99, so τ(x+y)=99. Since 92=81<99, we get τ(x+y)>9=τ(x)+τ(y), as Theorem 14.11.3 requires. Neither is an equality, and indeed y is not a multiple of x, since 13/5≠12/3.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1)

    For t∈R put v(t)=tanh⁡t=sinh⁡t/cosh⁡t.

    • (a) Prove that v:R→(−1,1) is a bijection, and that Bte1 is a positive multiple of (1,v(t),0,0) read in R1,1.
    • (b) Hence deduce that v(s+t)=v(s)+v(t)1+v(s)v(t), and say which part of Theorem 14.11.1 does the work.

    Solution.

    • (a) Since cosh⁡t>0 and cosh2⁡t−sinh2⁡t=1, we have |sinh⁡t|<cosh⁡t, so |v(t)|<1 and v maps into (−1,1). For injectivity and surjectivity, solve v(t)=c with |c|<1: the equations sinh⁡t=ccosh⁡t and cosh2⁡t−sinh2⁡t=1 give cosh2⁡t(1−c2)=1, so cosh⁡t=(1−c2)−1/2, which is a legitimate value since it is ≥1, and then sinh⁡t=c(1−c2)−1/2 determines t uniquely because sinh is a bijection. So each c has exactly one preimage.

      In R1,1, Bte1=(cosh⁡t,sinh⁡t)=cosh⁡t(1,v(t)), and cosh⁡t>0.

    • (b) By Theorem 14.11.1, BsBt=Bs+t. Comparing entries, v(s+t)=sinh⁡(s+t)cosh⁡(s+t)=sinh⁡scosh⁡t+cosh⁡ssinh⁡tcosh⁡scosh⁡t+sinh⁡ssinh⁡t, and dividing numerator and denominator by cosh⁡scosh⁡t>0 gives (v(s)+v(t))/(1+v(s)v(t)). The work is done by the addition law BsBt=Bs+t; the parametrization merely renames t as v(t), and because v is a bijection onto (−1,1) no information is lost in the renaming.
    Exercise (C2)
    • (a) Prove that {Pe1:P∈O(1,1)} is the set of all x∈R1,1 with q(x)=1, and that this set is unbounded.
    • (b) Deduce that no bound |pij|≤c holds for all P∈O(1,1), and contrast this with O(2), where |pij|≤1 always. Which hypothesis of Chapter 11’s O(2) theory is responsible?

    Solution.

    • (a) (⊆) If P∈O(1,1) then q(Pe1)=(Pe1)⊤D(Pe1)=e1⊤P⊤DPe1=e1⊤De1=1. (⊇) If q(x)=1, the proof of Theorem 14.11.1 shows x=(εcosh⁡t,sinh⁡t) for some t and sign ε; then x is the first column of Bt when ε=1, and of −B−t when ε=−1, and both lie in O(1,1). The set is unbounded because cosh⁡t→∞ as t→∞.

    • (b) The first column of P ranges over an unbounded set by (a), so its entries are unbounded, and no such c exists; this is the unboundedness Section 10 recorded, obtained here from the orbit rather than from a one-parameter family. In O(2) the columns are unit vectors for the Euclidean norm, so every entry has modulus at most 1 and the group sits inside a bounded region. The responsible hypothesis is positive definiteness of the form: it is what makes q(x)=1 a bounded set, namely the unit sphere. Once one sign is reversed, the level set is a hyperbola and nothing is bounded.

    Exercise (C3)

    The Quick check above showed that R1,3 has no plane on which the form vanishes identically. Show that R2,2 does have one, by exhibiting two vectors that span it, and reconcile the two answers with Theorem 14.7.5.

    Hint: pair each positive coordinate with a negative one.

    Solution. In R2,2 the form is η′(x,y)=x1y1+x2y2−x3y3−x4y4. Put u=(1,0,1,0) and w=(0,1,0,1). Then q(u)=1−1=0,q(w)=1−1=0,η′(u,w)=0, the last because every term pairs a zero with something. The list (u,w) is independent, since a relation au+bw=0 reads (a,b,a,b)=0. So span(u,w) is a totally isotropic plane, and by bilinearity η′ vanishes on all of it.

    The reconciliation: by Definition 14.7.4 the Witt index bounds the dimension of a totally isotropic subspace, it counts the hyperbolic planes of Theorem 14.7.5, and over R it equals min(n+,n−) (Proposition 14.7.7). For R1,3 this is min(1,3)=1, so totally isotropic subspaces are lines at most; for R2,2 it is min(2,2)=2, so a totally isotropic plane is exactly what one should expect, and the two vectors above realize it. What differs is not the dimension 4 but how evenly the four signs are split.
