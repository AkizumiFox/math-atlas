    12.2  Self-Adjoint Operators

    Among complex numbers, the real ones are those fixed by conjugation: z―=z. Among operators on an inner product space, conjugation is replaced by the adjoint, and the operators fixed by it are the self-adjoint ones, T∗=T. The analogy is not decoration. Self-adjoint operators have real eigenvalues, they come with a real-valued quantity ⟨Tv,v⟩ attached to every vector, they can be compared and ordered, and over R they are exactly the operators the spectral theorem applies to. This section collects everything about them that does not need a spectral theorem, and in doing so assembles every ingredient the real spectral theorem of Section 5 will use.

    Throughout, V is a finite-dimensional inner product space over F=R or F=C, and T∈L(V).

    12.2.1. The operators fixed by starring

    Chapter 11 already made the definition, so we only recall it: T∈L(V) is self-adjoint when T∗=T, equivalently when ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩for all u,v∈V (Definition 11.6.2). In an orthonormal basis this says the matrix is Hermitian, A∗=A, which over R is symmetric, A⊤=A (Theorem 11.6.3). Three facts were proved there and will be used without further comment: ⟨Tv,v⟩ is real for every v, even over C; the diagonal entries of the matrix in an orthonormal basis are real; and every self-adjoint operator is normal (Proposition 11.6.7).

    Deciding self-adjointness is always the same job — compute T∗, or check the displayed identity on a basis — but the traps differ by example.

    Example (Four Tests)

    Determine which of the following are self-adjoint. Justify your answer; for those that are not, name the failing pair of vectors.

    • (a) TA on C2 with the standard inner product, A=(21−i1+i3).
    • (b) TB on C2 with the standard inner product, B=(1ii1).
    • (c) Differentiation D on V=R[x]≤2 with ⟨p,q⟩=∫01pq.
    • (d) The orthogonal projection PU onto a subspace U of any V.

    Solution.

    • (a) Self-adjoint. Transposing swaps the off-diagonal entries and conjugating swaps them back, while the diagonal entries 2 and 3 are real; so A∗=A. The standard basis of C2 is orthonormal, so Theorem 11.6.3 turns this into TA∗=TA.

    • (b) Not self-adjoint. It is symmetric, B⊤=B, but B∗=(1−i−i1)≠B. The failing pair is e1,e2: ⟨Be1,e2⟩=i, while ⟨e1,Be2⟩=i―=−i.

    • (c) Not self-adjoint. Take p=x and q=1. Then Dp=1 and Dq=0, so ⟨Dp,q⟩=∫011dx=1,⟨p,Dq⟩=0. The obstruction is visible in general: integration by parts gives ⟨Dp,q⟩+⟨p,Dq⟩=p(1)q(1)−p(0)q(0), so D is as far from self-adjoint as it could be — the two sides add up rather than agreeing, apart from boundary terms.

    • (d) Self-adjoint, for every U. By Theorem 11.3.3 (b), ⟨PUu,v⟩=⟨u,PUv⟩ for all u,v, which is the displayed identity.

    Warning. Over C, symmetric is not the right notion; Hermitian is. Part (b) above is symmetric and its eigenvalues are not even real: trB=2 and detB=1−i2=2, so pB=x2−2x+2 and the eigenvalues are 1+i and 1−i. Every theorem in this section would fail for it. The word “symmetric matrix” belongs to the real case, where conjugation does nothing and A∗=A⊤; over C always write, and check, A∗=A.

    12.2.2. Eigenvalues are real

    The first theorem is the operator version of “a number equal to its own conjugate is real”, and its proof is the move that runs this whole chapter: compute one inner product two different ways.

    Theorem 12.2.1 (Self-adjoint Operators Have Real Eigenvalues)

    Let V be a finite-dimensional inner product space over F=R or F=C and let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint. Then every eigenvalue of T is real.

    Idea. Take Tv=λv with v≠0 and pair it with v. Computing ⟨Tv,v⟩ straight off the eigenvalue equation produces λ; computing it by pushing T into the second slot produces λ―, because a scalar leaving the second slot picks up a conjugate. The two answers must agree, and ‖v‖2≠0 lets us cancel.

    Proof. Let λ be an eigenvalue of T, with eigenvector v≠0. On one side, ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨λv,v⟩=λ‖v‖2, by linearity in the first slot. On the other, using Definition 11.6.2 and then conjugate-linearity in the second slot, ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨v,Tv⟩=⟨v,λv⟩=λ―‖v‖2. So λ‖v‖2=λ―‖v‖2. Since v≠0 we have ‖v‖2≠0 by positive definiteness (Definition 11.1.1), and dividing gives λ=λ―. A scalar equal to its own conjugate is real.

    Chapter 11 already proved this, in the remark after Proposition 11.6.7. It is promoted to a theorem here because Sections 3 to 5 lean on it constantly, and a result cited that often should have a number. Over R the conclusion sounds empty, because the eigenvalues of a real operator are real by definition. What is not empty over R is the corresponding statement about the roots of the characteristic polynomial, and that is the next theorem: it says the roots do not escape into C, which is exactly what guarantees that a real self-adjoint operator has an eigenvector at all.

    12.2.3. Existence over the real numbers

    Over C an eigenvalue is free (Theorem 9.2.4). Over R it is not, and the rotation of R2 by a quarter turn has none. Self-adjointness is what rules that out.

    Theorem 12.2.2 (Real Self-adjoint Operators Have an Eigenvalue)

    Let V≠{0} be a finite-dimensional inner product space over R and let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint. Then:

    • (a) every root of pT in C is real, so pT splits over R;
    • (b) T has an eigenvalue.

    Idea. There is no complex number in sight, so we put one there. Fix an orthonormal basis; the matrix A of T is real symmetric. The same array of numbers, read as a complex matrix, is Hermitian, and it acts on Cn, which is the complexification of Rn (Definition 1.9.2, Example (The Complexification of Rn Is Cn)) — the arena Chapter 1 built, and Chapter 9 put to work, precisely so that a real matrix could borrow complex eigenvalues. Over C an eigenvalue exists, and Theorem 12.2.1 says it is real. A real root of pT is an eigenvalue of T itself, and we are back where we started, one eigenvalue richer.

    Proof. Let n=dim⁡V≥1. By Theorem 11.2.2, V has an orthonormal basis B; put A=[T]BB∈Mn(R). By Theorem 11.6.3 and Definition 11.6.2, A∗=A, and since A is real this says A⊤=A.

    Now read A as an element of Mn(C), acting on Cn with the standard inner product; this is the extension of the real map TA to the complexification (Rn)C=Cn, which acts on x+iy by Ax+iAy (Chapter 9, Section 2). Because A is real and symmetric, A∗=A―⊤=A⊤=A, so TA is self-adjoint as an operator on Cn (Theorem 11.6.3 again, the standard basis of Cn being orthonormal).

    • (a) By Definition 7.10.2, pT=pA, and the entries of xI−A are real, so this is the same polynomial whether the determinant is expanded over R or over C; it lies in R[x]⊆C[x]. Let λ∈C be a root of pA. By Theorem 9.2.1, λ is an eigenvalue of A over C, and A is self-adjoint there, so λ∈R by Theorem 12.2.1. Since every root of pT in C lies in R, and pT splits over C by Corollary 6.5.5, it splits over R.

    • (b) pT is monic of degree n≥1 (Theorem 7.10.2), so by (a) it has a root λ∈R. By Theorem 9.2.1 ((e) ⇒ (a)), λ is an eigenvalue of T. This proves the theorem.

    Remark. There is a second, very different route to (b): the function v↦⟨Tv,v⟩ is real-valued and continuous on the unit sphere of V, which is closed and bounded, so it attains a maximum, and a vector where it does so turns out to be an eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue. That argument is shorter to state but it is analysis, not algebra, and it appeals to the extreme value theorem directly. We keep to the algebraic route, which reaches that theorem only at one remove: the proof just given rests on Corollary 6.5.5, hence on the fundamental theorem of algebra (Theorem 6.5.1), and the proof of that in Chapter 6 uses the extreme value theorem on a closed disc. The maximization picture returns in Chapter 17, where the Rayleigh quotient and the Courant–Fischer theorem describe every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator by optimization.

    Quick check. The rotation R=(0−110) on R2 has no eigenvalue, and over C its eigenvalues ±i are not real. Which hypothesis of Theorem 12.2.2 does it fail, and how do you see it in one line?

    Solution. Self-adjointness. In the standard orthonormal basis the matrix of R∗ is R⊤=(01−10)=−R, which is not R. So R is skew-adjoint, not self-adjoint, and neither theorem applies. (It is, however, normal, since R⊤R=RR⊤=I — which is why Section 5 has to insist on self-adjoint rather than normal over R.)

    12.2.4. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal

    The second theorem is the same move with two vectors instead of one, and it is the geometric half of the spectral theorem: the eigenspaces are not merely independent, as in Chapter 9, but perpendicular.

    Theorem 12.2.3 (Distinct Eigenvalues Give Orthogonal Eigenvectors)

    Let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint, and let u,v∈V satisfy Tu=λu and Tv=μv with λ≠μ. Then ⟨u,v⟩=0. Consequently Eλ(T)⊥Eμ(T) for distinct eigenvalues λ,μ.

    Proof. Compute ⟨Tu,v⟩ two ways. First, ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨λu,v⟩=λ⟨u,v⟩. Second, by Definition 11.6.2 and conjugate-linearity in the second slot, ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩=⟨u,μv⟩=μ―⟨u,v⟩=μ⟨u,v⟩, the last step because μ is real by Theorem 12.2.1. Subtracting, (λ−μ)⟨u,v⟩=0, and λ−μ≠0, so ⟨u,v⟩=0. Since every element of Eλ(T) is such a u and every element of Eμ(T) such a v, the two eigenspaces are orthogonal.

    Note where the previous theorem was spent: it made μ―=μ, so that the conjugation which appears on the way out of the second slot does no damage. Without real eigenvalues the computation would end at λ⟨u,v⟩=μ―⟨u,v⟩, which says nothing when λ=μ―.

    Example (A Hermitian Two-by-Two)

    For A=(21−i1+i3) on C2, find the eigenvalues and an eigenvector for each, and verify that the two eigenvectors are orthogonal.

    Solution. Here trA=5 and detA=6−(1−i)(1+i)=6−2=4, so pA=x2−5x+4=(x−1)(x−4). Both eigenvalues are real, as Theorem 12.2.1 promised.

    For λ=1: A−I=(11−i1+i2), and the first row gives x+(1−i)y=0, so u=(−1+i,1). Check the second row: (1+i)(−1+i)+2=(−1+i−i+i2)+2=−2+2=0.

    For λ=4: A−4I=(−21−i1+i−1), and the first row gives −2x+(1−i)y=0, so v=(1−i,2).

    Finally ⟨u,v⟩=(−1+i)(1−i)―+1⋅2―=(−1+i)(1+i)+2=−2+2=0, so u⊥v, as Theorem 12.2.3 requires. Normalizing, ‖u‖2=2+1=3 and ‖v‖2=2+4=6, so (13(−1+i,1), 16(1−i,2)) is an orthonormal basis of C2 consisting of eigenvectors of A. Section 4 proves that this always happens, for exactly the operators it happens for.

    12.2.5. Invariant complements

    Section 5 will prove the real spectral theorem by the move named at the front of the chapter: find one eigenvector, pass to its orthogonal complement, induct. For that to work the complement must be invariant, and the restriction must again be self-adjoint so that the induction can be applied to it. Both hold, and neither needs an eigenvector — the statement is about any invariant subspace.

    Theorem 12.2.4 (Self-adjointness Passes to Orthogonal Complements)

    Let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint and let U⊆V be a T-invariant subspace. Then:

    • (a) U⊥ is T-invariant;
    • (b) T|U∈L(U) and T|U⊥∈L(U⊥) are self-adjoint, for the inner products inherited from V.

    Proof.

    • (a) Since T is self-adjoint, T∗=T, so U is T∗-invariant. By Lemma 12.1.1, U⊥ is T-invariant.

    • (b) U is T-invariant, so T|U maps U to U and is an operator on U. For u1,u2∈U, ⟨T|Uu1,u2⟩=⟨Tu1,u2⟩=⟨u1,Tu2⟩=⟨u1,T|Uu2⟩, where the inner products are those of V, which is what U inherits. By Definition 11.6.2, T|U is self-adjoint. The same three equalities with U⊥ in place of U give the second claim, using (a) to know that T|U⊥ is an operator on U⊥.

    So the pair (U,U⊥) splits a self-adjoint operator into two self-adjoint operators on smaller spaces, and nothing is lost: V=U⊕U⊥ by Theorem 11.3.2 (a). Combined with Theorem 12.2.2, which guarantees that a nonzero space always offers an eigenvector to start from, this is a complete induction, and Section 5 will do no more than write it out.

    12.2.6. Two zero tests, and the gap between the fields

    An operator carries the function v↦⟨Tv,v⟩, sometimes called its quadratic form. How much of T does that one function remember? The answer depends on the field, and the difference is the sharpest illustration in this book of why C is the easier place to work.

    Theorem 12.2.5 (The Zero Test for Self-adjoint Operators)

    Let V be a finite-dimensional inner product space over F=R or F=C and let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint. If ⟨Tv,v⟩=0 for every v∈V, then T=0.

    Idea. The hypothesis only ever feeds T one vector, while the conclusion is about ⟨Tu,w⟩ for two. So manufacture two out of one: apply the hypothesis to u+w and expand. The diagonal terms die by hypothesis and what is left is a statement about the cross terms, which self-adjointness turns into a statement about ⟨Tu,w⟩ alone.

    Proof. Let u,w∈V. Expanding ⟨T(u+w),u+w⟩ in both slots, 0=⟨Tu,u⟩+⟨Tu,w⟩+⟨Tw,u⟩+⟨Tw,w⟩=⟨Tu,w⟩+⟨Tw,u⟩, the two outer terms vanishing by hypothesis. Since T is self-adjoint, ⟨Tw,u⟩=⟨w,Tu⟩=⟨Tu,w⟩― by conjugate symmetry. Writing z=⟨Tu,w⟩, the display says z+z―=0, that is (∗)Re⟨Tu,w⟩=0for all u,w∈V.

    If F=R this already says ⟨Tu,w⟩=0 for all u,w. If F=C, apply (∗) with iw in place of w: since ⟨Tu,iw⟩=i―z=−iz and Re(−iz)=Im(z), we also get Im⟨Tu,w⟩=0. Either way ⟨Tu,w⟩=0 for all u,w.

    Taking w=Tu gives ‖Tu‖2=0, so Tu=0 for every u, that is T=0. This proves the theorem.

    Now drop the hypothesis. Over C nothing is lost.

    Theorem 12.2.6 (The Zero Test over the Complex Numbers)

    Let V be a finite-dimensional complex inner product space and let T∈L(V) be any operator. If ⟨Tv,v⟩=0 for every v∈V, then T=0.

    Idea. The previous proof used self-adjointness in exactly one place: to convert ⟨Tw,u⟩ into ⟨Tu,w⟩―, which gave the real part only. Without it we must extract the imaginary part some other way, and over C there is another way — substitute u+iw as well as u+w, and take a combination of the two identities. There is no third substitution available over R, which is precisely why the real statement is false.

    Proof. Let u,w∈V. Applying the hypothesis to u+w and expanding as before, (1)⟨Tu,w⟩+⟨Tw,u⟩=0. Applying it to u+iw and using ⟨Tu,iw⟩=−i⟨Tu,w⟩, ⟨T(iw),u⟩=i⟨Tw,u⟩, and ⟨T(iw),iw⟩=ii―⟨Tw,w⟩=0, −i⟨Tu,w⟩+i⟨Tw,u⟩=0. Multiplying that by i gives (2)⟨Tu,w⟩−⟨Tw,u⟩=0. Adding (1) and (2), 2⟨Tu,w⟩=0, so ⟨Tu,w⟩=0 for all u,w. Taking w=Tu gives ‖Tu‖2=0, hence T=0.

    This statement appeared once already, as Exercise (C2: Linearity is a hypothesis, not a consequence) (b), where it was an exercise in the arithmetic of complex inner products. It is restated and proved here because Section 3 needs to cite it as a theorem: the four characterizations of a normal operator all come from applying it to T∗T−TT∗. The proof is the same one; what is new is the company it now keeps.

    Warning. Over R, the quadratic form does not determine the operator. Let T be the quarter turn of R2, T(x1,x2)=(−x2,x1). Then ⟨Tx,x⟩=−x2x1+x1x2=0for every x∈R2, and yet T≠0. So Theorem 12.2.6 genuinely fails over R, and Theorem 12.2.5 genuinely needs its hypothesis: this T is not self-adjoint, since T∗=−T. The general real statement is that ⟨Tv,v⟩=0 for all v forces T∗=−T and no more — Exercise (C3: What the real quadratic form does determine).

    Quick check. Over C, suppose ⟨Tv,v⟩ is real for every v∈V. Must T be self-adjoint?

    Solution. Yes. Put S=T−T∗. For every v, ⟨T∗v,v⟩=⟨v,Tv⟩=⟨Tv,v⟩―=⟨Tv,v⟩, the last step because the number is real. Hence ⟨Sv,v⟩=0 for all v, and Theorem 12.2.6 gives S=0, that is T∗=T. Combined with Proposition 11.6.7 (a), this says that over C “self-adjoint” and “⟨Tv,v⟩ always real” are the same condition. Over R the criterion is useless, since ⟨Tv,v⟩ is automatically real for every operator.

    12.2.7. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)
    • (a) State what it means for T∈L(V) to be self-adjoint, in terms of T∗ and in terms of inner products, and say what the matrix condition is and in which bases it may be used.
    • (b) True or false: a symmetric matrix in M2(C) has real eigenvalues. Justify your answer.
    • (c) Which field does Theorem 12.2.2 concern, and which earlier theorem supplies the eigenvalue it borrows?
    • (d) True or false: if T is self-adjoint and U is any subspace, then U⊥ is T-invariant. Justify your answer.
    • (e) State the difference between Theorem 12.2.5 and Theorem 12.2.6, and give the standard example showing the second has no real analogue.
    • (f) Give a normal operator on R2 that is not self-adjoint.

    Solution.

    • (a) T∗=T; equivalently ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩ for all u,v∈V (Definition 11.6.2). The matrix condition is A∗=A, and by Theorem 11.6.3 it may be used only in an orthonormal basis.

    • (b) False. B=(1ii1) is symmetric with eigenvalues 1±i (Example (Four Tests) (b) and the warning after it). Over C the relevant condition is A∗=A, not A⊤=A.

    • (c) The real field: it says a self-adjoint operator on a nonzero real inner product space has an eigenvalue. The eigenvalue is borrowed from Theorem 9.2.4, applied to the matrix read as a complex matrix on the complexification Cn of Rn, and is then shown to be real by Theorem 12.2.1.

    • (d) False as stated: U must be T-invariant (Theorem 12.2.4). For example, T=TA on R2 with A=(0110) is self-adjoint, and U=span(e1) has U⊥=span(e2) with Te2=e1∉U⊥. Here U was not invariant either, since Te1=e2.

    • (e) Theorem 12.2.5 holds over R and C but assumes T self-adjoint; Theorem 12.2.6 drops that assumption but holds only over C. The quarter turn T(x1,x2)=(−x2,x1) of R2 satisfies ⟨Tx,x⟩=0 for every x and is not zero.

    • (f) The same quarter turn: T∗=−T, so T∗T=TT∗=id and T is normal, while T∗≠T.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1: Determine which are self-adjoint)

    Determine which of the following operators are self-adjoint. Justify your answer; for those that are not, exhibit a pair u,v with ⟨Tu,v⟩≠⟨u,Tv⟩, or compute T∗.

    • (a) TA on C2, A=(12+i2−i−5), standard inner product.
    • (b) TB on C2, B=(01−10), standard inner product.
    • (c) TC on R3, C=(120213031), standard inner product.
    • (d) S∗S, for an arbitrary S∈L(V,W) between finite-dimensional inner product spaces.
    • (e) PU−PU⊥, for a subspace U of a finite-dimensional V.
    • (f) T(x1,x2)=(x2,x1) on R2 with the standard inner product, whose matrix in the basis C=((1,0),(1,2)) is (−12321212), which is not symmetric.

    Solution.

    • (a) Self-adjoint. The diagonal entries 1 and −5 are real and the off-diagonal pair 2+i, 2−i are conjugates, so A∗=A; the standard basis is orthonormal, so Theorem 11.6.3 applies.

    • (b) Not self-adjoint. B∗=B⊤=(0−110)=−B, so TB is skew-adjoint. Concretely ⟨Be1,e2⟩=−1 while ⟨e1,Be2⟩=1.

    • (c) Self-adjoint. C is real and C⊤=C, and the standard basis of R3 is orthonormal.

    • (d) Self-adjoint, for every S. By Theorem 11.6.2 (c) and (d), (S∗S)∗=S∗S∗∗=S∗S.

    • (e) Self-adjoint. Each of PU and PU⊥ is self-adjoint (Example (Four Tests) (d)), and by Theorem 11.6.2 (a) and (b) the difference of self-adjoint operators is self-adjoint: (PU−PU⊥)∗=PU∗−PU⊥∗=PU−PU⊥.

    • (f) Self-adjoint, in spite of the nonsymmetric matrix. The basis C is not orthonormal — ⟨(1,0),(1,2)⟩=1≠0 — so Theorem 11.6.3 does not apply to it, and the shape of [T]CC says nothing either way. Test the definition instead: for x,y∈R2, ⟨Tx,y⟩=x2y1+x1y2=⟨x,Ty⟩, so T∗=T. In the standard basis, which is orthonormal, the matrix is (0110), symmetric as it must be. The moral is the one Chapter 11, Section 6 closes with: self-adjointness is a property of the operator and the inner product, never of a matrix taken on its own.

    Exercise (B2: Eigenvalues and orthogonality)
    • (a) For A=(0−ii0)∈M2(C), check that A is Hermitian, find its eigenvalues and an eigenvector for each, and verify that the eigenvectors are orthogonal.
    • (b) For S=(311131113)∈M3(R), find the eigenvalues with their multiplicities and bases of the eigenspaces, and verify that the two eigenspaces are orthogonal.
    • (c) Hence write down an orthonormal basis of R3 consisting of eigenvectors of S.

    Solution.

    • (a) A∗=A―⊤=(0−ii0)=A, so A is Hermitian. Then trA=0 and detA=0−(−i)(i)=−1, so pA=x2−1 and the eigenvalues are 1 and −1, both real.

      A(1,i)=(−i⋅i,i⋅1)=(1,i), so u=(1,i) works for λ=1. A(1,−i)=(−1,i)=−(1,−i), so v=(1,−i) works for λ=−1. Finally ⟨u,v⟩=1⋅1―+i(−i)―=1+i⋅i=0.

    • (b) Expanding the determinant gives pS=(x−5)(x−2)2, so spec(S)={5,2} with algebraic multiplicities 1 and 2. The eigenspaces are read off from S=2I+J, where J is the all-ones matrix: since J(1,1,1)=(3,3,3) and Jx=0 exactly when x1+x2+x3=0, E5(S)=span((1,1,1)),E2(S)=span((1,−1,0),(1,0,−1)), of dimensions 1 and 2. Orthogonality: (1,1,1)⋅(1,−1,0)=0 and (1,1,1)⋅(1,0,−1)=0, so E5(S)⊥E2(S), as Theorem 12.2.3 requires.

    • (c) The two vectors spanning E2(S) are not orthogonal to each other, so run Gram–Schmidt inside that eigenspace (Theorem 11.2.2). With w1=(1,−1,0), (1,0,−1)−12(1,−1,0)=12(1,1,−2). Normalizing all three vectors gives the orthonormal eigenbasis 13(1,1,1),12(1,−1,0),16(1,1,−2). The eigenvalues are 5,2,2 in that order.

    Exercise (B3: A self-adjoint square root of zero)

    Let T∈L(V) be self-adjoint with T2=0. Prove that T=0. Deduce that a self-adjoint T with Tk=0 for some k≥1 is zero.

    Solution. For every v∈V, by Definition 11.6.2, ‖Tv‖2=⟨Tv,Tv⟩=⟨T2v,v⟩=⟨0,v⟩=0, so Tv=0. As v was arbitrary, T=0.

    For the deduction, use strong induction on k (Theorem 0.2.10). If k=1 there is nothing to prove. Let k≥2 with Tk=0, and put m=⌈k/2⌉, so that 1≤m<k and 2m≥k. Then (Tm)2=T2m=T2m−kTk=0, and Tm is self-adjoint, since (Tm)∗=(T∗)m=Tm by Theorem 11.6.2 (c). By the first part, Tm=0, and the inductive hypothesis applied to the exponent m gives T=0.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1: The operator S∗S)

    Let S∈L(V,W) between finite-dimensional inner product spaces over F, and set T=S∗S∈L(V).

    • (a) Prove that T is self-adjoint and that ⟨Tv,v⟩≥0 for every v∈V, with equality if and only if Sv=0.
    • (b) Deduce that every eigenvalue of T is a nonnegative real number.
    • (c) Prove that T is invertible if and only if S is injective.

    Hint for (a): the quantity ⟨Tv,v⟩ is a norm squared in disguise.

    Solution.

    • (a) T∗=(S∗S)∗=S∗S∗∗=S∗S=T, by Theorem 11.6.2 (c) and (d). For the second claim, use conjugate symmetry and then Definition 11.6.1: ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨v,S∗Sv⟩―=⟨Sv,Sv⟩―=‖Sv‖2, the last step because a norm squared is a nonnegative real number and is its own conjugate. So ⟨Tv,v⟩≥0, with equality exactly when ‖Sv‖=0, that is Sv=0 (Definition 11.1.1).

    • (b) Let Tv=λv with v≠0. By Theorem 12.2.1, λ∈R. By (a), 0≤⟨Tv,v⟩=λ‖v‖2, and ‖v‖2>0, so λ≥0.

    • (c) (⇒) If Sv=0 then Tv=0, so injectivity of T forces v=0; and an invertible operator is injective. (⇐) If S is injective and Tv=0, then by (a) ‖Sv‖2=⟨Tv,v⟩=0, so Sv=0 and v=0. Thus kerT={0}, so T is injective, hence bijective by Corollary 2.3.2 (e). This is Corollary 11.6.6 (b) again, seen from the quadratic form.

    Operators with the property in (a) are called positive, and they are the subject of Chapter 13; part (b) is the first half of the characterization proved there.

    Exercise (C2: Sums and products)

    Let S,T∈L(V) be self-adjoint.

    • (a) Prove that S+T and cT are self-adjoint for every real scalar c, and show by example that cT need not be self-adjoint for c∈C.
    • (b) Prove that ST is self-adjoint if and only if ST=TS.
    • (c) Give self-adjoint S,T∈L(C2) with ST not self-adjoint.

    Solution.

    • (a) By Theorem 11.6.2 (a), (S+T)∗=S∗+T∗=S+T. By (b) of the same theorem, (cT)∗=c―T∗=c―T, which equals cT exactly when c―=c or T=0. For a complex counterexample take T=id on C2 and c=i: then (iid)∗=−iid≠iid.

    • (b) By Theorem 11.6.2 (c), (ST)∗=T∗S∗=TS. So (ST)∗=ST if and only if TS=ST.

    • (c) Take S=TA and T=TB on C2 with A=(1000),B=(0110). Both are Hermitian. But AB=(0100) and BA=(0010), which differ, so by (b) the product is not self-adjoint. Indeed (AB)∗=BA≠AB.

    Exercise (C3: What the real quadratic form does determine)

    Let V be a finite-dimensional inner product space over R and T∈L(V). Call T skew-adjoint if T∗=−T.

    • (a) Prove that ⟨Tv,v⟩=0 for every v∈V if and only if T is skew-adjoint.
    • (b) Deduce that every T∈L(V) can be written uniquely as T=A+B with A self-adjoint and B skew-adjoint, and that ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨Av,v⟩ for every v.
    • (c) Conclude that over R the function v↦⟨Tv,v⟩ determines A and nothing about B. Contrast this with the complex case.

    Solution.

    • (a) (⇐) If T∗=−T, then for every v, ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨v,T∗v⟩=−⟨v,Tv⟩=−⟨Tv,v⟩, the last step by symmetry of a real inner product. A real number equal to its own negative is 0.

      (⇒) Suppose ⟨Tv,v⟩=0 for all v. Expanding ⟨T(u+w),u+w⟩=0 and canceling the two diagonal terms, ⟨Tu,w⟩+⟨Tw,u⟩=0. Over R, ⟨Tw,u⟩=⟨u,Tw⟩, so ⟨Tu,w⟩=⟨u,−Tw⟩ for all u,w. By the uniqueness in Theorem 11.6.1, T∗=−T.

    • (b) Put A=12(T+T∗) and B=12(T−T∗). Then A+B=T, and by Theorem 11.6.2 (a), (b), and (d), A∗=A and B∗=−B. For uniqueness, if T=A′+B′ is another such splitting, then T∗=A′−B′, so A′=12(T+T∗)=A and B′=B. Finally ⟨Bv,v⟩=0 for every v by (a), so ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨Av,v⟩+⟨Bv,v⟩=⟨Av,v⟩.

    • (c) By (b), two operators T and T′ with the same self-adjoint part have the same quadratic form; and conversely, if ⟨Tv,v⟩=⟨T′v,v⟩ for all v, then A−A′ is self-adjoint with vanishing quadratic form, so A=A′ by Theorem 12.2.5. So the quadratic form sees exactly A. The skew part is invisible: the quarter turn of R2 is skew-adjoint, and adding it to any T changes nothing. Over C, by contrast, Theorem 12.2.6 says the quadratic form determines T completely — there is no invisible part at all, which is why the complex theory is the easier one.
