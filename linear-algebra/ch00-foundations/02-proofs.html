    0.2  How Proofs Are Built

    The previous section taught us to read statements: what an implication claims, what the quantifiers demand, and how to negate. Now we write proofs of them. A proof is a chain of statements, each one justified by a definition, a hypothesis, or an earlier result, that ends in the statement we want. This section collects the handful of shapes that almost every proof in this book takes, shows each on a small fact about integers, and ends with the conventions that the book’s proofs follow.

    Throughout, a theorem will usually come with two blocks. The Idea says how the argument was found, often by working backwards from the goal. The Proof then presents the argument forwards, one justified step at a time. Read the Idea first, then check the Proof line by line.

    0.2.1. Direct proof

    Most statements have the form “for every x, if P(x) then Q(x)”. The direct way to prove this follows the shape of the statement:

    1. Let x be arbitrary. By Definition 0.1.3, a universal statement is a claim about each element, so we must not pick a convenient one.
    2. Suppose P(x). By Definition 0.1.2, the implication can only fail when P(x) is true, so that is the only case we need to handle.
    3. Unwind P(x) and Q(x) into their definitions. Then push forward from what we have towards what we want, often working backwards from the goal on scrap paper first.

    Before we can prove anything about integers, we need definitions to unwind.

    Definition 0.2.1 (Even and odd)

    An integer n is even if there exists an integer k with n=2k. An integer n is odd if there exists an integer k with n=2k+1.

    So 0=2⋅0 is even, −6=2⋅(−3) is even, and 7=2⋅3+1 is odd. Each definition is an existential statement: to show a number is even, we must produce the integer k. We use the school fact that every integer is even or odd, and not both; an exercise at the end of this section proves a more general fact about remainders.

    Proposition 0.2.1 (Sum of even integers)

    For all integers m and n, if m and n are even, then m+n is even.

    Idea. Givens: m=2a and n=2b for some integers a,b. Goal: m+n=2k for some integer k. So we must find k. Adding the givens gives m+n=2a+2b=2(a+b), and k=a+b is the witness.

    Proof. Let m and n be even integers. By Definition 0.2.1, there exist integers a and b with m=2a and n=2b. Then m+n=2a+2b=2(a+b). Since a+b is an integer, Definition 0.2.1 shows that m+n is even. This proves the claim.

    The pattern is worth naming. The hypothesis “m is even” is an existence statement, so using it hands us an integer a to work with. The goal “m+n is even” is also an existence statement, so proving it means producing an integer, here a+b. The two letters a and b must be different: m and n are arbitrary, so their halves have nothing to do with each other.

    Warning. A proof of a “for every” statement cannot start with a specific example. Writing “4+6=10, which is even” checks one case and proves nothing about all even integers. Equally, writing m=2k and n=2k with the same k silently assumes m=n, so it only proves that 2m is even.

    0.2.2. Proof by contrapositive

    Sometimes the direct route stalls. Consider the statement “if n2 is even, then n is even”. Going directly, we would suppose n2=2k and try to say something about n. But n=±2k is not visibly of the form 2⋅(integer), and we are stuck.

    By Theorem 0.1.1, “if P then Q” is equivalent to “if not Q then not P”. Here the contrapositive is “if n is not even, then n2 is not even”, that is, “if n is odd, then n2 is odd”. Now the hypothesis hands us a formula for n, and squaring a formula is easy.

    Proposition 0.2.2 (Even squares)

    For every integer n, if n2 is even, then n is even.

    Idea. The hypothesis n2 even says little about n, but “n odd” gives n=2k+1 explicitly. So prove the contrapositive: from n=2k+1, expand n2 and look for the form 2(…)+1.

    Proof. Let n be an integer. By Theorem 0.1.1, it suffices to prove that if n is not even, then n2 is not even. Suppose n is not even. Since every integer is even or odd, n is odd, so by Definition 0.2.1 there is an integer k with n=2k+1. Then n2=4k2+4k+1=2(2k2+2k)+1. Since 2k2+2k is an integer, n2 is odd by Definition 0.2.1. Since no integer is both even and odd, n2 is not even. This proves the contrapositive, and hence the statement.

    A contrapositive proof is a direct proof of a different, equivalent implication. Use it when the negated conclusion gives more to work with than the hypothesis does.

    0.2.3. Proof by contradiction

    To prove a statement S by contradiction, suppose that S is false and deduce something impossible: a statement together with its negation. Why does this work? We have shown that the implication ¬S⇒(R∧¬R) is true, for some statement R. Its conclusion R∧¬R is false in every row, so by Definition 0.1.2 the implication can only be true if its hypothesis ¬S is false. Hence S is true.

    Contradiction is the natural tool for statements that say something cannot happen, or that there is no object of some kind. Such statements give nothing to start from directly, while their negation hands us an object to study.

    Statements about fractions and primes need the language of divisibility.

    Definition 0.2.2 (Divisibility and primes)

    Let a and b be integers. We say a divides b, written a∣b, if there exists an integer k with b=ak. An integer p is prime if p≥2 and the only positive divisors of p are 1 and p.

    For example, 3∣12 since 12=3⋅4, and 3∤7. The first primes are 2,3,5,7,11. The integer 1 is not prime, because the definition requires p≥2; this convention is what makes factorizations into primes unique. In this language, n is even exactly when 2∣n.

    A real number is rational if it equals a/b for some integers a and b with b≠0, and irrational otherwise. We use the school fact that every rational number can be written in lowest terms: as a/b with b≥1, where no integer d≥2 divides both a and b. (We justify this fact at the end of this section.)

    Theorem 0.2.3 (Irrationality of 2)

    There is no rational number whose square is 2. That is, 2 is irrational.

    Idea. “There is no rational x with x2=2” gives nothing to hold, so suppose there is one, and write it in lowest terms a/b. Clearing denominators gives a2=2b2, an integer equation. Then Proposition 0.2.2 applies twice: first a is even, then (after substituting a=2c) b is even. That contradicts lowest terms.

    Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that some rational number x satisfies x2=2. Write x=a/b in lowest terms, so b≥1 and no integer d≥2 divides both a and b. Then a2/b2=2, and multiplying by b2, which is nonzero, gives a2=2b2. Therefore a2 is even by Definition 0.2.1, and hence a is even by Proposition 0.2.2. Write a=2c with c an integer. Substituting gives 4c2=2b2, so b2=2c2 is even. By Proposition 0.2.2 again, b is even. Thus 2 divides both a and b, which contradicts the choice of a/b in lowest terms. Hence no rational number has square 2.

    The contradiction came from the lowest-terms condition, which is the hypothesis we chose to impose. That is typical: in a contradiction proof, look for the assumption you have not yet used, because the contradiction usually comes from there.

    Remark. If a contradiction proof of “P⇒Q” assumes P and ¬Q and then only ever derives ¬P, it is really a contrapositive proof in disguise. Write it as a contrapositive; it is shorter and clearer.

    A second classic contradiction proof, that there are infinitely many primes, needs one more tool and appears at the end of this section.

    0.2.4. Proof by cases

    When a hypothesis splits naturally into possibilities, handle each one separately. If P1∨P2 is true, and both P1⇒Q and P2⇒Q are true, then Q is true: whichever of P1,P2 holds, its implication delivers Q. The cases must cover every possibility. They are allowed to overlap.

    Proposition 0.2.4 (n2+n is even)

    For every integer n, the integer n2+n is even.

    Idea. Nothing about n is given, but every integer is even or odd, and each case gives a formula for n. Factoring first, n2+n=n(n+1), makes each case a one-line computation.

    Proof. Let n be an integer. Then n2+n=n(n+1). Since every integer is even or odd, there are two cases.

    Case 1: n is even. By Definition 0.2.1, n=2k for some integer k. Then n(n+1)=2(k(n+1)), and k(n+1) is an integer.

    Case 2: n is odd. By Definition 0.2.1, n=2k+1 for some integer k. Then n+1=2(k+1), so n(n+1)=2(n(k+1)), and n(k+1) is an integer.

    In both cases n2+n is even by Definition 0.2.1. This shows the claim for every integer n.

    0.2.5. Proving “if and only if”

    By the definition of the biconditional in the previous section, P⇔Q means (P⇒Q)∧(Q⇒P). So a proof of an “iff” has two directions, labeled (⇒) and (⇐), and each is proved by any method we like. Often one direction is already known.

    Proposition 0.2.5 (Parity of a square)

    For every integer n, n is even if and only if n2 is even.

    Proof. Let n be an integer.

    (⇒) Suppose n is even, so n=2k for some integer k by Definition 0.2.1. Then n2=4k2=2(2k2), and 2k2 is an integer, so n2 is even.

    (⇐) This is Proposition 0.2.2.

    Both directions hold, so n is even if and only if n2 is even.

    Warning. Reversing the steps is not automatic. A common slip is to “prove the converse” by rewriting the same argument backwards without checking that every step reverses. For instance, from x=2 we get x2=4; but from x2=4 we only get x=2 or x=−2. Each direction needs its own justification.

    0.2.6. Existence and uniqueness

    To prove “there exists x with P(x)”, the most direct method is to produce a witness and check it. For example, “there exists an integer n with n2=n+2” is proved by n=2: indeed 22=4=2+2.

    To prove “there is at most one x with P(x)”, the standard move is: suppose x and x′ both satisfy P, and show x=x′. Notice that we do not assume x≠x′; we simply take two objects with the property and prove they are the same. “There exists a unique x” means both existence and at most one, so it takes both arguments.

    Here is an example that we will meet again when we study fields and groups. A binary operation ∗ on a collection S is a rule that assigns to each ordered pair a,b of elements of S an element a∗b of S. Addition and multiplication of integers are binary operations on Z. An identity element for ∗ is an element e of S such that e∗a=a and a∗e=a for every a in S.

    Proposition 0.2.6 (Uniqueness of the identity)

    Let ∗ be a binary operation on S. Then ∗ has at most one identity element.

    Idea. Suppose e and e′ are both identities. The only thing we know about them is how they behave in a product, so look at the one product that involves both, e∗e′, and evaluate it in two ways.

    Proof. Suppose e and e′ are both identity elements for ∗. Since e′ is an identity, e∗e′=e, taking a=e in its defining property. Since e is an identity, e∗e′=e′, taking a=e′ in its defining property. Therefore e=e∗e′=e′. This shows that any two identity elements are equal.

    So 0 is the identity for addition on Z, and 1 is the identity for multiplication. The result says nothing about existence. Subtraction on Z has no identity at all: a−e=a for every a forces e=0, but then e−a=−a, which is not a when a=1. Uniqueness proofs of exactly this shape will recur for zero vectors, negatives, inverse matrices, and inverse functions.

    0.2.7. Counterexamples

    By Definition 0.1.4, the negation of “∀x:P(x)” is “∃x:¬P(x)”. So to refute a universal statement, one witness suffices: a single x where P(x) fails. Such an x is a counterexample. Conversely, no number of examples can prove a universal statement over an infinite range.

    Example (Refuting “every prime is odd”)

    Decide whether every prime number is odd. Justify your answer.

    Solution. False. The statement is ∀p prime : p is odd, so by Definition 0.1.4 one prime that is not odd refutes it. Take p=2. It is prime, since 2≥2 and a positive divisor of 2 is at most 2, so it is 1 or 2. It is even, since 2=2⋅1, and hence not odd. So 2 is a counterexample.

    Warning. Many examples prove nothing. The number n2+n+41 is prime for every integer n from 0 to 39. Yet the statement “n2+n+41 is prime for every n≥0” is false: at n=40 we get 402+40+41=1681=412, which is not prime.

    0.2.8. Mathematical induction

    Some statements are really infinitely many statements, one for each natural number: “for every n≥1, 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)/2”. Checking cases one at a time never finishes. Induction replaces the infinite list of checks by two finite tasks, like knocking over a row of dominoes: push the first one, and make sure each domino knocks over the next.

    We take this as a basic property of the integers, not something to prove.

    Theorem 0.2.7 (Principle of Mathematical Induction)

    Let n0 be an integer, and for each integer n≥n0 let P(n) be a statement. Suppose

    • (i) (base case) P(n0) is true, and
    • (ii) (inductive step) for every integer n≥n0, if P(n) is true then P(n+1) is true.

    Then P(n) is true for every integer n≥n0.

    In the inductive step, the assumption “P(n) is true” is called the induction hypothesis. It is not circular: we do not assume the whole conclusion, only that the statement holds at one arbitrary n, and we prove that it then holds at n+1.

    Proposition 0.2.8 (Sum of the first n integers)

    For every integer n≥1, 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)2.

    Idea. Passing from n to n+1 adds exactly one term, n+1, to the left side. So the step reduces to the algebra identity n(n+1)2+(n+1)=(n+1)(n+2)2, which holds after taking out the factor n+1.

    Proof. We prove this by induction on n, using Theorem 0.2.7 with n0=1. Let P(n) be the statement 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)/2.

    Base case. For n=1 the left side is 1 and the right side is 1⋅2/2=1. So P(1) is true.

    Inductive step. Let n≥1 and suppose P(n) is true. Then 1+2+⋯+n+(n+1)=n(n+1)2+(n+1)=(n+1)(n2+1)=(n+1)(n+2)2, where the first equality uses the induction hypothesis P(n). This is P(n+1).

    By Theorem 0.2.7, P(n) is true for every integer n≥1, as claimed.

    The base case does not have to be 1, and the step does not have to be an equation. In this book the natural numbers are N={0,1,2,…}: they include 0. (Some books start at 1; the next section fixes our convention formally.)

    Proposition 0.2.9 (2n>n)

    For every natural number n, we have 2n>n.

    Proof. We prove this by induction on n, with n0=0. For n=0, 20=1>0. Now let n≥0 and suppose 2n>n. Then 2n+1=2n+2n>n+2n≥n+1, where the strict inequality uses the induction hypothesis and the last step uses 2n≥1. Hence 2n+1>n+1. By Theorem 0.2.7, 2n>n for every n∈N.

    The inductive step must work for every n≥n0, including the smallest ones. The following fake proof fails exactly there.

    Warning. The “all horses are the same color” fallacy. Claim: in every group of n≥1 horses, all horses have the same color. “Proof”: for n=1 there is nothing to show. Suppose the claim holds for groups of n horses, and take a group of n+1 horses h1,…,hn+1. The group h1,…,hn is single-colored by the induction hypothesis, and so is h2,…,hn+1. The two groups share a horse, so all n+1 horses have its color. The claim is plainly false, so this “proof” has a gap. Before opening the check below, try to find the exact value of n where the step breaks.

    Quick check. Where does the inductive step in the horse argument fail?

    Solution. It fails when passing from n=1 to n+1=2. The two groups are then {h1} and {h2}, which share no horse, so nothing links the color of h1 to that of h2. The sentence “the two groups share a horse” is true only when n≥2. Since the step P(1)⇒P(2) is not proved, the chain of dominoes breaks at the very first push, and the base case alone gives nothing.

    0.2.9. Strong induction and well-ordering

    In some arguments, knowing P(n) alone is not enough to reach P(n+1): we need the statement at some earlier value, and we do not know in advance which one. Strong induction allows the hypothesis at all earlier values at once.

    Theorem 0.2.10 (Strong induction)

    Let n0 be an integer, and for each integer n≥n0 let P(n) be a statement. Suppose that for every integer n≥n0, if P(k) is true for all integers k with n0≤k<n, then P(n) is true. Then P(n) is true for every integer n≥n0.

    Idea. Ordinary induction applies to the stronger statement Q(n): “P(k) holds for all k from n0 to n”. The hypothesis is exactly what pushes Q(n) to Q(n+1). At n=n0 the condition “for all k with n0≤k<n0” is vacuous, so the hypothesis already contains the base case.

    Proof. For each integer n≥n0, let Q(n) be the statement “P(k) is true for all integers k with n0≤k≤n”. We prove Q(n) for all n≥n0 by Theorem 0.2.7.

    Base case. There is no integer k with n0≤k<n0, so the condition “P(k) is true for all such k” is vacuously true. By the hypothesis with n=n0, P(n0) is true. Hence Q(n0) is true.

    Inductive step. Let n≥n0 and suppose Q(n) is true, so P(k) holds for all k with n0≤k<n+1. By the hypothesis applied to n+1, P(n+1) is true. Together with Q(n), this gives Q(n+1).

    By Theorem 0.2.7, Q(n) holds for all n≥n0. In particular P(n) holds for all n≥n0, since Q(n) includes k=n. This proves the theorem.

    The same property of the natural numbers can be phrased without any statements P(n) at all: there is no endless strictly decreasing sequence of natural numbers, so every search for a smallest example ends.

    Theorem 0.2.11 (Well-Ordering Principle)

    Every nonempty collection of natural numbers has a least element: an element m of the collection with m≤s for every s in the collection.

    We take this as a basic property too. It is equivalent to the Principle of Mathematical Induction, in the sense that each can be proved from the other, but we will not need that. Well-ordering is the natural language for “take a smallest counterexample” arguments, which are contradiction proofs in disguise.

    Here is a result that needs strong induction, because the smaller number we reach is not n−1.

    Proposition 0.2.12 (Existence of a prime factor)

    Every integer n≥2 has a prime divisor.

    Idea. If n is prime, n itself works. If not, n=ab with 1<a<n, and a prime dividing a also divides n. The factor a can be any size below n, so we need the statement for all smaller values, which is strong induction.

    Proof. We use Theorem 0.2.10 with n0=2. Let n≥2, and suppose every integer k with 2≤k<n has a prime divisor.

    Case 1: n is prime. Then n∣n, so n is a prime divisor of n.

    Case 2: n is not prime. Since n≥2, Definition 0.2.2 gives a positive divisor a of n with a≠1 and a≠n. Write n=ab with b an integer. Since a and n are positive, b≥1, and b≠1 because a≠n; so b≥2 and a=n/b<n. Thus 2≤a<n, and by the hypothesis a has a prime divisor p, say a=pc. Then n=p(cb), so p∣n.

    In both cases n has a prime divisor. By Theorem 0.2.10, this holds for every n≥2.

    Now the promised contradiction proof.

    Theorem 0.2.13 (Infinitely many primes)

    There are infinitely many prime numbers.

    Idea. Suppose the primes form a finite list. Build a number that no prime on the list can divide: multiply them all and add 1. Dividing by any pi leaves remainder 1. But Proposition 0.2.12 says this number has some prime divisor, and that prime must be on the list.

    Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes, and list them all as p1,p2,…,pk. Let N=p1p2⋯pk+1. The list is nonempty, since 2 is prime, and each pi≥2; hence N≥3. By Proposition 0.2.12, N has a prime divisor p. Since the list contains every prime, p=pi for some i. Then pi∣N and pi∣p1p2⋯pk, so there are integers s,t with N=pis and p1⋯pk=pit. Subtracting gives 1=pi(s−t). Since s−t is an integer and pi≥2, this is impossible: pi(s−t) is 0 when s=t, and has absolute value at least 2 otherwise. This contradiction shows that there are infinitely many primes.

    Remark. Well-ordering also justifies the “lowest terms” fact used in Theorem 0.2.3. Let x be rational. There is at least one way of writing x=a/b with b≥1: if x=a/b with b<0, then also x=(−a)/(−b) with −b≥1. Among all such ways, the collection of possible denominators b is a nonempty collection of natural numbers, so by Theorem 0.2.11 it has a least element. Take a representation a/b with that least b. If some d≥2 divided both, say a=da′ and b=db′, then x=a′/b′ with 1≤b′<b, contradicting minimality. So a/b is in lowest terms.

    0.2.10. Writing proofs in this book

    The proofs in this book follow a few conventions. Knowing them makes proofs faster to read, and copying them makes your own proofs easier to check.

    • The first sentence does mathematics. Proofs open with “Let …”, “Suppose …”, “Since …”, or “By …”. The motivation has already been given in the Idea block.
    • Every step names its reason. Each line cites a hypothesis, a definition, or an earlier result by name, as in “by Proposition 0.2.2”. When a display hides a reason, a clause such as “where the first equality uses the induction hypothesis” points to it.
    • Structure is marked. Two directions of an iff are labeled (⇒) and (⇐), cases are labeled Case 1 and Case 2, and inductions name their base case and inductive step.
    • No hidden gaps. Words like “obviously” and “clearly” never stand in for an argument. If a step is short, it is written out; if it is routine and has been done before, the proof says so.
    • The ending restates the result: “This shows …”, “This proves …”, “as claimed”.

    How to read a proof here. First read the statement and identify the givens and the goal. Then read the Idea, which says why the argument has the shape it has. Then read the Proof with a pencil. At each line, ask which earlier line or result justifies it; if you cannot answer, that is the line to reread. Finally, ask which hypothesis each step used. If a hypothesis was never used, either it was not needed or you have missed a step.

    0.2.11. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)

    For each statement, name the proof technique you would use first (direct, contrapositive, contradiction, cases, induction, or counterexample), and give a one-sentence reason. Then carry out the proof or refutation.

    • (a) The sum of two odd integers is even.
    • (b) For every integer n, if 3n+2 is odd, then n is odd.
    • (c) For every integer n≥1, 3∣4n−1.
    • (d) For every integer n≥1, n2≥2n.
    • (e) There is no largest integer.

    Solution.

    • (a) Direct. The hypothesis gives formulas to add. Let m=2a+1 and n=2b+1 with a,b integers, by Definition 0.2.1. Then m+n=2(a+b+1), which is even.

    • (b) Contrapositive. The conclusion’s negation “n even” gives a formula, while “3n+2 odd” does not. By Theorem 0.1.1 it suffices to show: if n is even then 3n+2 is even. If n=2k, then 3n+2=2(3k+1), which is even, hence not odd.

    • (c) Induction. It is a statement for every n≥1, and 4n+1−1 is built from 4n−1. For n=1, 4−1=3=3⋅1. If 4n−1=3k, then 4n+1−1=4(4n−1)+3=3(4k+1). By Theorem 0.2.7 the claim holds for all n≥1.

    • (d) Counterexample. The statement is false, so a single witness refutes it. At n=1, n2=1<2=2n.

    • (e) Contradiction. It says an object does not exist, so assume it does. Suppose m is a largest integer. Then m+1 is an integer with m+1>m, contradicting the choice of m. Hence there is no largest integer.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1: Contrapositive)

    Prove that for every integer n, if n2 is odd, then n is odd.

    Solution. Let n be an integer. By Theorem 0.1.1, it suffices to prove that if n is not odd, then n2 is not odd. Suppose n is not odd. Since every integer is even or odd, n is even, so n=2k for some integer k by Definition 0.2.1. Then n2=4k2=2(2k2), so n2 is even. Since no integer is both even and odd, n2 is not odd. This proves the statement.

    Exercise (B2: Induction)

    Prove that for every integer n≥1, the sum of the first n odd numbers is n2: 1+3+5+⋯+(2n−1)=n2.

    Solution. We prove this by induction on n, using Theorem 0.2.7 with n0=1. Let P(n) be the displayed statement.

    Base case. For n=1 the left side is 1 and the right side is 12=1, so P(1) is true.

    Inductive step. Let n≥1 and suppose P(n) is true. The (n+1)-st odd number is 2(n+1)−1=2n+1. Then 1+3+⋯+(2n−1)+(2n+1)=n2+2n+1=(n+1)2, where the first equality uses the induction hypothesis. This is P(n+1).

    By Theorem 0.2.7, P(n) holds for every n≥1.

    Exercise (B3: 3 is irrational)

    You may use the school fact that every integer has exactly one of the forms 3k, 3k+1, 3k+2 with k an integer.

    • (a) Prove that for every integer n, if 3∣n2 then 3∣n.
    • (b) Hence prove that there is no rational number whose square is 3.

    Solution.

    • (a) Let n be an integer. By Theorem 0.1.1, it suffices to show that if 3∤n then 3∤n2. Suppose 3∤n. Then n=3k+1 or n=3k+2 for some integer k.

      Case 1: n=3k+1. Then n2=9k2+6k+1=3(3k2+2k)+1.

      Case 2: n=3k+2. Then n2=9k2+12k+4=3(3k2+4k+1)+1.

      In both cases n2 has the form 3j+1 with j an integer. Since an integer has only one of the three forms, n2 is not of the form 3j, so 3∤n2. This proves (a).

    • (b) Suppose, for a contradiction, that a rational number x satisfies x2=3. Write x=a/b in lowest terms, so b≥1 and no integer d≥2 divides both a and b. Multiplying a2/b2=3 by b2≠0 gives a2=3b2. Therefore 3∣a2, and by (a), 3∣a. Write a=3c. Then 9c2=3b2, so b2=3c2 and 3∣b2. By (a) again, 3∣b. Thus 3 divides both a and b, contradicting lowest terms. Hence no rational number has square 3.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1: Stamps of 4 and 5)
    • (a) Show that each of 12,13,14,15 can be written as 4a+5b with a,b∈N.
    • (b) Prove by strong induction that every integer n≥12 can be written as 4a+5b with a,b∈N.
    • (c) Show that 11 cannot be written in this form, so the bound 12 in (b) cannot be lowered to 11.

    Hint: for n≥16, look at n−4.

    Solution.

    • (a) 12=4⋅3+5⋅0, 13=4⋅2+5⋅1, 14=4⋅1+5⋅2, and 15=4⋅0+5⋅3.

    • (b) We use Theorem 0.2.10 with n0=12. Let n≥12, and suppose every integer k with 12≤k<n can be written as 4a+5b with a,b∈N.

      Case 1: 12≤n≤15. Then n has the required form by (a).

      Case 2: n≥16. Then k=n−4 satisfies 12≤k<n, so by the hypothesis n−4=4a+5b with a,b∈N. Hence n=4(a+1)+5b, and a+1∈N.

      In both cases n has the required form. By Theorem 0.2.10, every integer n≥12 does.

    • (c) Suppose 11=4a+5b with a,b∈N. Since 5b≤11, b∈{0,1,2}. If b=0 then 4a=11; if b=1 then 4a=6; if b=2 then 4a=1. None of 11,6,1 is a multiple of 4, so no such a exists. Hence 11 is not of the form 4a+5b.
    Exercise (C2: Division with remainder)

    Let a and b be integers with b≥1. Let R be the collection of all nonnegative integers of the form a−qb with q an integer.

    • (a) Show that R is nonempty.
    • (b) Let r be the least element of R, which exists by Theorem 0.2.11, and write r=a−qb. Prove that 0≤r<b.
    • (c) Prove that the pair (q,r) is unique: if a=qb+r=q′b+r′ with 0≤r<b and 0≤r′<b, then q=q′ and r=r′.

    Hint for (a): try q=−|a|.

    Solution.

    • (a) Take q=−|a|. Then a−qb=a+|a|b. Since b≥1 and |a|≥0, we have |a|b≥|a|, so a+|a|b≥a+|a|≥0, where the last step uses |a|≥−a. Hence a+|a|b belongs to R, and R is nonempty.

    • (b) By (a) and Theorem 0.2.11, R has a least element r=a−qb with q∈Z. Since r belongs to R, r≥0. Suppose, for a contradiction, that r≥b. Then r−b=a−(q+1)b≥0, and q+1∈Z, so r−b belongs to R. But b≥1 gives r−b<r, contradicting the choice of r as the least element of R. Hence 0≤r<b.

    • (c) Suppose qb+r=q′b+r′ with 0≤r,r′<b. Then r′−r=(q−q′)b, so b∣r′−r. From the bounds, −b<r′−r<b. Suppose q≠q′. Then |q−q′|≥1, so |r′−r|=|q−q′|b≥b, contradicting −b<r′−r<b. Hence q=q′, and then r′−r=0, so r=r′. This proves uniqueness.

    Together, (a)–(c) show that for integers a and b≥1 there are unique integers q,r with a=qb+r and 0≤r<b. With b=2, this is the fact that every integer is even or odd, and not both.
