    18.11  Three Classical Inequalities

    §10 built the basic theory of convex functions. This section spends it on three classical inequalities, each obtained from the one before. Jensen’s inequality for the logarithm is the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality; with two weights it is Young’s inequality; and Young’s inequality, applied coordinate by coordinate, is Hölder’s. Hölder’s inequality then settles two questions the book left open: the triangle inequality for the p-norm, which Chapter 16 §01 recorded without proof, and the dual of the p-norm, which §04 computed only for p=1,2,∞.

    Throughout, the field is R, and “increasing” and “strictly increasing” are meant as in §10.

    18.11.1. The logarithm and real powers

    The first inequality needs a logarithm, and the book has none. It has the exponential: by fact (A1) of Chapter 10 §09, for real s the series ∑isi/i! converges to a real number es, with es+u=eseu, e0=1, and es≥1 for s≥0; and ddtet=et is the 1×1 case of Theorem 10.9.4 (b). We construct the logarithm as its inverse.

    Lemma 18.11.1 (Exponential and Logarithm)
    • (a) es>0 for every real s, and s↦es is strictly increasing and strictly convex on R.
    • (b) For every real y>0 there is exactly one real s with es=y; write s=log⁡y.
    • (c) log:(0,∞)→R is strictly increasing and strictly concave, with log⁡(xy)=log⁡x+log⁡y and log⁡1=0.

    Idea. The exponential is positive and is its own derivative, so it is strictly increasing with a strictly increasing derivative, hence strictly convex. The intermediate value theorem makes it take every positive value, so it has an inverse. Applying that increasing inverse to the convexity inequality of exp turns it into concavity of log.

    Proof.

    • (a) If s≥0 then es≥1>0. If s<0 then e−s≥1, and ese−s=e0=1, so es=1/e−s>0. The derivative es is therefore positive everywhere, and by the linearity of differentiation, fact (A3) of Chapter 10 §09, −exp has the negative derivative −exp. By fact (A6) of Chapter 16’s introduction, −exp is strictly decreasing, so exp is strictly increasing. So g=exp has a strictly increasing derivative, and Lemma 18.10.5 makes it strictly convex.

    • (b) Uniqueness holds because exp is strictly increasing. For existence, note first that exp is continuous: et+h−et=eth⋅(eh−1)/h, and (eh−1)/h→1 is the derivative at 0, so the right side tends to 0. Let y≥1. For s≥0 every partial sum of the series with at least two terms is at least 1+s, so es≥1+s, since a nonstrict inequality survives a limit. Hence ey≥1+y>y, while e0=1≤y. The intermediate value theorem, fact (A5) of Chapter 16’s introduction, gives s∈[0,y] with es=y. If 0<y<1, apply this to 1/y>1 to get es′=1/y; then e−s′=y.

    • (c) If 0<x<y and log⁡x≥log⁡y, then x=elog⁡x≥elog⁡y=y, since exp is increasing, which is false. So log is strictly increasing. Next, elog⁡x+log⁡y=elog⁡xelog⁡y=xy, so log⁡(xy)=log⁡x+log⁡y by uniqueness in (b), and e0=1 gives log⁡1=0. For concavity, let x,y>0 and 0≤t≤1, and put a=log⁡x, b=log⁡y. By (a), eta+(1−t)b≤tea+(1−t)eb=tx+(1−t)y. Applying the increasing function log to both sides gives tlog⁡x+(1−t)log⁡y≤log⁡(tx+(1−t)y). If x≠y and 0<t<1, then a≠b, the first inequality is strict by strict convexity, and log keeps it strict. So −log is strictly convex.

    Real powers. For t>0 and real r put tr=erlog⁡t, and 0r=0 for r>0. For a positive integer r this is the usual power, since erlog⁡t=(elog⁡t)r=tr by es+u=eseu. For t,a,b>0 and real r,u, the rules es+u=eseu and log⁡(ab)=log⁡a+log⁡b, with log⁡(tr)=rlog⁡t from the uniqueness in (b), give trtu=tr+u,(tr)u=tru,(ab)r=arbr,(a/b)r=ar/br. The first three extend to t,a,b≥0, and the last to a≥0, when the exponents r and u are positive: then a zero base makes both sides 0. They do not extend further, since 0r is not defined for r≤0. Finally, for r>0 the function t↦tr is strictly increasing on [0,∞): on (0,∞) it is exp applied to the strictly increasing rlog⁡t, and 0r=0<tr for t>0. This is the meaning of |xi|p and of the exponent 1/p in the p-norm ‖x‖p=(∑i|xi|p)1/p of Chapter 16 §01.

    18.11.2. Arithmetic and geometric means

    Chapter 17 §08 proved Lemma 17.8.6, the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, by pure algebra: an induction that merges a number above the mean with one below it. Concavity of the logarithm gives a second proof, and a stronger statement, with arbitrary weights.

    Theorem 18.11.2 (Weighted Arithmetic–Geometric Mean Inequality)

    Let t1,…,tm≥0, and let w1,…,wm>0 with w1+⋯+wm=1. Then t1w1t2w2⋯tmwm ≤ w1t1+w2t2+⋯+wmtm, with equality if and only if t1=t2=⋯=tm.

    Idea. Take logarithms. The product becomes the weighted average ∑iwilog⁡ti, and the inequality becomes Jensen’s inequality for the concave log. Then exponentiate back.

    Proof. If some tj=0, the left side is 0, since 0wj=0, and the right side is ≥0. Equality then means ∑iwiti=0, which with every wi>0 and ti≥0 forces every ti=0; conversely, if every ti=0, both sides are 0.

    So let every ti>0. By Lemma 18.11.1 (c), −log is strictly convex on (0,∞), so Theorem 18.10.2 gives ∑i=1mwilog⁡ti ≤ log⁡(∑i=1mwiti), with equality only when all ti are equal. Apply the strictly increasing function exp. The right side becomes ∑iwiti, and the left side becomes ∏iewilog⁡ti=∏itiwi, by es+u=eseu and the definition of real powers. A strictly increasing function preserves both the inequality and its strictness, so equality holds exactly when all ti are equal.

    The case of equal weights is Lemma 17.8.6. With wi=1/n, the rules for powers turn the theorem into (t1⋯tn)1/n≤1n∑iti. Since u↦un is strictly increasing on [0,∞), raising both sides to the n-th power gives t1⋯tn≤(1n∑iti)n, with the same equality case. That is Lemma 17.8.6, now with arbitrary weights and seen as Jensen’s inequality for the logarithm.

    With two weights, the theorem becomes an inequality about products that is exactly what Hölder’s inequality needs.

    Lemma 18.11.3 (Young’s Inequality)

    Let p,q>1 with 1p+1q=1. Then for all real a,b≥0, ab ≤ app+bqq, with equality if and only if ap=bq.

    Proof. Apply Theorem 18.11.2 with m=2, t1=ap, t2=bq, w1=1/p, and w2=1/q. The weights are positive and add up to 1, and (ap)1/p=a, (bq)1/q=b by the rules for powers. So the left side of the theorem is ab and the right side is ap/p+bq/q, with equality exactly when ap=bq.

    18.11.3. Hölder’s inequality and the dual p-norms

    Two exponents p,q∈(1,∞) with 1p+1q=1 are called conjugate; equivalently q=p/(p−1). The pair p=q=2 is conjugate to itself. Hölder’s inequality is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with the two factors measured in two different norms.

    Theorem 18.11.4 (Hölder’s Inequality)

    Let p,q∈(1,∞) be conjugate exponents. For all x,y∈Rn, |⟨x,y⟩| ≤ ∑i=1n|xiyi| ≤ ‖x‖p‖y‖q.

    Idea. Both sides of the second inequality scale the same way when x or y is multiplied by a positive number. So normalize to ‖x‖p=‖y‖q=1. Then apply Young’s inequality coordinate by coordinate and add: the right-hand sides add up to 1p+1q=1.

    Proof. The first inequality is the triangle inequality for numbers (Theorem 0.6.4) applied to ∑ixiyi. For the second, if x=0 or y=0 both sides are 0. Otherwise ‖x‖p>0 and ‖y‖q>0; put ai=|xi|/‖x‖p and bi=|yi|/‖y‖q. Then ∑iaip=∑i|xi|p/‖x‖pp=1, because ‖x‖pp=∑i|xi|p by the rules for powers, and likewise ∑ibiq=1. By Lemma 18.11.3 for each i, and adding, ∑i=1naibi ≤ 1p∑i=1naip+1q∑i=1nbiq=1p+1q=1. Multiplying by ‖x‖p‖y‖q gives ∑i|xiyi|≤‖x‖p‖y‖q, as claimed.

    For p=q=2 this is Theorem 11.1.5 for the standard inner product on Rn, reached without the inner product’s geometry. The limiting pair p=1, q=∞ holds too, and more simply: ∑i|xiyi|≤∑i|xi|‖y‖∞.

    Chapter 16 §01 recorded the p-norm for every p≥1, but verified only p=1,2,∞, because the triangle inequality for general p had not been proved. Hölder’s inequality proves it.

    Corollary 18.11.5 (Minkowski’s Inequality)

    Let 1<p<∞. For all x,y∈Rn, ‖x+y‖p≤‖x‖p+‖y‖p. Consequently ‖⋅‖p is a norm on Rn.

    Idea. Split |zi|p=|zi|⋅|zi|p−1 and use the triangle inequality on the first factor. That leaves two sums, and Hölder’s inequality bounds each, with the same second factor (|zi|p−1)i. Dividing by that common factor’s norm leaves the triangle inequality.

    Proof. Let q=p/(p−1) be the conjugate exponent, and put z=x+y. If z=0 there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, by the triangle inequality for numbers in each coordinate (Theorem 0.6.4), ∑i|zi|p=∑i|zi||zi|p−1≤∑i|xi||zi|p−1+∑i|yi||zi|p−1. Apply Theorem 18.11.4 to each sum, with the vector w=(|z1|p−1,…,|zn|p−1) as the second factor. Since (p−1)q=p, ‖w‖q=(∑i|zi|p)1/q=‖z‖pp/q. So ‖z‖pp≤(‖x‖p+‖y‖p)‖z‖pp/q. Divide by ‖z‖pp/q>0, and use p−p/q=p(1−1/q)=1, to get ‖z‖p≤‖x‖p+‖y‖p. This is (N3) of Definition 16.1.1. (N1) holds because ‖x‖p=0 forces every |xi|p=0, and (N2) because (∑i|c|p|xi|p)1/p=|c|‖x‖p by the rules for powers.

    The proof uses the coordinates only through their moduli: the triangle inequality |xi+yi|≤|xi|+|yi|, which Theorem 0.6.4 proves for complex numbers as well, and Hölder’s inequality for the real vectors (|xi|)i, (|yi|)i, and w, whose p-norms are those of x and y. So it works verbatim on Cn, and ‖⋅‖p is a norm on Fn for F=R and for F=C, as Chapter 16 §01 recorded.

    Now the dual norm. Recall from Definition 18.4.4 that the dual norm of ‖⋅‖ at y is the largest value of ⟨x,y⟩ over the unit ball ‖x‖≤1. §04 computed it for p=1,2,∞: the 1-norm and the ∞-norm are dual to each other, and the 2-norm is dual to itself. Hölder’s inequality supplies an upper bound for every other p, and a matching vector shows it is attained.

    Theorem 18.11.6 (The Dual of the p-Norm)

    Let p,q∈(1,∞) be conjugate exponents. On Rn with the standard inner product, the dual norm of ‖⋅‖p is ‖⋅‖q: max{⟨x,y⟩:‖x‖p≤1}=‖y‖q(y∈Rn). For y≠0 the maximum is attained at x with xi=σi|yi|q−1/‖y‖qq−1, where σi=1 if yi≥0 and σi=−1 otherwise.

    Idea. Hölder’s inequality bounds ⟨x,y⟩ by ‖y‖q on the whole unit ball. To show the bound is attained, give xi the sign of yi and make |xi|p proportional to |yi|q, which is when Young’s inequality (Lemma 18.11.3) is an equality in each coordinate. That is the stated witness, and a direct computation checks it.

    Proof. If ‖x‖p≤1, then ⟨x,y⟩≤‖x‖p‖y‖q≤‖y‖q by Theorem 18.11.4. For y=0 the bound 0 is attained at x=0. For y≠0, take x as in the statement. Since (q−1)p=q, ∑i|xi|p=∑i|yi|q‖y‖qq=1, so ‖x‖p=1; and since σiyi=|yi|, ⟨x,y⟩=∑i|yi|q‖y‖qq−1=‖y‖qq‖y‖qq−1=‖y‖q. So the bound is attained, and the largest value is ‖y‖q.

    Example (A dual norm by hand)

    Let y=(3,−4,5)∈R3, and let p=32 and q=3. Compute the dual norm of y with respect to ‖⋅‖3/2, find a vector x with ‖x‖3/2=1 at which it is attained, and compare with the value at the unit vector e3.

    Solution. The exponents are conjugate, since 1p+1q=23+13=1. Next, |3|3+|−4|3+|5|3=27+64+125=216=63, so ‖y‖3=2161/3=6, using (63)1/3=6. By Theorem 18.11.6 the dual norm is 6.

    For the maximizer, q−1=2, so xi=σi|yi|2/62 with σi the sign of yi: x=(936,−1636,2536)=(14,−49,2536). Check: |xi|3/2=(|yi|2/36)3/2=|yi|3/216, giving 18+827+125216=27+64+125216=1. So ‖x‖3/2=1. And ⟨x,y⟩=34+169+12536=27+64+12536=6.

    The unit vector e3 has ‖e3‖3/2=1 and gives ⟨e3,y⟩=5<6. Putting all the weight on the largest coordinate of y is not optimal; the maximizer spreads it in proportion to |yi|2.

    Exchanging the roles of p and q shows that the dual of ‖⋅‖q is ‖⋅‖p, just as Theorem 18.4.8 requires. Together with §04, the dual norm of ‖⋅‖p is now known for every p∈[1,∞]: it is ‖⋅‖q with 1p+1q=1, reading 1∞ as 0. This completes §04’s computation. Chapter 16 measured vectors and matrices but never a linear functional; §04’s dual norm filled that gap, and now it is computed for every p-norm.

    Look back at the chain. One convexity fact, that −log is strictly convex, went through Jensen’s inequality to the weighted arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, through two weights to Young’s inequality, and through normalization to Hölder’s. Hölder’s inequality then did two jobs that earlier chapters could not: it proved that every ‖⋅‖p is a norm, so that its unit ball is one of the convex sets this chapter began with, and it computed the dual of every such norm.

    18.11.4. Summary and transfer

    The chapter began with a word the book had been using without studying it. Convexity turned up in the positive semidefinite cone of Chapter 13, in the unit balls of Chapter 16, and in the Ky Fan sums of Chapter 17. The chapter ends with convexity as a subject: convex sets, the hyperplanes that separate them, the duality between a cone and its dual, extreme points and polytopes, and convex functions. Six moves did the work, and each transfers beyond this chapter.

    • Nearest point, then the normal direction. Project onto a closed convex set; the vector from the projection to the point is the normal of a separating hyperplane (§03, Separating Hyperplanes). This is Chapter 11’s best approximation, with an inequality where the equation was. Transfer: to prove that a point lies outside a closed convex set, and to certify it, look for the nearest point.
    • Count, then drop a point. Too many points in n dimensions are affinely dependent, and moving the weights along the dependence removes one of them without leaving the hull (§02, Carathéodory’s Theorem). Among too many generators of a cone, a linear dependence does the same (§05, Cones and Farkas’s Lemma). Transfer: when a representation uses more pieces than the dimension allows, the surplus is a dependence, and a dependence can be spent.
    • Dualize. A closed convex cone is determined by its dual cone, and every statement about the one has a statement about the other. Farkas’s lemma is this move made into a theorem, linear programming duality (§06, Linear Programming Duality) is Farkas applied to one combined system, and the minimax theorem (§07, Matrix Games and the Minimax Theorem) is a duality statement of the same kind. Transfer: when a feasibility question will not yield, ask for the certificate that it fails.
    • Lift, then eliminate. A hull of finitely many points is the projection of a polyhedron in a bigger space, with the weights as extra coordinates, and Fourier–Motzkin elimination removes those coordinates one at a time while keeping a system of inequalities (§09, Polytopes, Theorem 18.9.5). Transfer: to describe the image of a set given by inequalities, add the hidden variables as coordinates and eliminate them.
    • A supremum of linear functions is convex. Support functions, dual norms, λ1, and the Ky Fan sums are convex for this one reason (§04, Gauges, Support Functions, and Dual Norms, and §10, Convex Functions, Proposition 18.10.3). Its companion: a linear function on a compact convex set peaks at an extreme point (§08, Extreme Points). Transfer: to prove a function convex, write it as a maximum of affine functions.
    • Compactness turns a supremum into a maximum. Once a hull, a ball, or a set of weights is known to be compact, facts (A3) and (A4) of Chapter 16’s introduction produce a nearest point, a maximizer, or an extreme point that can be named and used. Transfer: before optimizing, check that the set is compact; afterwards, feed the maximizer back into the argument.

    §§10–11 added two moves of their own. Second differences: on a segment, convexity is read off from f(z+sv)+f(z−sv)−2f(z) (§10, Theorem 18.10.6). Normalize, then use a two-point inequality: Hölder’s inequality is Young’s inequality after scaling both vectors to norm 1 (§11, Theorem 18.11.4). Transfer: if multiplying any one variable by c>0 multiplies both sides by the same power of c, check the cases with a zero variable directly, and then assume every variable has norm 1.

    The chapter also paid promises made in earlier chapters.

    The promise Made in Paid by
    “Chapter 18 studies convex functions in general, and the observation that a pointwise supremum of linear functions is convex is one of its basic facts” Chapter 17 §06 §10 (Convex Functions), Proposition 18.10.3, with λ1 and the Ky Fan sums as its instances (Corollary 18.10.4)
    Nonnegativity and capacities turn traffic flow “into one about inequalities, which is the subject of linear programming” Chapter 3 §07 §06 (Linear Programming Duality)
    A way to measure a linear functional: Chapter 16 measured vectors and matrices, but never a functional Chapter 16 §04 (Gauges, Support Functions, and Dual Norms): the definition, the double dual, and p=1,2,∞; §11 (Three Classical Inequalities): every 1<p<∞, Theorem 18.11.6
    The triangle inequality for ‖⋅‖p, recorded without proof, “which Chapter 18 proves from Hölder’s inequality” Chapter 16 §01 §11 (Three Classical Inequalities), Corollary 18.11.5

    Some things the chapter did not do. It stated Birkhoff’s theorem, that the extreme points of the doubly stochastic matrices are exactly the permutation matrices, and left its proof to Chapter 19. The convexity of A↦trf(A) for convex f, and the consequences of a majorization for every convex function, belong to Chapter 21. Convex optimization beyond linear programs, where a duality gap can occur, lies outside this chapter.

    18.11.5. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)
    • (a) State Young’s inequality, with its hypotheses and its equality case.
    • (b) Decide whether the following is correct, and justify your answer: the exponent conjugate to p=4 is q=43.
    • (c) Decide whether the following is correct, and justify your answer: (tr)u=tru for every real t≥0 and all real r,u.
    • (d) Decide whether the following is correct, and justify your answer: if p,q are conjugate and ⟨x,y⟩=‖x‖p‖y‖q for x,y∈Rn, then one of x,y is a scalar multiple of the other.
    • (e) Name the convex function to which the proof of Theorem 18.11.2 applies Jensen’s inequality, and say why the equality case needs it to be strictly convex.

    Solution.

    • (a) Let p,q>1 with 1p+1q=1. Then ab≤ap/p+bq/q for all real a,b≥0, with equality if and only if ap=bq (Lemma 18.11.3).

    • (b) Correct. 14+34=1; equivalently q=p/(p−1)=4/3.

    • (c) Incorrect. Take t=0, r=1, and u=−1. Then tr=0, and 0−1 is not defined, since 0r is defined only for r>0; neither side has a value. The rule holds for t>0 and all real r,u, and for t=0 when r,u>0.

    • (d) Incorrect. Take p=3, q=32, x=(1,2), and y=(1,4). Then ⟨x,y⟩=1+8=9, ‖x‖3=(1+8)1/3=91/3, and ‖y‖3/2=(1+43/2)2/3=92/3, since 43/2=8; so ‖x‖3‖y‖3/2=91/3+2/3=9. But neither vector is a multiple of the other, since 1⋅4−2⋅1≠0. Equality asks for |xi|p to be proportional to |yi|q, not x to y (exercise C2).

    • (e) It is −log on (0,∞), which is strictly convex by Lemma 18.11.1 (c). Theorem 18.10.2 proves its equality clause only for strictly convex functions, and the hypothesis is needed: an affine function makes Jensen’s inequality an equality for every choice of points.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1: A dual norm by hand)

    Let y=(1,−6,8)∈R3, and let p=32 and q=3.

    • (a) Check that p and q are conjugate, and compute ‖y‖3.
    • (b) Find x∈R3 with ‖x‖3/2=1 and ⟨x,y⟩=‖y‖3.
    • (c) Hence determine the dual norm of y with respect to ‖⋅‖3/2, and compare it with the value ⟨e3,y⟩ at the unit vector e3.

    Solution.

    • (a) 1p+1q=23+13=1. Next, |1|3+|−6|3+|8|3=1+216+512=729=93, so ‖y‖3=7291/3=9, using (93)1/3=9.

    • (b) Follow Theorem 18.11.6: xi=σi|yi|2/92 with σi the sign of yi, since q−1=2. So x=(181,−3681,6481)=(181,−49,6481). Check: |xi|3/2=(|yi|2/81)3/2=|yi|3/729, giving 1729+216729+512729=1. So ‖x‖3/2=1. And ⟨x,y⟩=181+21681+51281=72981=9.

    • (c) By Theorem 18.11.6 the dual norm is ‖y‖3=9, attained at the x of (b). The unit vector e3 has ‖e3‖3/2=1 and gives ⟨e3,y⟩=8<9: even when one coordinate of y dominates, putting all the weight on it is not optimal.

    Exercise (B2: Harmonic and arithmetic means)

    Let x1,…,xn>0. Prove that n1x1+⋯+1xn ≤ x1+⋯+xnn, with equality if and only if x1=⋯=xn.

    Solution. By Exercise (B1: Which are convex?) (d), g(t)=1/t is strictly convex on (0,∞). Let m=1n(x1+⋯+xn)>0. Theorem 18.10.2 with the weights wi=1/n>0 gives 1m=g(∑i=1n1nxi)≤∑i=1n1ng(xi)=1n(1x1+⋯+1xn), with equality only when all xi are equal. Both sides are positive, and taking reciprocals reverses the inequality: m≥n/(∑i1/xi), which is the claim. If all xi equal c, both sides equal c. So equality holds exactly when the xi are all equal.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1: The exponential above its tangent)
    • (a) Deduce from Exercise (C2: Tangent lines) (a) that ey≥1+y for every real y.
    • (b) Deduce that log⁡x≤x−1 for every real x>0.

    Solution.

    • (a) By Lemma 18.11.1 (a), the function exp is convex and differentiable on the open interval R, with derivative exp. So Exercise (C2: Tangent lines) (a) applies, and at x=0 it gives ey≥e0+e0(y−0)=1+y. For y≥0 this was visible from the series, as in the proof of Lemma 18.11.1 (b); for y<0 it is new.

    • (b) Let x>0 and put y=log⁡x, so that ey=x by Lemma 18.11.1 (b). Part (a) gives x≥1+log⁡x, that is, log⁡x≤x−1.

    Exercise (C2: When Hölder is an equality)

    Let p,q∈(1,∞) be conjugate exponents, and let x,y∈Rn be nonzero.

    • (a) Prove that ∑i|xiyi|=‖x‖p‖y‖q if and only if |xi|p/‖x‖pp=|yi|q/‖y‖qq for every i.
    • (b) Deduce that the maximizer in Theorem 18.11.6 is unique: if y≠0, ‖x‖p≤1, and ⟨x,y⟩=‖y‖q, then x is the vector given there.

    Hint: in (a), look at where the proof of Theorem 18.11.4 adds up the inequalities of Lemma 18.11.3.

    Solution.

    • (a) Put ai=|xi|/‖x‖p and bi=|yi|/‖y‖q, as in the proof of Theorem 18.11.4, so that ∑iaip=∑ibiq=1. Dividing by ‖x‖p‖y‖q>0, the equality ∑i|xiyi|=‖x‖p‖y‖q says ∑iaibi=1=∑i(aip/p+biq/q). By Lemma 18.11.3, aibi≤aip/p+biq/q for each i. A sum of nonnegative differences (aip/p+biq/q)−aibi is 0 exactly when each difference is 0, so equality holds exactly when aibi=aip/p+biq/q for every i, that is, by the equality case of Lemma 18.11.3, when aip=biq for every i. By the rule (a/b)p=ap/bp for real powers, aip=|xi|p/‖x‖pp and biq=|yi|q/‖y‖qq.

    • (b) Let y≠0, ‖x‖p≤1, and ⟨x,y⟩=‖y‖q. By Theorem 18.11.4, ‖y‖q=⟨x,y⟩≤∑i|xiyi|≤‖x‖p‖y‖q≤‖y‖q, so every inequality is an equality. First, ‖x‖p=1, since ‖y‖q>0; in particular x≠0. Second, ∑ixiyi=∑i|xiyi|, and since xiyi≤|xiyi| for each i, every xiyi≥0. Third, by (a), |xi|p=|yi|q/‖y‖qq for every i. Raising to the power 1/p and using q/p=q−1, which follows from 1p+1q=1, gives |xi|=|yi|q−1/‖y‖qq−1. If yi=0, then xi=0, as in the formula. If yi≠0, then xi≠0 and xiyi≥0, so xi has the sign σi of yi. Hence xi=σi|yi|q−1/‖y‖qq−1 for every i, which is the vector of Theorem 18.11.6.
