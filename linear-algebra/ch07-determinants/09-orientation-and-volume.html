    2. 7.9  Orientation and Volume

    7.9  Orientation and Volume

    The chapter began in §1 with a picture: the area of a parallelogram, with a sign attached, obeys a few simple rules, and those rules pinned down ad−bc. We have since built the determinant in every size from the same rules. It is time to return to geometry and say precisely what the determinant measures. There are two halves to the answer. Its absolute value measures volume, and its sign measures orientation, the difference between a left hand and a right hand. Both halves need care. “Volume” in R7 has no meaning until someone defines it, and “orientation” has no meaning at all over C.

    Throughout this section the field is R.

    7.9.1. What should the volume of a parallelepiped be?

    For vectors a1,…,an∈Rn, the parallelepiped they span is the set P(a1,…,an):={t1a1+⋯+tnan:0≤ti≤1 for every i}. For n=2 it is the parallelogram with sides a1,a2 at the origin, and for n=3 a slanted box. For n=1 it is the segment from 0 to a1.

    We would like to say “its volume is |det(a1⋯an)|”. But a theorem needs two sides, and one side is missing: we have no definition of the volume of a subset of Rn. School geometry supplies one for n=2 and n=3 through “base times height”, and the proper general notion, Lebesgue measure, belongs to a course in measure theory. So we proceed honestly in three steps. We define the volume of a parallelepiped by the determinant. We prove that this is the only function obeying the rules that any reasonable notion of volume must obey. And we state, without proof, that it agrees with Lebesgue measure.

    The volume of a parallelepiped is the size of the determinant of its edge vectors.

    Definition 7.9.1 (Volume of a Parallelepiped)

    Let n≥1 and a1,…,an∈Rn. The (n-dimensional) volume of the parallelepiped spanned by a1,…,an is vol(a1,…,an):=|det(a1a2⋯an)| ≥0.

    In words: stack the n edge vectors as the columns of a square matrix, take its determinant, and forget the sign. There must be exactly n vectors in Rn, so that the matrix is square.

    Examples.

    • The unit cube P(e1,…,en) has volume |detIn|=1.
    • A box with edges d1e1,…,dnen has volume |detdiag(d1,…,dn)|=|d1⋯dn| by Theorem 7.4.6: the product of its side lengths, as it should be.
    • The parallelepiped spanned by (1,2,0), (0,1,1), (1,0,3) in R3 has volume |det(101210013)|=|1⋅(3−0)−0+1⋅(2−0)|=5, expanding along the first row.
    • Degenerate case. If a1,…,an are linearly dependent, the parallelepiped is flattened into a proper subspace, and its volume is 0 by Theorem 7.2.1. For n=2, P((1,2),(2,4)) is the segment from (0,0) to (3,6), with area |4−4|=0. This case matters: “volume zero” is the geometric face of “not invertible”.

    Nonexample by minimal change. Drop one vector: (1,2,0) and (0,1,1) span a genuine parallelogram in R3, but the matrix (102101) is 3×2 and has no determinant. The clause that fails is exactly n vectors in Rn. The 3-dimensional volume of that flat parallelogram would be 0, and its area needs a different formula (Exercise (C1: Area of a triangle in space)).

    Why this definition. Reordering the vectors multiplies the determinant by a sign (Theorem 7.2.1), which the absolute value removes, so the volume does not depend on the order in which the edges are listed. The absolute value is also what makes volume nonnegative. The sign we threw away is not garbage: it is the orientation, the subject of the second half of this section.

    Warning. Volume is not linear. Scaling one edge by 2 doubles the volume, but scaling the whole parallelepiped by 2 multiplies it by 2n: vol(2a1,…,2an)=|det(2A)|=2nvol(a1,…,an), where A has columns a1,…,an. A cube of side 2 in R3 has volume 8, not 2; this failure persists even with the sign kept, since det is linear in each edge separately, not in all edges at once. The absolute value also destroys additivity in a single edge: vol(a1+b1,a2,…) is in general not vol(a1,a2,…)+vol(b1,a2,…): with a1=e1, b1=−e1 in R2, and a2=e2, the left side is 0 and the right side is 2.

    7.9.2. Volume is forced by its rules

    Why should anyone accept Definition 7.9.1? Because of what any notion of volume of parallelepipeds must satisfy. The pictures in §1 showed three facts about area, and they have obvious n-dimensional versions: stretching one edge by a factor c multiplies the volume by |c|; sliding one edge parallel to another (a shear) does not change the volume; and the unit cube has volume 1. The next theorem says that these three rules alone determine the volume completely. So any definition that obeys them, whether from school geometry or from measure theory, must agree with ours.

    Theorem 7.9.1 (Volume Is Determined by Its Rules)

    Let n≥1, and let g be a function assigning a real number g(a1,…,an) to every list of n vectors in Rn. Suppose that for every list, every i≠j, and every c∈R:

    • (V1) g(a1,…,cai,…,an)=|c|g(a1,…,ai,…,an) (only slot i changes);
    • (V2) g(a1,…,ai+caj,…,an)=g(a1,…,ai,…,an) (only slot i changes);
    • (V3) g(e1,…,en)=1.

    Then g(a1,…,an)=vol(a1,…,an) for every list. Moreover, vol itself satisfies (V1)–(V3).

    Idea. Think of g as a function of the matrix M with columns a1,…,an. ① If the columns are dependent, shear one column to 0 and scale by 0: g=0. ② Each elementary column operation changes g by the factor |detE|. Scaling and shearing are (V1) and (V2); a swap is not among the rules, but three shears and a sign change perform it. ③ An invertible M is a product of elementary matrices, so peel them off one at a time until only I is left.

    Proof. For M∈Mn(R) with columns a1,…,an, write h(M)=g(a1,…,an).

    Step 1: dependent columns give h(M)=0. Suppose the columns are linearly dependent. By Theorem 1.4.1, some ai=c1a1+⋯+ci−1ai−1 (for i=1, a1=0). Applying (V2) to slot i with the scalars −c1,…,−ci−1, one at a time, does not change g, and it replaces ai by 0 while the other slots stay fixed. Then (V1) with c=0 gives g(…,0,…)=g(…,0⋅0,…)=0⋅g(…,0,…)=0. Hence h(M)=0.

    Step 2: h(ME)=|detE|h(M) for every elementary matrix E. By Theorem 3.4.1 and Theorem 0.9.5, ME=(E⊤M⊤)⊤, and E⊤ is the elementary matrix Pij, Di(c), or I+cEji when E is Pij, Di(c), or I+cEij. So right multiplication by E performs a column operation: swap columns i and j; multiply column i by c≠0; or add c times column i to column j. The determinants are detPij=−1, detDi(c)=c, and det(I+cEij)=1 by Theorem 7.5.1.

    • For E=I+cEij, (V2) gives h(ME)=h(M)=|1|h(M).
    • For E=Di(c), (V1) gives h(ME)=|c|h(M).
    • For E=Pij, watch only slots i and j, holding x and y. By (V2) three times, (x,y)→(x+y,y)→(x+y,−x)→(y,−x) leaves g unchanged, where the second step subtracts slot i from slot j and the third adds slot j to slot i. By (V1) with c=−1 on slot j, g at (y,x) equals |−1| times g at (y,−x). Hence h(MPij)=h(M)=|−1|h(M).

    Step 3: conclusion. If the columns of M are dependent, then h(M)=0 by Step 1 and detM=0 by Theorem 7.2.1, so h(M)=|detM|. Otherwise M is invertible by Theorem 3.4.4, hence M=E1E2⋯Ek is a product of elementary matrices by the same theorem. Applying Step 2 k times, starting from I, and then (V3), h(M)=h(IE1⋯Ek)=|detE1|⋯|detEk|h(I)=|det(E1⋯Ek)|=|detM|, where the third equality uses Theorem 7.5.4 and |xy|=|x||y|. This proves g=vol.

    Finally, vol satisfies (V1) because the determinant is linear in each column (Theorem 7.4.3) and |cd|=|c||d|; (V2) by Theorem 7.2.1; and (V3) because detIn=1.

    Agreement with area and volume from school. For n=2, let g(a1,a2) be the area of the parallelogram P(a1,a2) in the sense of school geometry, base times height. The pictures of §1 are exactly (V1)–(V3) for this g: stretching a side by c stretches the base or the height by |c|; a shear a1↦a1+ca2 slides the opposite side along a line parallel to the base a2, keeping base and height; and the unit square has area 1. Hence, by Theorem 7.9.1, school area equals |det(a1a2)|. For n=3 the same argument works with “area of base times height”. What the theorem cannot supply is the geometric input itself: it takes the three rules as given.

    Agreement with Lebesgue measure. In measure theory, every reasonable subset S⊆Rn has an n-dimensional volume, its Lebesgue measure. One can prove that the Lebesgue measure of P(a1,…,an) satisfies (V1)–(V3), and hence equals vol(a1,…,an). We state this without proof; nothing in this book depends on it.

    Quick check. Which of the rules (V1)–(V3) does the function g(a1,a2)=|det(a1a2)|2 on R2 break?

    Solution. It satisfies (V2), since shears do not change the determinant, and (V3), since 12=1. It breaks (V1): g(2e1,e2)=4, while |2|g(e1,e2)=2. So the theorem does not apply, and indeed g≠vol.

    7.9.3. Linear maps scale volume

    Applying a linear map to a parallelepiped gives another parallelepiped: by linearity, T(∑tiai)=∑tiTai, so T(P(a1,…,an))=P(Ta1,…,Tan). How does the volume change? Multiplicativity answers this in one line, and the answer does not depend on the parallelepiped.

    Theorem 7.9.2 (Linear Maps Scale Volume by |det|)

    Let T∈L(Rn) and a1,…,an∈Rn. Then vol(Ta1,…,Tan)=|detT|⋅vol(a1,…,an). In particular, the image of the unit cube under T has volume |detT|.

    Proof. Let B be the matrix of T in the standard basis, so Tx=Bx for all x (Theorem 4.1.1) and detT=detB (Definition 7.5.1). Let A=(a1⋯an). By Theorem 0.9.2, BA=(Ba1⋯Ban). By Theorem 7.5.4, vol(Ta1,…,Tan)=|det(BA)|=|detB||detA|=|detT|vol(a1,…,an). For the unit cube take ai=ei, with vol(e1,…,en)=1.

    This is the meaning of the determinant of a real operator: the factor by which it scales every volume. An operator with detT=0 squashes Rn into a lower-dimensional subspace, where n-dimensional volume is 0. In multivariable calculus the same fact, applied to the linear approximation of a smooth map, becomes the Jacobian factor |detJ| in the change-of-variables formula for integrals.

    Example (Area of an Image)

    Let T:R2→R2 be T(x,y)=(3x+y, x+2y). Find the area of the image of the unit square, and of the image of the parallelogram P((1,1),(−1,2)).

    Solution. The standard matrix is B=(3112), with detT=6−1=5. By Theorem 7.9.2 the image of the unit square, the parallelogram spanned by (3,1) and (1,2), has area 5. The parallelogram P((1,1),(−1,2)) has area |2+1|=3, so its image has area 5⋅3=15. Check directly: T(1,1)=(4,3) and T(−1,2)=(−1,3), and |det(4−133)|=|12+3|=15.

    7.9.4. Orientation

    Now the sign. In R2, the ordered basis (e1,e2) turns counterclockwise from its first vector to its second, while (e2,e1) turns clockwise. No rotation carries one arrangement to the other: a rotation of the plane keeps counterclockwise turns counterclockwise. The determinant sees the difference: det(e1e2)=1>0 and det(e2e1)=−1<0.

    To compare two bases of an arbitrary real space, we compare them with each other rather than with a fixed standard basis, using the change-of-coordinates matrix of Chapter 4.

    Two bases have the same orientation when the matrix converting one into the other has positive determinant.

    Definition 7.9.2 (Orientation)

    Let V be a vector space over R with dim⁡V=n≥1, and let B and C be ordered bases of V. We say B and C have the same orientation if det[id]BC>0, and opposite orientations if det[id]BC<0.

    In words: write each vector of B in coordinates with respect to C, put the coordinate columns side by side (Definition 4.2.1), and look at the sign of the determinant. Exactly one of the two cases occurs, because [id]BC is invertible (Theorem 4.2.1 (c)), so its determinant is nonzero (Theorem 7.5.2), and a nonzero real number is either positive or negative. The bases must be ordered: reordering a basis can change the answer.

    For V=Rn there is a shortcut. If B and C are the matrices whose columns are the vectors of B and C, then [id]BE=B and [id]CE=C for the standard basis E. By Theorem 4.2.1 (b) and (c), [id]BC=C−1B. Since detCdetC−1=detI=1 (Theorem 7.5.4), det[id]BC=detB/detC. So B and C have the same orientation if and only if detB and detC have the same sign.

    Examples.

    • In R2, (e1,e2) and (e2,e1) have opposite orientations: detI2=1 and det(0110)=−1.
    • In R3, (e1,e2,e3) and (e2,e3,e1) have the same orientation. The second matrix is the permutation matrix of a 3-cycle, whose sign is (−1)3−1=1. A cyclic shift of three vectors is two swaps.
    • In V=R[x]≤1, the bases B=(1,x) and C=(x,1+x) have 1=−x+(1+x) and x=x+0⋅(1+x), so [id]BC=(−1110), with determinant −1. They have opposite orientations. Orientation makes sense in any real space, with no picture at all.
    • Degenerate case, n=1. Bases of R are single nonzero numbers (a), and [id](a)(b)=(a/b). So (a) and (b) have the same orientation exactly when a and b have the same sign. The two orientations of a line are its two directions.

    Nonexample by minimal change. In R2, (−e1,−e2) has the same orientation as (e1,e2): det(−I2)=1, and indeed it is the rotation of (e1,e2) by a half-turn. Change only one sign, to (e1,−e2): the determinant becomes −1, and the orientation flips. What changed is the sign of the determinant, which is the whole content of the definition. In R3, by contrast, −I3 has determinant −1: negating all three vectors reverses orientation.

    Why this definition. Using the change-of-coordinates matrix, rather than the matrices B and C above, makes the definition work in every real space, including spaces like R[x]≤1 that have no standard picture. Requiring det>0 rather than det=1 is what gives a coarse, two-valued answer: rescaling a basis vector by a positive number should not change its handedness.

    The payoff is that “same orientation” really sorts all bases into two families, as the pictures suggest.

    Theorem 7.9.3 (There Are Exactly Two Orientations)

    Let V be a vector space over R with dim⁡V=n≥1.

    • (a) “Having the same orientation” is an equivalence relation on the set of ordered bases of V.
    • (b) It has exactly two equivalence classes. If B=(v1,…,vn), then B−=(−v1,v2,…,vn) lies in the class not containing B.

    Idea. The three axioms are three facts about change-of-coordinates matrices: [id]BB=I; the matrix back is the inverse; and matrices compose along a chain B→C→D. Determinants turn these into 1>0, 1/positive>0, and positive⋅positive>0. For “exactly two”, flipping one vector gives a second class, and “negative times negative is positive” shows there is no third.

    Proof. Write d(B,C)=det[id]BC. By Theorem 4.2.1 (b) and Theorem 7.5.4, for bases B,C,D, (∗)d(B,D)=d(C,D)d(B,C).

    • (a) Reflexive: [id]BB=In, so d(B,B)=1>0. Symmetric: by (∗) with D=B, d(C,B)d(B,C)=d(B,B)=1, so d(C,B)=1/d(B,C), which is positive when d(B,C) is. Transitive: if d(B,C)>0 and d(C,D)>0, then d(B,D)>0 by (∗), since a product of positive reals is positive. By Definition 0.5.2, this is an equivalence relation.

    • (b) At least two classes. B− is a basis, and [id]B−B=diag(−1,1,…,1), because −v1=(−1)v1 and vj=1⋅vj. Its determinant is −1 by Theorem 7.4.6. So d(B−,B)<0, and B− and B lie in different classes.

    At most two classes. Let C and D be bases, neither of which has the same orientation as B. Then d(C,B)<0 and d(D,B)<0, and by symmetry d(B,D)<0 as well. By (∗), d(C,D)=d(B,D)d(C,B) is a product of two negative reals, hence positive. So all bases outside the class of B lie in one class, and there are exactly two classes.

    Choosing one of the two classes is called orienting V; its bases are then called positively oriented, and the others negatively oriented. On Rn the standard choice is the class of (e1,…,en). By the shortcut above, (a1,…,an) is positively oriented if and only if det(a1⋯an)>0. So the determinant of a real square matrix carries two independent pieces of information: its absolute value is a volume, and its sign is an orientation.

    Warning. Orientation needs R. The proof used two facts about the reals: every nonzero number is positive or negative, and products and inverses of positive numbers are positive. Over C there is no such splitting. Suppose we tried “B and C have the same orientation if d(B,C) has positive real part”. In C1, with z=eiπ/3, the bases (e1), (ze1), (z2e1) give d((e1),(ze1))=z−1 and d((ze1),(z2e1))=z−1, both with real part 12>0, but d((e1),(z2e1))=z−2 has real part −12: transitivity fails. Over F2, −1=1, so the second basis B− of the proof is B itself. The theorem holds verbatim over any field with a compatible order, such as Q, but not over C or a finite field.

    Quick check. Do ((1,2),(3,4)) and ((3,4),(1,2)) have the same orientation? Does ((1,2),(3,4)) have the standard orientation of R2?

    Solution. det(1324)=4−6=−2 and det(3142)=6−4=2. The signs differ, so the two bases have opposite orientations. The first has negative determinant, so it is negatively oriented: it is not in the class of (e1,e2).

    7.9.5. The right-hand rule

    In R3, the standard orientation is the one described by the right hand: curl the fingers of the right hand from the first basis vector toward the second, and the thumb points along the third. For (e1,e2,e3) with the usual picture of the axes, this is the familiar arrangement below.

    The cross product of a=(a1,a2,a3) and b=(b1,b2,b3) in R3 is a×b:=(a2b3−a3b2, a3b1−a1b3, a1b2−a2b1). Physics defines it by the right-hand rule. Here is the linear-algebra reason the two descriptions match: (a,b,a×b) is always positively oriented.

    Expand det(aba×b) along its third column (Theorem 7.6.1). The cofactors of that column are C13=a2b3−a3b2, C23=−(a1b3−a3b1) and C33=a1b2−a2b1, which are exactly the three components of a×b. Hence det(aba×b)=(a2b3−a3b2)2+(a3b1−a1b3)2+(a1b2−a2b1)2 ≥ 0. If a and b are linearly independent, the 3×2 matrix (ab) has rank 2, so one of its 2×2 minors, which are ± the components of a×b, is nonzero (Theorem 7.7.1). Then the determinant is strictly positive, so (a,b,a×b) is a basis (Theorem 7.5.2) with the standard orientation. For example, e1×e2=(0,0,1)=e3, matching the picture.

    7.9.6. Orientation-preserving maps

    An invertible operator carries each basis to a basis. Does it keep the orientation? The answer is the same for every basis and depends only on the sign of detT.

    Proposition 7.9.4 (Determinant Sign and Orientation)

    Let V be a vector space over R with dim⁡V=n≥1, let T∈L(V) be invertible, and let B=(v1,…,vn) be any basis of V. Then TB=(Tv1,…,Tvn) is a basis of V, and [id]TBB=[T]BB. Hence B and TB have the same orientation if and only if detT>0, and this does not depend on B.

    Proof. Since T is invertible, it is an isomorphism, so TB is a basis by Theorem 2.5.2. By Definition 4.2.1, column j of [id]TBB is [Tvj]B, which is column j of [T]BB by Definition 4.1.1. Hence the two matrices are equal, and by Definition 7.5.1, det[id]TBB=detT. By Definition 7.9.2 and the symmetry in Theorem 7.9.3 (a), B and TB have the same orientation exactly when detT>0.

    An invertible operator with detT>0 is orientation-preserving, and one with detT<0 is orientation-reversing. Rotations of the plane preserve orientation and reflections reverse it (Exercise (C2: Rotations and reflections)). In R3, the map x↦−x reverses orientation, since det(−I3)=−1: a right hand, pushed through the origin, becomes a left hand.

    7.9.7. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)
    • (a) State the definition of the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by a1,…,an∈Rn, and say what it is when the vectors are dependent.
    • (b) True or false: vol(a1+b,a2,…,an)=vol(a1,…,an)+vol(b,a2,…,an). Justify your answer.
    • (c) Which three rules characterize volume in Theorem 7.9.1?
    • (d) True or false: if detT=−2, then T doubles every volume. Justify your answer.
    • (e) Define “same orientation” for two ordered bases of a real vector space.
    • (f) True or false: for every n≥1, the map x↦−x on Rn reverses orientation. Justify your answer.

    Solution.

    • (a) vol(a1,…,an)=|det(a1⋯an)| (Definition 7.9.1). For dependent vectors it is 0, by Theorem 7.2.1.

    • (b) False. With n=2, a1=e1, b=−e1, a2=e2: the left side is vol(0,e2)=0, the right side is 1+1=2. The absolute value destroys additivity.

    • (c) (V1) scaling one vector by c scales the volume by |c|; (V2) adding a multiple of one vector to another does not change it; (V3) the unit cube has volume 1.

    • (d) True. By Theorem 7.9.2, vol(Ta1,…,Tan)=|−2|vol(a1,…,an). The sign only affects orientation.

    • (e) B and C have the same orientation if det[id]BC>0 (Definition 7.9.2).

    • (f) False. det(−In)=(−1)n, so by Proposition 7.9.4 the map reverses orientation for odd n and preserves it for even n. In R2 it is the rotation by a half-turn.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1: Volume of a parallelepiped)

    Find the volume of the parallelepiped in R3 spanned by (2,1,0), (1,3,1), and (0,1,1). Hence find the volume of its image under the map T(x,y,z)=(x+y, y+z, 2z).

    Solution. Expanding along the first row, det(210131011)=2(3−1)−1⋅(1−0)+0=3, so the volume is 3. The standard matrix of T is (110011002), upper triangular with determinant 2 (Theorem 7.4.6). By Theorem 7.9.2 the image has volume 2⋅3=6.

    Exercise (B2: Comparing orientations)

    In R3, let B=((1,1,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,1)),C=((0,1,0),(1,0,0),(1,1,1)),D=((1,0,1),(1,1,0),(0,1,1)). Determine which pairs have the same orientation, and which are positively oriented. Justify your answer.

    Solution. By the shortcut after Definition 7.9.2, compare signs of the determinants of the matrices with these columns. Expanding along the first row: det(101110011)=1⋅(1−0)−0+1⋅(1−0)=2,det(011101001)=−1, the second by expanding along the last row: 1⋅det(0110)=−1. The list D is B shifted cyclically, (b3,b1,b2); moving the last column to the front takes two column swaps, so by Theorem 7.2.1 its determinant is (−1)2⋅2=2. Hence B and D are positively oriented and have the same orientation; C is negatively oriented, opposite to both.

    Exercise (B3: Areas of images)

    Let T(x,y)=(2x+y, −x+3y) on R2.

    • (a) Find the area of the image of the unit square under T.
    • (b) Find the area of the image of the parallelogram P((1,0),(1,2)), first by Theorem 7.9.2 and then directly.
    • (c) Is T orientation-preserving?

    Solution.

    • (a) The standard matrix (21−13) has determinant 6+1=7, so the image of the unit square has area 7.

    • (b) vol((1,0),(1,2))=|det(1102)|=2, so the image has area 7⋅2=14. Directly, T(1,0)=(2,−1) and T(1,2)=(4,5), and |det(24−15)|=|10+4|=14.

    • (c) Yes: detT=7>0, so by Proposition 7.9.4 T preserves orientation.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1: Area of a triangle in space)

    For u,v∈R3, let A=(uv)∈M3×2(R). We define the area of the parallelogram P(u,v)⊆R3 to be det(A⊤A); Chapter 13 justifies this with inner products, where det(A⊤A) is recognized as a Gram determinant.

    • (a) Show that det(A⊤A)≥0, so the square root exists, and that det(A⊤A)=(u2v3−u3v2)2+(u3v1−u1v3)2+(u1v2−u2v1)2.
    • (b) Show that if u and v lie in the plane z=0, this area equals the area vol((u1,u2),(v1,v2)) of Definition 7.9.1 in R2.
    • (c) Find the area of the triangle with vertices (1,0,0), (0,2,0), (0,0,3).

    Hint: apply Corollary 7.7.3 to A⊤.

    Solution.

    • (a) A⊤∈M2×3(R) and (A⊤)⊤=A, so by Corollary 7.7.3 applied to A⊤, det(A⊤A)=∑|S|=2(det(A⊤)[2],S)2≥0. The 2×2 minors of A⊤=(u1u2u3v1v2v3) are u1v2−u2v1, u1v3−u3v1, u2v3−u3v2 for S={1,2},{1,3},{2,3}; squaring removes the signs, giving the stated sum.

    • (b) If u3=v3=0, two of the three squares vanish, and det(A⊤A)=(u1v2−u2v1)2. Hence det(A⊤A)=|u1v2−u2v1|=|det(u1v1u2v2)|, which is vol((u1,u2),(v1,v2)).

    • (c) The triangle is half of the parallelogram spanned by u=(0,2,0)−(1,0,0)=(−1,2,0) and v=(0,0,3)−(1,0,0)=(−1,0,3). By (a), det(A⊤A)=(6−0)2+(0+3)2+(0+2)2=36+9+4=49. As a check, A⊤A=(51110) has determinant 50−1=49. So the parallelogram has area 7, and the triangle has area 72.

    Exercise (C2: Rotations and reflections)

    For θ∈R, let Rθ=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ) (rotation by θ) and Sθ=(cos⁡2θsin⁡2θsin⁡2θ−cos⁡2θ) (reflection in the line through 0 at angle θ).

    • (a) Show that every rotation preserves orientation and area, and every reflection reverses orientation and preserves area.
    • (b) Show that SθSφ is orientation-preserving for all θ,φ. (It is in fact a rotation, but you need not show this.)
    • (c) Prove that no M∈M2(R) satisfies M2=S0=diag(1,−1). Does the same argument rule out M2=−I2?

    Solution.

    • (a) detRθ=cos2⁡θ+sin2⁡θ=1>0 and detSθ=−cos2⁡2θ−sin2⁡2θ=−1<0. By Proposition 7.9.4, rotations preserve and reflections reverse orientation. Both have |det|=1, so both preserve area by Theorem 7.9.2.

    • (b) By Theorem 7.5.4, det(SθSφ)=(−1)(−1)=1>0.

    • (c) If M2=S0, then (detM)2=detS0=−1 by Theorem 7.5.4, impossible for a real number detM. The argument says nothing about −I2, since det(−I2)=1; and indeed Rπ/22=Rπ/2Rπ/2=(0−110)2=−I2. An orientation-reversing map can never be the square of a real map, since the square of anything preserves orientation.
