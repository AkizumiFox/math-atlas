    Affine and Projective Geometry

    Twenty chapters have studied the vector space, and a vector space has an origin. Geometry does not. Nothing a geometer says — these three points are collinear, these two lines are parallel, this point is the midpoint of that segment — mentions a zero vector, and the sets that geometry actually hands us are usually not subspaces at all. Chapter 3’s solution set of Ax=b with b≠0 is the first example in the book: it misses 0, the sum of two of its points is not one of its points, and yet it is a perfectly good flat piece of space. Something is being described there that the word “subspace” does not reach.

    There are two classical repairs, and this chapter is both of them. Affine geometry deletes the origin and keeps what survives: points, differences of points, flats, parallelism, ratios along a line, and the maps that send lines to lines. Projective geometry goes the other way and adds points — one for every direction — so that two lines in a plane always meet, the dimension formula for a join loses its second case, every quadric in a plane is one of a very short list, and interchanging “point” with “hyperplane” turns every theorem into another theorem.

    Everything here is proved with linear algebra, and mostly with the linear algebra of Chapters 1 to 5. An affine space is a vector space acting freely and transitively on a set; a projective space is the set of lines through the origin of a vector space; a projectivity is an invertible linear map read on those lines; a conic is a quadratic form; a line in space is a wedge of two vectors. The geometry is the payoff, not the method.

    Conventions

    • Points are not bold. P,Q,R are points of an affine space A, and PQ→∈V is the vector from P to Q; vectors follow the book’s rule by type, bold in coordinates, x∈Fn, and plain in an abstract space, v∈V. We write An for affine n-space, the set Fn with its origin deliberately forgotten.
    • A flat is an affine subspace, and A→ is its direction space, a subspace of V. The join A∨B is the smallest flat containing both, the affine counterpart of U+W.
    • P(V) is the projective space of V, of dimension dim⁡V−1, with Pn(F)=P(Fn+1); [x]=[x0:x1:⋯:xn] is the point determined by x≠0. The empty projective space has dimension −1, and that convention is used, not merely tolerated. The projective linear group is PGL(V).
    • The cross-ratio (A,B;C,D) is a point of P1(F), not a scalar. Over a field where the affine chart applies it is the familiar number, and Section 7 gives the formula; keeping it projective is what lets it take the value [1:0] without apology.
    • V∗ is Chapter 5’s dual space and P(V∗) the dual projective space, whose points are the hyperplanes of P(V). Section 8 writes S0 for the subspace of P(V∗) dual to S, built from the annihilator.
    • Z(q) is the zero set of a quadratic form, and “conic” means Z(q) in a projective plane. Section 5’s affine quadric is instead the zero set of a degree-two polynomial x⊤Ax+2b⊤x+c, and carries the (n+1)×(n+1) matrix A~ alongside the small matrix A.

    Which field, and where the characteristic bites

    Sections 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 9 work over an arbitrary field F, with no hypothesis on the characteristic at all. That includes the chapter’s finite-field computations: the four-point affine plane over F2 in Section 1, and the count of Pn(Fq) with the Fano plane in Section 6.

    Characteristic 2 is barred exactly where quadratic forms appear, which is Section 5 throughout and Section 10 up to the Plücker material. The reason is Chapter 14’s: over F2 a quadratic form is not determined by a symmetric matrix, and Section 5 opens by showing that there is then nothing left to classify — every x⊤Ax with A symmetric collapses to the square of a linear form. Section 5 names the hypothesis at each of the three places it does work, and Section 10 states it at the head and lifts it explicitly for the Plücker subsection, which needs no assumption on F. Two smaller restrictions are local and flagged where they occur: Section 2’s centroid needs 3≠0 as well as 2≠0, and over F3 the three medians really are parallel; Section 7’s negative result about n+1 points needs F to have at least three elements, and Section 7 proves that over F2 the negative result is false.

    Three sections are metric, and they are over R with the standard inner product: Section 4 entirely, the second half of Section 5, and Section 3, which inherits Chapter 18’s setting of Rn with the dot product. Everywhere else a distance would be meaningless: a projectivity does not preserve one.

    What the chapter quotes, and what it does not prove

    The chapter imports no analysis. It develops none of its own — no limits, no continuity, no compactness argument — and it adds nothing to the list of analytic facts (A1)–(A6) that Chapter 16 introduced. It uses that list exactly once, at one remove: Section 3’s proof that a bounded polyhedron is the hull of its vertices cites Chapter 16’s Corollary 16.2.5 to supply the compactness hypothesis of Chapter 18’s Theorem 18.8.5. That is the whole of it. Section 3 says so at its head, and uses the words “closed”, “bounded”, and “compact” only inside the results it cites.

    Topology is likewise absent. P2(R) is a famous topological object, and a reader who has met it will expect the words “compact” and “nonorientable”; Section 6 says plainly that this chapter does not use them, because every argument here is a statement about subspaces of a vector space, valid over any field, including finite ones where topology has nothing to say.

    Six things are quoted and not proved. Each is flagged again where it appears, and nothing in the chapter rests on any of them.

    • Section 9 — the coordinatization theory of axiomatic projective planes: that Desargues’s theorem holds in a plane exactly when the plane comes from a division ring, that Pappus’s theorem holds exactly when that ring is commutative, that Pappus therefore implies Desargues, and that non-Desarguesian planes exist. This book has built neither division rings nor synthetic planes, and “projective plane” has meant P(V) throughout. Section 9 states these with credit in one remark and proves Desargues and Pappus itself, over an arbitrary field, by linear algebra.
    • Section 8 — the duality principle is claimed as a working method, not a meta-theorem. What Section 8 proves is a list of five equivalences about the correspondence S↦S0; to turn “every incidence statement has a dual” into a theorem one has to define a formal language and induct on it, which this book has not set up. So every dual statement the chapter uses is proved individually, and Section 8 says this in as many words.
    • Section 4 — the classification of motions is proved in full for R2 and R3, both the direct and the opposite cases. The general shape in Rn — a translation along a flat together with mutually orthogonal rotation planes — is described in a warning and is not proved; the warning says so, and gives Rπ/2⊕Rπ/3 in R4 as the witness that the three-dimensional list does not extend.
    • Section 3 — that a dual variable is the exact rate at which the optimal value gains when a constraint is loosened. Section 3 proves the inequality, that yi bounds the gain, and then exhibits a two-row program where the exact reading fails. It also declines to develop the finite description of an unbounded polyhedron as a polytope plus a cone, pointing at Chapter 18’s exercise instead.
    • Section 10 — which quadric the Klein quadric is. The main text records that it is smooth, with inertia (3,3,0) over R; the Gram matrix that shows this is computed in Exercise (C2: The Klein quadric), and nothing in the section rests on the answer.
    • Section 10 — which proportionality factors turn the parametrization of the circle into primitive Pythagorean triples. That is arithmetic about parities and common factors, not linear algebra, and Section 10 says so where it stops.

    Two exercises from earlier chapters are cited, both for credit only: Chapter 15 §10’s Exercise (C1: The Plücker relation), which reaches the Plücker relation by the ω∧ω route, and Chapter 18 §09’s Exercise (C1: Unbounded polyhedra). Section 10 proves the Plücker relation in both directions from results in the text, and its proof needs no hypothesis on the characteristic, which the exercise’s route does. No theorem here rests on an exercise.

    One debt is paid. Chapter 15 §08 hinted at Plücker coordinates without naming the chapter that would build them; Section 10 does.

    Roadmap

    • Affine Spaces and Affine Maps. A set on which a vector space acts freely and transitively: differences of points are vectors, sums of points are meaningless, and Chasles’s relation is the one law. Choosing an origin identifies the space with V, and the identification is canonical only after the choice. Affine combinations are exactly the combinations whose coefficients sum to 1, and no other coefficient sum survives a change of origin. An affine map is a linear map plus a translation, for a unique linear part, and the whole affine group fits in one (n+1)×(n+1) block matrix — the device the rest of the chapter computes with.
    • Flats, Independence, and Coordinates. Flats are the cosets, parallelism is an equivalence relation on flats of equal dimension, and two flats either meet or lie in two distinct parallel hyperplanes. The dimension of a join has two cases: the subspace formula when they meet, and a corrected formula with a +1 when they miss, in which the intersection of directions takes over. No convention for dim⁡∅ merges the two. Then affine frames, barycentric coordinates, and an affine map pinned down by its values on an affine frame of n+1 points; on any set lying inside a proper flat — in particular on any n points — two distinct affine maps agree. The medians of a triangle meet at the centroid, in one line of barycentric coordinates — and over F3 they do not meet at all.
    • Convex Polyhedra, Geometrically. A reading of Chapter 18, not a reproof: it says so in its first paragraph. A face is what a supporting hyperplane cuts out; faces are polyhedra and there are finitely many; vertex, extreme point, and basic feasible solution are three names for one thing. The face lattices of a cube and of a simplex are computed in full. A linear program is a family of parallel hyperplanes sweeping across a solid, the optimal set is a whole face, and the dual variables are the nonnegative weights expressing the objective as a combination of the active outer normals. Negative cases: an unbounded slab with no vertex at all, whose optimum is nonetheless attained on a face that contains none, and a quadrant that has a vertex and no optimum. No algorithm appears here or anywhere in this book; Chapter 24 is where algorithms live.
    • Euclidean Motions. Distance alone forces everything: a map of Rn preserving distances is affine with orthogonal linear part, and surjectivity is not assumed — it is proved. Polarization is the move, and the Euclidean norm is doing real work, as a distance-preserving nonaffine map for the maximum norm shows. Motions form a group with the translations normal in it and the orthogonal group as the quotient’s content, recorded without quotient groups, which this book has not built. Then both classifications in full: in the plane, translation, rotation, reflection, glide reflection; in space, translation, rotation, screw, reflection, glide, rotatory reflection, told apart by the fixed set. From R4 on the list breaks, and the section says so.
    • Affine and Euclidean Quadrics. A quadric is a quadratic form that has forgotten where the origin is. An affine substitution acts on the (n+1)×(n+1) matrix by congruence, a center exists exactly when the linear vector lies in the column space of the small matrix, and the parabola is precisely the centerless case. Over R every quadric is affinely equivalent to exactly one of three families, separated by the pair of inertias; for n=2 that is nine normal forms, two of which have empty zero sets and are still inequivalent, and for n=3 it is seventeen. Allowing only motions instead keeps the eigenvalues, and they are the reciprocal squared semi-axes — the Rayleigh quotient read on an ellipsoid.
    • Projective Space. Stop working with the points of a vector space and start working with its lines through the origin. The affine chart splits Pn(F) into a copy of An and a hyperplane at infinity of directions, and the two parallel lines of the opening now meet, at a point whose coordinates are computed. The dimension formula dim⁡(S∨T)+dim⁡(S∩T)=dim⁡S+dim⁡T has no exceptional case, which is the whole reason for the construction; two distinct lines of a projective plane meet in exactly one point. Finite fields give |Pn(Fq)|=(qn+1−1)/(q−1), and the Fano plane is drawn and checked.
    • Projective Transformations. An invertible linear map descends to P(V), and two of them give the same projectivity exactly when they differ by a nonzero scalar — so PGL(V) is GL(V) modulo scalars, said without quotient groups. The fundamental theorem: n+2 points in general position can be carried to any other such list by exactly one projectivity. n+1 points are not enough, and the explicit failure is a diagonal matrix; over F2, and only there, n+1 points do suffice, which is proved. Then the cross-ratio, invariant under projectivities and a complete invariant of four points on a line, computed exactly; perspectivities; and the sentence that ties the chapter together — affine maps are exactly the projectivities fixing the hyperplane at infinity as a set.
    • Duality. A point of P(V∗) is a hyperplane of P(V). The annihilator gives an inclusion-reversing bijection S↦S0 between the projective subspaces of the two spaces, with dim⁡S0=n−1−dim⁡S, exchanging joins with meets and the two extreme subspaces with each other. Dualizing twice returns the original. The duality principle is then stated as five equivalences and used as a recipe, each application being its own proof; “two distinct points lie on a unique line” dualizes to “two distinct lines meet in a unique point”, and the projective dimension formula turns out to be self-dual. The trap is spelled out: the dual of “point” is “hyperplane”, and in P3 two lines need not meet.
    • Desargues and Pappus. Two triangles are perspective from a point if and only if they are perspective from a line, proved inside P2 by choosing representatives so that the hypothesis reads a′=a+o, with every scaling step verified — that is where such proofs go wrong. The converse costs no second theorem: the same lemma, applied to a pair of auxiliary triangles built out of the same picture. Pappus follows in coordinates, with the frame chosen by the previous section’s fundamental theorem to make the computation short, and the degenerate configurations excluded by hypothesis. Both hold over every field, and the remark that closes the section records, with credit and without proof, that Desargues means “division ring” and Pappus means “commutative”.
    • Conics and Lines in Space. Over an algebraically closed field a projective quadric is classified by rank alone; over R by rank and inertia up to sign, giving five conics in P2(R): empty, a point, a line, two lines, a smooth conic. Five points in general position lie on exactly one conic, and the independence of the five conditions is proved, not assumed. A smooth conic with one point is in bijection with P1 by projection from that point, which turns one Pythagorean triple into all of them. Pole, polar, and the dual conic use Section 8’s duality. Finally Plücker coordinates, over any field: a line of P3 is a point of P5, the six coordinates satisfy p01p23−p02p13+p03p12=0, and every point of that Klein quadric is a line — both directions proved. A summary closes the chapter.

    Named moves

    • Choose an origin, then say that you chose it. An affine space becomes a vector space the moment a point is picked, and every affine statement is then a linear statement about the difference vectors. The discipline is to record which conclusions survive a different choice: those are the affine ones, and the coefficient sum 1 is exactly the test.
    • Add a coordinate to linearize. An affine map of Fn is not linear, but x↦(1,x) — Section 1 writes the extra coordinate last instead — makes it the restriction of a linear map of Fn+1, with matrix (10⊤bM). The same extra coordinate is what turns a quadric polynomial into a quadratic form and an affine space into a chart of a projective space. Three of this chapter’s theorems are that one observation in three costumes.
    • Pass from points to lines through the origin. Scaling is the enemy of a well-defined point and the friend of a well-defined condition: q(λx)=λ2q(x) vanishes when q(x) does. Anything homogeneous descends to P(V), which is why every object in the second half of the chapter is the solution set of homogeneous polynomial equations.
    • Choose the representatives so the hypothesis becomes an equation. A projective point is a whole line of vectors, and the content of most projective proofs is the scaling chosen at the start: a′=a+o in Desargues, a normalized frame in the fundamental theorem, the second intersection point in the conic parametrization. State out loud which scalings remain free afterwards.
    • Split the translation and ask what I−Q can absorb. To classify a map x↦Qx+b, decompose b along the kernel of I−Q and its complement. The part in the image is absorbed by moving the fixed set; the part in the kernel survives and is the glide. That single question produces both of Section 4’s classification theorems.
    • Read an old theorem geometrically instead of proving a new one. Section 3 adds no convexity theorem to Chapter 18. It renames the objects — face, vertex, edge, facet — and the theorems become statements about a solid with flat sides. Knowing when a chapter is a translation rather than a construction is worth as much as the construction.
    • Count the conditions, then prove they are independent. Five points impose five linear conditions on a 6-dimensional space of forms, so a conic exists by counting alone. Uniqueness is the real theorem, and it is where general position earns its keep: a second independent solution would let us push a conic through a sixth point on one of the lines, a conic meeting a line three times contains it, so it splits into two lines, and the second line then carries three of the original five. The count is cheap; the independence is the content.
    • Dualize by the dimension formula, never by the words. The dual of a d-dimensional subspace has dimension n−1−d. Compute that number before translating any statement: in a plane it sends points to lines, but in P3 it sends points to planes and lines to lines, and translating “point” to “line” there produces a false theorem.
