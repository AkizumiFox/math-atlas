    23.5  Burnside and the Double Commutant

    Twice already the book has computed a commutant: Chapter 10 §04 found the matrices commuting with a basic Weyr matrix, and Chapter 12 §10 found the matrices commuting with a block diagonal matrix whose blocks have separated spectra. Both times the question was given one operator, who commutes with it? This section turns the question round. Given a whole algebra of operators, how much of the algebra can be read off from its commutant, and when is the algebra as large as it could possibly be? Two theorems answer this. Burnside’s theorem says that over an algebraically closed field an algebra with no invariant subspace to hide in must be everything; the double commutant theorem says that a semisimple algebra is exactly the set of operators commuting with everything that commutes with it.

    Throughout, V is a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F, and End(V) is the space L(V) of operators regarded as an algebra under composition. A subalgebra A⊆End(V) contains idV by (SA3) of Definition 23.1.1; this is not decoration, and the warning after Theorem 23.5.5 shows what goes wrong without it.

    23.5.1. Commutants, and when an operator is a scalar

    For a subalgebra A⊆End(V), Definition 23.1.6 sets A′={t∈End(V):ta=at for all a∈A}, and Proposition 23.1.4 says A′ is again a subalgebra containing idV.

    Applying the prime twice gives the double commutant A″:=(A′)′. One inclusion is free and worth recording at once: by Proposition 23.1.4 (c), A⊆A″. The content of this section is that the reverse inclusion holds under a hypothesis we can name exactly.

    Both theorems below end by proving that some operator is a scalar, and §01 has already supplied the fact that lets us conclude this. By Proposition 23.1.5 the center of Mn(F) consists of the scalar matrices or, in the operator form recorded there, for a finite-dimensional V≠{0} the operators commuting with every operator on V are exactly the scalar multiples of idV: End(V)′={λidV:λ∈F}. Nothing new is proved here; it is the operator form that the proofs below call on.

    23.5.2. Algebras that act irreducibly

    Recall from §03 that V is irreducible as an A-space (§03 calls such a space simple, Definition 23.3.3) when V≠{0} and the only A-invariant subspaces of V are {0} and V. The following one-line consequence is used in every proof of this section, so we isolate it.

    Lemma 23.5.1 (An Irreducible Algebra Moves One Vector Everywhere)

    Let A⊆End(V) be a subalgebra with idV∈A, acting irreducibly on V. Then Av:={av:a∈A}=V for every v≠0.

    Proof. The set Av is the image of the linear map A→V, a↦av, hence a subspace. It is A-invariant: b(av)=(ba)v∈Av for b∈A, since ba∈A. It contains idVv=v≠0, so it is not {0}. Irreducibility leaves only Av=V.

    Warning. The assumption idV∈A is doing real work here. Take V=F, a line, and A={0}, a subspace closed under composition but not a subalgebra, since it does not contain idV. The only subspaces of V at all are {0} and V, so A satisfies the letter of irreducibility, yet Av={0}≠V and A≠End(V)=F. Every statement below about an algebra acting irreducibly assumes the identity belongs to the algebra.

    23.5.3. Burnside’s theorem

    Here is the question. An algebra A⊆End(V) that acts irreducibly has nowhere to hide: no proper nonzero subspace survives it. Does that force A to be all of End(V)? Over C the answer is yes. The proof is worth the trouble: the corollary below, which says that an irreducibly acting set of operators already spans End(V), is what the representation theory later in this chapter uses to turn statements about a group into statements about all matrices.

    Theorem 23.5.2 (Burnside’s Theorem)

    Let F be an algebraically closed field, that is, one in which every nonconstant polynomial in F[x] has a root. Let V be a vector space over F with 1≤dim⁡V<∞, and let A⊆End(V) be a subalgebra with idV∈A. If V is irreducible as an A-space, then A=End(V).

    Idea. Step roadmap. ① Among the nonzero members of A, let r be the smallest rank, attained at a. ② Show r=1. If r≥2, the image ima has two independent vectors au1,au2, and irreducibility supplies b∈A carrying au1 to u2. The operator ab maps ima into itself; an eigenvalue λ of that restriction — this is the one place algebraic closure is spent — makes aba−λa a member of A of strictly smaller rank, hence zero, and then au2=λau1 contradicts independence. ③ One rank-one operator v0φ0 in A gives all of them: multiplying on the left moves v0 anywhere, and multiplying on the right moves φ0 anywhere. ④ Rank-one operators span End(V).

    Proof. Write vφ for the operator x↦φ(x)v, where v∈V and φ∈V∗; it has rank 1 exactly when v≠0 and φ≠0, and every operator of rank at most 1 has this form, since an operator with image span(v), v≠0, sends each x to a unique multiple φ(x)v, and φ so defined is linear.

    Step 1: a member of least rank. Since idV∈A and dim⁡V≥1, the set {a∈A:a≠0} is nonempty. Let r=min{ranka:a∈A, a≠0}≥1, and fix a∈A with a≠0 and ranka=r.

    Step 2: r=1. Suppose r≥2. Then dim⁡ima≥2, so there are u1,u2∈V with au1 and au2 independent; in particular au1≠0. By Lemma 23.5.1 there is b∈A with b(au1)=u2.

    Put W=ima, a subspace of dimension r. Then ab(W)⊆ima=W, so s:=(ab)|W is an operator on W. Its characteristic polynomial ps is monic of degree r≥1, hence nonconstant, hence has a root λ∈F because F is algebraically closed; by Theorem 9.2.1, λ is an eigenvalue of s, so ker(s−λidW)≠{0}.

    Consider c:=aba−λa, which lies in A because A is closed under products and linear combinations. For every x∈V, cx=(ab−λidV)(ax)=(s−λidW)(ax), the second equality because ax∈W. Hence imc=(s−λidW)(W), and Theorem 2.3.1 applied to s−λidW on W gives rankc=r−dim⁡ker(s−λidW)≤r−1. By the minimality of r, an element of A of rank less than r must be 0, so c=0, that is, aba=λa. Applying both sides to u1 and using bau1=u2, au2=(aba)u1=λau1, which contradicts the independence of au1 and au2. Therefore r=1.

    Step 3: A contains every operator of rank at most one. By Step 2 there is a0∈A with ranka0=1; write a0=v0φ0 with v0≠0 and φ0≠0.

    Left multiplication. For b∈A and x∈V we get ba0x=φ0(x)bv0, that is, ba0=(bv0)φ0. By Lemma 23.5.1, Av0=V, so vφ0∈Afor every v∈V.

    Right multiplication. For c∈A we get a0c=v0(φ0∘c). Put S={φ0∘c:c∈A}⊆V∗, the image of the linear map A→V∗, c↦φ0∘c, and hence a subspace of V∗.

    Claim. S=V∗.

    Proof. Let U={x∈V:φ(x)=0 for every φ∈S}, a subspace of V. It is A-invariant: if x∈U and d∈A, then for every c∈A we have cd∈A and so φ0(c(dx))=φ0((cd)x)=0, whence dx∈U. It is not all of V: taking c=idV shows φ0∈S, and φ0≠0 gives some x with φ0(x)≠0. Irreducibility therefore forces U={0}.

    Let d=dim⁡S and pick a basis φ1,…,φd of S. The linear map Θ:V→Fd, Θ(x)=(φ1(x),…,φd(x)), has kernel exactly U={0}, since a vector killed by a basis of S is killed by all of S. So Θ is injective and Theorem 2.3.1 gives dim⁡V≤d. On the other hand S is a subspace of V∗, and dim⁡V∗=dim⁡V by Corollary 5.1.4, so d≤dim⁡V. Hence d=dim⁡V∗ and S=V∗ by Theorem 1.6.8.

    Now let v∈V and φ∈V∗ be arbitrary. Choose c∈A with φ=φ0∘c, which the Claim allows, and b∈A with bv0=v, which Lemma 23.5.1 allows when v≠0 (and for v=0 take b=0). Then for every x, (ba0c)x=b(φ0(cx)v0)=φ(x)v, so vφ=ba0c∈A.

    Step 4: rank-one operators span. Let t∈End(V), let (v1,…,vn) be a basis of V and (φ1,…,φn) its dual basis (Definition 5.1.3). The operator ∑i=1n(tvi)φi sends vj to tvj for each j, because φi(vj)=δij; two operators agreeing on a basis are equal, so t=∑i(tvi)φi. Each summand lies in A by Step 3, and A is a subspace, so t∈A. This proves A=End(V).

    The theorem is stronger than it may look. It says that irreducibility, a statement about subspaces, forces a statement about dimension: over an algebraically closed field, an irreducibly acting subalgebra of End(V) has dimension exactly (dim⁡V)2, the largest possible. There is no room between “acts irreducibly” and “is everything”.

    The version about sets rather than algebras is worth recording alongside it, since a group of invertible operators is a set closed under composition and not a subspace.

    Corollary 23.5.3 (An Irreducible Multiplicative Set Spans the Whole Algebra)

    Let F be algebraically closed, V a vector space over F with 1≤dim⁡V<∞, and let S⊆End(V) be a set with idV∈S that is closed under composition. If the only subspaces U⊆V with s(U)⊆U for all s∈S are {0} and V, then span(S)=End(V).

    Proof. Put A=span(S). It is a subspace containing idV, and it is closed under multiplication: a product of two linear combinations of members of S expands, by bilinearity of composition, into a linear combination of products of members of S, which lie in S. So A is a subalgebra. A subspace U is A-invariant if and only if it is invariant under every member of S, since invariance is preserved by linear combinations. So V is irreducible as an A-space, and Theorem 23.5.2 gives A=End(V).

    Warning. Burnside is false over a field that is not algebraically closed. Let F=R, V=R2 and A={(x−yyx):x,y∈R}, the span of I2 and Q=(0−110). Since Q2=−I2, this is a subalgebra, and it acts irreducibly: an invariant line would be spanned by a real eigenvector of Q, and pQ=x2+1 has no real root. Yet dim⁡A=2, not 4, so A≠M2(R). This is the same witness §04 used against the second half of Schur’s lemma (Theorem 23.4.1), and the reason is the same: an operator on a real space need have no eigenvalue, and Step 2 of the proof needs one.

    Quick check. Let A⊆M2(C) be the subalgebra generated by X=(0110) and Z=(100−1). Without computing products, what must dim⁡A be, and why?

    Solution. It must be 4, so A=M2(C). A one-dimensional invariant subspace would be a common eigenvector of X and Z. The eigenvectors of Z are the multiples of e1 and of e2, and Xe1=e2 is not a multiple of e1, nor is Xe2=e1 a multiple of e2. So A acts irreducibly on C2, and Theorem 23.5.2 gives A=M2(C). Directly: I2,X,Z and XZ=(0−110) are independent, which confirms it.

    23.5.4. The double commutant theorem

    Burnside handles the irreducible case. The general case is not “A is everything” — a diagonal algebra is not everything — but a statement about how faithfully A is recorded by its commutant. The right hypothesis is semisimplicity of the action, and it cannot be weakened to nothing, as the warning below shows.

    The engine is a single step: an operator in A″ can be matched by a member of A on one given vector.

    Lemma 23.5.4 (Matching on One Vector)

    Let W be a finite-dimensional vector space over F and let B⊆End(W) be a subalgebra with idW∈B which acts semisimply on W: every B-invariant subspace of W has a B-invariant complement (Definition 23.3.4). Then for every t∈B″ and every w∈W there is b∈B with bw=tw.

    Idea. The subspace Bw is invariant, so it has an invariant complement, and the projection p onto Bw along that complement commutes with B. Being in B″, the operator t commutes with p, hence cannot move w out of the image of p, which is Bw.

    Proof. Put U=Bw={bw:b∈B}, a subspace of W (the image of b↦bw) which is B-invariant, since b1(bw)=(b1b)w for b1∈B. By hypothesis there is a B-invariant subspace U1 with W=U⊕U1. Let p∈End(W) be the projection onto U along U1 (Theorem 4.5.1 (b)).

    p∈B′. Indeed, let b∈B and write x=u+u1 with u∈U, u1∈U1. Then bu∈U and bu1∈U1 by invariance, so bx=bu+bu1 is the decomposition of bx, giving p(bx)=bu=b(px).

    Since t∈B″ and p∈B′, we have tp=pt. Also w=idWw∈Bw=U, so pw=w. Therefore tw=t(pw)=p(tw)∈imp=U=Bw, so tw=bw for some b∈B.

    Matching on one vector is not yet matching everywhere. The trick that upgrades it is to run the lemma not on V but on n copies of V at once, where one cleverly chosen vector carries a whole basis.

    Theorem 23.5.5 (Double Commutant Theorem)

    Let F be any field, let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over F, and let A⊆End(V) be a subalgebra with idV∈A. Assume that A acts semisimply on V: every A-invariant subspace of V has an A-invariant complement (Definition 23.3.4). Then A″=A.

    Idea. Step roadmap. ① Lemma 23.5.4 matches t∈A″ with some a∈A on one vector. ② To match on a whole basis at once, let A act diagonally on Vn=V⊕⋯⊕V (n=dim⁡V) and apply the lemma there to the single vector whose n entries are the basis vectors. ③ For that we must check two things about the copied algebra A~: that it still acts semisimply, which Theorem 23.3.3 supplies, and that t~ lies in A~″, which is a computation with the n×n array of components of an operator on Vn.

    Proof. The inclusion A⊆A″ was noted above, so only A″⊆A needs proof. Let t∈A″, let n=dim⁡V; if n=0 there is nothing to prove, so let n≥1.

    Step 1: the copied algebra. Let Vn=V⊕⋯⊕V be the product of n copies of V (Definition 4.4.1), and for s∈End(V) let s~∈End(Vn) be given by s~(x1,…,xn)=(sx1,…,sxn). The map s↦s~ is linear and satisfies s1s2~=s~1s~2 and idV~=idVn, so A~={a~:a∈A} is a subalgebra of End(Vn) containing the identity.

    Step 2: A~ acts semisimply on Vn. Since A acts semisimply on V, Theorem 23.3.3 writes V=W1+⋯+Wq with each Wj an irreducible A-invariant subspace. For 1≤k≤n let ιk:V→Vn place a vector in the k-th slot and 0 elsewhere; it is injective and satisfies ιk(sx)=s~ιk(x). Hence ιk(Wj) is A~-invariant, and it is irreducible, because ιk carries the A-invariant subspaces of Wj bijectively onto the A~-invariant subspaces of ιk(Wj). Every element of Vn is the sum of its n slots, so Vn=∑k=1n∑j=1qιk(Wj), a sum of irreducible A~-invariant subspaces. By the converse half of Theorem 23.3.3, A~ acts semisimply on Vn.

    Step 3: t~∈A~″. Let πk:Vn→V be the k-th coordinate map. Every s∈End(Vn) is determined by its components skl=πk∘s∘ιl∈End(V), through πk(sx)=∑lsklxl. Comparing components in sa~=a~s gives (sa~)kl=skla,(a~s)kl=askl, so s∈A~′ if and only if every skl lies in A′. Now let s∈A~′. Then (t~s)kl=tskl and (st~)kl=sklt, and these agree because skl∈A′ and t∈A″. Hence t~ commutes with every s∈A~′, that is, t~∈A~″.

    Step 4: conclude. Let (v1,…,vn) be a basis of V and put v~=(v1,…,vn)∈Vn. By Steps 1–3 the hypotheses of Lemma 23.5.4 hold for B=A~ on W=Vn, with t~∈A~″. So there is a∈A with a~v~=t~v~, that is, avi=tvi for every i. Two operators agreeing on a basis are equal, so t=a∈A. This proves A″⊆A, and with the reverse inclusion, A″=A.

    Notice which hypotheses were used where. Algebraic closure was not used: the double commutant theorem holds over every field, unlike Burnside. Finite dimension was used for the basis in Step 4 and inside Theorem 23.3.3. Semisimplicity was used exactly once, to produce the invariant complement in Lemma 23.5.4.

    Warning. Neither hypothesis can simply be dropped.

    Without the identity. Take A={0}⊆End(F2), a subspace closed under composition but with no identity element of End(F2) in it. Every subspace of F2 is A-invariant and every subspace has a complement, so the condition on invariant complements holds; but A′=M2(F) and A″={λI2} by Proposition 23.1.5, so A″≠A.

    Without semisimplicity. Let A⊆M2(F) be the upper triangular matrices. The line span(e1) is A-invariant, and it is the only invariant line: if v=(v1,v2) has v2≠0, then E12v=v2e1 is a nonzero multiple of e1 and does not lie in span(v). A complement of span(e1) would be an invariant line other than span(e1), so there is none and A does not act semisimply. Here A′={λI2}: a matrix commuting with E11 and E22 is diagonal, and commuting with E12 then forces the two diagonal entries to agree. Hence A″={λI2}′=M2(F), which is strictly larger than A.

    Remark. Semisimplicity is sufficient but not necessary. Let N=(0100) and A={xI2+yN}. Solving XN=NX gives exactly the matrices xI2+yN, so A′=A and therefore A″=A — even though A does not act semisimply, the invariant line span(e1)=kerN again being the only one.

    Remark. The name has two sources. In finite dimensions the result above is due to Wedderburn, and the argument through n copies of V is the finite-dimensional shadow of Jacobson’s density theorem, which we do not prove. For algebras of operators on a Hilbert space there is an analytic statement of the same shape, von Neumann’s double commutant theorem, which is outside this book.

    23.5.5. The commutant of one operator, revisited

    The two earlier computations of the book now read as computations of A′ for the smallest interesting algebra: the one generated by a single operator.

    Corollary 23.5.6 (The Commutant of a Single Operator)

    Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over F, let T∈L(V), and let A=F[T]⊆End(V) be the algebra of all polynomials in T. Then A′={S∈L(V):ST=TS}. Consequently the following two earlier results are computations of A′.

    • (a) If F=C and the matrix of T in some basis is A1⊕⋯⊕Ar with the Ap of pairwise disjoint spectra, then in that same basis every member of A′ has a block diagonal matrix for the partition into those block sizes (Corollary 12.10.4).
    • (b) If the matrix of T in some basis is a basic Weyr matrix of Weyr structure (w1,…,ws), then in that same basis every member of A′ has a block upper triangular matrix for the partition w1+⋯+ws (Proposition 10.4.6).

    If moreover T is diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λk and di=dim⁡Eλi(T), then A acts semisimply, dim⁡A=k, dim⁡A′=∑i=1kdi2, and A″=A.

    Idea. Nothing in (a) and (b) is new: whatever commutes with T commutes with every polynomial in it, so the two earlier computations were computations of A′ all along. The diagonalizable case is where the second commutant enters. The Lagrange polynomials evaluated at T are the projections onto the eigenspaces, and they turn out to be a basis of A; a commuting operator is exactly one that preserves each eigenspace and is otherwise unconstrained, which counts A′; and the same projections assemble an invariant complement for any invariant subspace, which is the hypothesis Theorem 23.5.5 needs.

    Proof. An operator commutes with every polynomial in T as soon as it commutes with T, and T∈A; this gives the displayed description of A′. Parts (a) and (b) are then the cited results verbatim, read for the matrix of T in the given basis.

    For the last sentence, let T be diagonalizable, Ei=Eλi(T), so V=E1⊕⋯⊕Ek by Theorem 9.4.1 (c). Let ℓi∈F[x] be the Lagrange polynomial with ℓi(λj)=δij (Theorem 6.6.1) and Pi=ℓi(T). Evaluating on each Ej, where T acts as λj, shows Pi is the projection onto Ei along the other summands. Since Tm=∑iλimPi for every m≥0, the algebra A is spanned by P1,…,Pk, which are independent because PiPj=δijPi and each Pi≠0: applying ∑iciPi=0 to a nonzero vector of Ej gives cj=0. So dim⁡A=k.

    By Theorem 9.9.1, every S∈A′ maps each Ei into itself; conversely such an S commutes with T, because both ST and TS act as λiS on Ei. So A′ is the set of operators preserving all the Ei, and restricting gives a linear bijection A′→End(E1)×⋯×End(Ek); hence dim⁡A′=∑idi2. Each Pi preserves every Ej, so Pi∈A′.

    Finally, A acts semisimply. Let U be an A-invariant subspace. Then U is T-invariant, and T|U is diagonalizable (Corollary 9.8.3), so U has a basis of eigenvectors of T (Theorem 9.4.1, (a) ⇔ (b)) and therefore U=(U∩E1)⊕⋯⊕(U∩Ek). Choose a complement Ci of U∩Ei inside Ei for each i (Theorem 1.7.6) and put C=C1⊕⋯⊕Ck. Then V=U⊕C, and C is A-invariant because Pj(C)=Cj⊆C and the Pj span A. So Theorem 23.5.5 applies and gives A″=A.

    So the diagonalizable case is an instance of Theorem 23.5.5, reached here by hand; the Weyr case in (b) is not, since F[W] does not act semisimply once s≥2. That is the honest shape of the relationship: the two earlier sections computed first commutants in cases where the second commutant theorem has nothing to say, and this section explains what extra hypothesis would make the computation reversible.

    Example (The Two Extremes)

    Inside Mn(F) with n≥2, compute A′ and A″ for (i) A the diagonal matrices, and (ii) A the scalar matrices.

    Solution.

    • (i) Let A={diag(c1,…,cn)}. Then X commutes with Eii for every i exactly when X is diagonal, since (XEii)kl=xkiδil and (EiiX)kl=δikxil, and comparing at (k,i) with k≠i gives xki=0. So A′=A, and applying the prime again, A″=A′=A. This agrees with Theorem 23.5.5: the diagonal algebra acts semisimply, Fn being the direct sum of the n coordinate lines, each of which is invariant and one-dimensional, hence irreducible.

    • (ii) Let A={λIn}. Every matrix commutes with a scalar matrix, so A′=Mn(F), and A″=Mn(F)′={λIn}=A by Proposition 23.1.5. Again A″=A, as it must be: the scalars act semisimply because every subspace is invariant and every subspace has a complement.

    The two examples sit at opposite ends. In (i) the commutant is as small as the algebra; in (ii) the commutant is everything. In both, applying the prime a second time returns to the start, which is what the theorem promises whenever the action is semisimple.

    23.5.6. Exercises

    A. Check your understanding

    Exercise (A1)
    • (a) State Burnside’s theorem, with every hypothesis.
    • (b) Give the field hypothesis of Theorem 23.5.5, and say why it differs from the one in (a).
    • (c) True or false, with a reason: if A⊆End(V) is a subalgebra containing idV, then A‴=A′.
    • (d) Explain in one sentence why {0} and V being the only A-invariant subspaces does not by itself imply Av=V for v≠0.

    Solution.

    • (a) F algebraically closed, V finite-dimensional over F with dim⁡V≥1, A⊆End(V) a subalgebra containing idV, and V irreducible as an A-space; the conclusion is A=End(V).

    • (b) Theorem 23.5.5 has no field hypothesis at all: it holds over every field. Algebraic closure entered Burnside only to produce an eigenvalue of (ab)|ima, and the double commutant proof never needs an eigenvalue — it needs an invariant complement, which semisimplicity hands over directly.

    • (c) True, and it holds for any subset: it is Proposition 23.1.4 (d). The two ingredients are (c) of that proposition, S⊆S″, and (b), that S⊆T implies T′⊆S′. Applying the prime to A⊆A″ gives A‴⊆A′, and applying the inclusion B⊆B″ to B=A′ gives A′⊆A‴.

    • (d) Because Av could be {0} if A contained no operator sending v anywhere nonzero; the identity in A is what rules this out, as in the warning after Lemma 23.5.1.

    B. Practice

    Exercise (B1: Determine which are irreducible)

    For each subalgebra of M2(C), determine whether it acts irreducibly on C2, and hence whether it equals M2(C). Justify your answer.

    • (a) The algebra generated by (0100).
    • (b) The algebra generated by (0110) and (100i).
    • (c) The algebra of all matrices (ab0a).

    Solution.

    • (a) Not irreducible. With N=(0100) we have N2=O, so the algebra is {xI2+yN}, and span(e1)=imN is invariant, proper, and nonzero. It is not M2(C); its dimension is 2.

    • (b) Irreducible, so it equals M2(C) by Theorem 23.5.2. The eigenvectors of diag(1,i) are the multiples of e1 and of e2 (the diagonal entries are distinct), and the swap matrix maps each of those lines to the other, so no line is invariant under both.

    • (c) Not irreducible: this is the algebra of (a), written out. The invariant line is again span(e1).

    Exercise (B2: A commutant and a double commutant)

    Let A=diag(1,1,2)∈M3(R) and let A=R[A]⊆M3(R).

    • (a) Compute dim⁡A.
    • (b) Describe A′ explicitly and compute dim⁡A′.
    • (c) Compute A″, and check the answer against Theorem 23.5.5.

    Solution.

    • (a) The eigenvalues are 1 and 2, each with a nonzero eigenspace, so mA=(x−1)(x−2) and, by the count in Corollary 23.5.6 with k=2, dim⁡A=2: a basis is I3,A, equivalently P1=diag(1,1,0), P2=diag(0,0,1).

    • (b) E1=span(e1,e2) and E2=span(e3). Every matrix in A′ preserves both (Theorem 9.9.1), and conversely a matrix preserving both commutes with A, since A acts as a scalar on each; so a matrix lies in A′ exactly when it has the shape (pq0uv000w). Its dimension is 22+12=5.

    • (c) A matrix commuting with all of these must commute in particular with diag(1,1,0) and diag(0,0,1), hence be of the same block shape, and its 2×2 block must commute with all of M2(R), hence be scalar (Proposition 23.1.5). So A″={αP1+βP2}=A, of dimension 2. Since A is diagonalizable, A acts semisimply, and Theorem 23.5.5 predicts exactly A″=A.

    Exercise (B3: One theorem, once)

    Let F be algebraically closed, let V be finite-dimensional over F with dim⁡V≥2, and let A⊆End(V) be a commutative subalgebra containing idV. Prove that A does not act irreducibly on V.

    Solution. Suppose it did. By Theorem 23.5.2, A=End(V). But End(V) is not commutative when dim⁡V≥2: choosing a basis and the operators with matrices E12 and E21 gives E12E21=E11≠E22=E21E12. This contradicts commutativity of A, so A is not irreducible.

    C. Going deeper

    Exercise (C1: The rank-one step needs its hypothesis)

    In Step 2 of the proof of Theorem 23.5.2, the operator ab was restricted to W=ima and an eigenvalue was extracted.

    • (a) Prove that ab(W)⊆W, and give an example of a,b∈M2(F) with ba(ima)⊈ima, so that the order of the product matters.
    • (b) Take F=R, A={xI2+yQ} with Q=(0−110). Compute the minimal rank r of a nonzero member of A and confirm that the conclusion r=1 fails.
    • (c) Which sentence of Step 2 fails for this A? Say exactly where the hypothesis on F was used.

    Solution.

    • (a) ab(W)=a(b(W))⊆ima=W, since a is applied last. For the other order take a=E11 and b=E21 in M2(F). Then ima=span(e1) and bae1=E21e1=e2∉span(e1).

    • (b) A nonzero xI2+yQ has determinant x2+y2>0 for real x,y not both zero, so it is invertible and has rank 2. Hence r=2, and the conclusion r=1 fails; consistently, A contains no rank-one matrix and is not all of M2(R).

    • (c) The sentence “its characteristic polynomial has a root λ∈F because F is algebraically closed”. With a=I2 and b=Q we get W=R2 and s=Q, whose characteristic polynomial x2+1 has no real root. Everything before that sentence is valid over any field.

    Exercise (C2: Commutants under a change of basis)

    Let V be finite-dimensional over F, let A⊆End(V) be a subalgebra containing idV, and let g∈End(V) be invertible. Put gAg−1={gag−1:a∈A}.

    • (a) Prove that gAg−1 is a subalgebra containing idV and that (gAg−1)′=gA′g−1.
    • (b) Deduce that if A″=A then (gAg−1)″=gAg−1.
    • (c) Hence show that the conclusion of Theorem 23.5.5 is a property of the algebra up to similarity, and use this to give, for each n≥2, a subalgebra of Mn(F) with A″≠A.

    Solution.

    • (a) The map a↦gag−1 is linear, sends idV to idV, and satisfies (ga1g−1)(ga2g−1)=g(a1a2)g−1, so the image is a subalgebra containing the identity. For the commutant: t commutes with every gag−1 if and only if g−1tg commutes with every a, that is, if and only if g−1tg∈A′, that is, t∈gA′g−1.

    • (b) Apply (a) twice: (gAg−1)″=(gA′g−1)′=gA″g−1=gAg−1.

    • (c) By (b), whether A″=A holds is unchanged by replacing A with a similar copy, so it is a property of the similarity class. For a failure in Mn(F) take An = all upper triangular matrices. The same computation as in the warning after Theorem 23.5.5, done with the matrix units Eii and Ei,i+1, gives An′={λIn}, hence An″=Mn(F)≠An; and by (b) every similar copy gAng−1 fails too.

    Exercise (C3: Is this look-alike a theorem?)

    Let V be finite-dimensional over an algebraically closed F and let A⊆End(V) be a subalgebra with idV∈A.

    • (a) Prove: if V is irreducible as an A-space then A′={λidV}.
    • (b) Is the converse true? That is, does A′={λidV} force V to be irreducible? Justify your answer.

    Hint for (b): consider the upper triangular matrices in M2(F).

    Solution.

    • (a) By Theorem 23.5.2, A=End(V), so A′=End(V)′={λidV} by Proposition 23.1.5. (This recovers the second half of Schur’s lemma, Theorem 23.4.1, in this special case.)

    • (b) No. Let A be the upper triangular matrices in M2(F). The warning after Theorem 23.5.5 computes A′={λI2}, yet span(e1) is a proper nonzero invariant subspace, so F2 is not irreducible as an A-space — and indeed A≠M2(F). A trivial commutant is therefore strictly weaker than irreducibility; it is semisimplicity, not a small commutant, that makes the commutant remember the algebra.
