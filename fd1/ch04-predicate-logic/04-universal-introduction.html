4.4  Proving “For Every”: Let x Be Arbitrary

Section 4.3 gave the rule for using a universal statement, and ended at a stall: from ∀x(P(x)∧Q(x)) it could derive P(c) for every term c we cared to name, but never ∀xP(x) itself. No list of instances is a statement about everything. This section gives the rule that proves a universal statement, ∀I. It needs one new device, a box that carries a name instead of an assumption, and one new side condition, which turns the mathematician’s “let x be arbitrary” into something a reader can check line by line.

4.4.1. One number we know nothing about

Here is a fact of school algebra.

For every real number x, x2−2x+1≥0.

We cannot check it number by number; there are too many numbers. The proof everyone writes takes one real number and argues about it: Let x∈R. Then x2−2x+1=(x−1)2, and a square is never negative. So x2−2x+1≥0. Then, often without saying so: since x was arbitrary, this holds for every real x.

Chapter 1 adopted a convention for sentences like “let x be a real number”: the letter names one fixed number, not further specified (Section 1.1). It promised that Chapter 4 would explain why an argument of that kind proves a statement about every x at once. Section 2.2 met the same step at the end of the proof of Theorem 2.2.1, passing from an arbitrary rational r to every rational; Section 3.2 met it in induction, passing from “let n∈N” to “for every n”. Each time the step was taken on trust. Here is why it is correct, and when it is not.

The argument about x used nothing about x except that it is a real number. It never said “suppose x>0” or “take x=3”. So it could be repeated word for word with 0, with −7, with π, with any real number in place of x, and it would prove the claim for that number. One argument about a number we know nothing about is a recipe for an argument about each number, and that is what “for every” asks for.

Everything depends on “nothing”. If the argument had used a fact about x that not every number has, the recipe would break for some numbers. “Let x be a real number with x>0. Then x>0. Since x was arbitrary, every real number is positive” is the same shape, and its conclusion is false. The step “since x was arbitrary” is legitimate only when x was arbitrary: when no premise and no assumption still in force says anything about it. The rule of this section makes that condition exact.

4.4.2. The flagged box

To prove “for every x”, take a fresh name that nothing is known about, prove the statement for that name, and generalize.

In a derivation the argument about the arbitrary x is drawn as a box, as Chapter 1 drew the argument under an assumption. The box opens with no assumption at all. Instead it carries the arbitrary name, written at its top left, and is called a flagged box; the name is its flag.

Definition 4.4.1 (Rules for the Universal Quantifier)
ix0⋮
jφ[x0/x]
k∀xφ∀I i–j
i∀xφ
kφ[t/x]∀E i

The rule on the left is ∀I (universal introduction, or generalization). It cites a flagged box, with flag x0, from its first line i to its last line j, and writes ∀xφ on line k. The flag x0 is a variable. The box’s last line j is φ[x0/x], and it is a line of the flagged box itself, not of a box inside it. The side condition is on the name: at line k,

  • x0 appears in no premise,
  • x0 appears in no assumption of a box still open at line k, and
  • x0 does not appear in ∀xφ.

The rule on the right is ∀E, as in Definition 4.3.1: t is any term free for x in φ.

In words: to prove that φ holds for every x, open a box flagged with a new name x0, prove φ[x0/x], “φ holds of x0”, as the box’s last line, close the box, and write ∀xφ. The side condition says that x0 really was arbitrary.

What a flagged box is. Definition 1.5.2 (Section 1.5) said that a box is a block of consecutive lines whose first line is an assumption. We now extend that definition, and two things change. First, a box may be flagged: it carries a name, a variable written at its top, and a flagged box need not open with an assumption. The box of ∀I has none; the box of the rule for using “there exists” in Section 4.6 is flagged and has one. Second, the rules that close a flagged box carry side conditions about its name, such as the one above. Everything else in Definition 1.5.2 stays word for word: two boxes are disjoint or one is inside the other, a box ends at one of its lines, and the scope rule decides which lines and boxes are available where. A flagged box with no assumption adds nothing to what is assumed; it only marks where the name is in use.

Here are the two boxes side by side: the box of →I from Definition 1.5.1, and the flagged box.

iφ
⋮
jψ
kφ→ψ→I i–j
ix0⋮
jφ[x0/x]
k∀xφ∀I i–j

Both draw a boundary and both are cited as a range i–j. The →I box opens with what we suppose; the flagged box opens with the name we have fixed. In the schema the flagged box begins with a gap ⋮ on line i, only so that the range has a first line; in a real derivation line i is whatever the box’s first line is. When a flagged box immediately contains another box, as in the swap of two quantifiers in the examples below, the range starts at the first line of that inner box.

Read the rule part by part: the flag, the three clauses of the side condition, and the last line.

  • The flag. Opening the box is the written “let x0 be arbitrary”. The flag is always a variable, never a constant: a constant has a fixed meaning, while the whole point of x0 is that it may stand for anything. The book writes flags with a subscript 0, x0, y0, ε0, so that they stand out.
  • No premise, no open assumption (the first and second clauses). This is “we know nothing about x0”. What is known at line k is the premises and the assumptions of the boxes still open there. If none of them mentions x0, then nothing that was used about x0 inside the box is special to x0.
  • Not in the conclusion (the third clause). The name must not leak out. Inside the box it stood for an arbitrary object, and ∀xφ is meant to speak about all objects through the bound x. A conclusion that still mentions x0 ties what it says to the one object x0 names, while claiming it of everything.
  • The last line. φ[x0/x] is what was proved about x0, and it must be a line of the flagged box itself, just as the last line of a →I box must be (Section 1.5). A line inside a further box would still depend on that box’s assumption.

“Appear” is the word of Section 4.2: written anywhere in the formula, including just after a quantifier symbol. So a box flagged x can never end in ∀xφ, since x appears in ∀xφ. That is one more reason the book never uses the quantified letter itself as the flag.

Fresh is not the same as new. The condition asks about three places only: the premises, the open assumptions, and the conclusion. Lines outside the box may mention x0, before it or after it. An earlier line ∀yR(x0,y), obtained by ∀E from a premise, is harmless; Exercise C1 builds a derivation with such a line. And once a flagged box has closed, its name may flag a later box. A name that satisfies the side condition at the line that closes its box is called fresh there. It need not be new to the whole derivation.

Why each clause. Each clause stops a derivation of a false conclusion from true premises. Here is one failure for each.

A name in a premise. If ∀I could generalize over a name in a premise, then P(x0)⊢∀xP(x):

1P(x0)
2x0P(x0)R 1
3∀xP(x)∀I 2–2

Over Z, read P(x) as “x is even” and let x0 name 2. The premise is true and line 3, “every integer is even”, is false. Line 3 breaks the first clause: x0 appears in the premise on line 1. The premise does say something about x0, so x0 is not arbitrary.

A name in an open assumption. Suppose x0>0, and inside that supposition generalize over x0:

1x0x0>0
2x0x0>0R 1
3∀x(x>0)∀I 2–2
4x0>0→∀x(x>0)→I 1–3
5∀y(y>0→∀x(x>0))∀I 1–4

Line 3 breaks the second clause: x0 appears in the assumption on line 1, whose box is still open at line 3. Every other line is legal, including line 5, where the box of line 1 has closed. The inner box on line 2 is flagged x0 while the outer box flagged x0 is still open; the definition allows this, since its side condition asks only about premises, open assumptions and the conclusion. And line 5 says “every positive number makes every number positive”, which over Z is false: at y=1 the hypothesis 1>0 holds and ∀x(x>0) fails at x=0. This is the “let x be a real number with x>0 … since x was arbitrary” of the opening, made into lines.

A name in the conclusion. From the premise ∀x(x≤x), true over N, open a box flagged y0:

1∀x(x≤x)
2y0y0≤y0∀E 1
3∀x(x≤y0)∀I 2–2

Line 2 is (x≤y0)[y0/x], so line 3 has the shape of ∀I with φ=(x≤y0). But y0 appears in the conclusion, which breaks the third clause. Line 3 says “every natural number is at most y0”, and that is false whatever y0 names: y0+1 is a counterexample. (Exercise C1 of Section 4.5 turns this into a sentence, “there is a largest natural number”.)

The requirement on the last line is the one Chapter 1 already imposed on every box rule, for the same reason.

4.4.3. Derivations with flagged boxes

The search method of Section 2.6 gets a new goal-directed step: when the goal is ∀xφ, open a flagged box with a fresh name x0, and make φ[x0/x] the box’s last line, the new goal. The examples below use it, each with an Idea.

Example (Flagged boxes at work)

Give a derivation of each sequent; in (b), of each direction.

  • (a) ∀x∀yR(x,y)⊢∀xR(x,x).
  • (b) ∀yφ⊣⊢∀zφ[z/y], for a formula φ and variables y, z with z not appearing in φ.
  • (c) ∀x∀yR(x,y)⊢∀y∀xR(x,y).

Solution.

  • (a) Idea. The goal begins with ∀x, so the goal-directed step opens a box flagged x0, and the new goal is R(x,x)[x0/x]=R(x0,x0). That goal names the term for ∀E (Section 4.3’s step): instantiate the premise at x0, twice.
    1∀x∀yR(x,y)
    2x0∀yR(x0,y)∀E 1
    3R(x0,x0)∀E 2
    4∀xR(x,x)∀I 2–3

    The side condition at line 4: x0 does not appear in the premise, no box is open at line 4, and x0 does not appear in ∀xR(x,x). Lines 2 and 3 instantiate at the variable x0, which is free for x and for y, since the formulas contain no quantifier on x0. Compare Section 4.3, (b), which reached only R(c,c): there the term was a constant, a name fixed by the language, and nothing can be generalized over a constant; here it is a flag, a variable that no premise mentions.

  • (b) Idea. This is the renaming of a bound variable at the front of a formula, which Section 4.2 took as a convention and promised to derive. Each direction has a ∀ goal, so a flagged box with a name that appears in neither formula, say z0; inside, one ∀E at z0 gives the instance the box needs. The only thought is checking that the two instances are the same formula.
    1∀yφ
    2z0φ[z0/y]∀E 1
    3∀zφ[z/y]∀I 2–2
    1∀zφ[z/y]
    2z0φ[z0/y]∀E 1
    3∀yφ∀I 2–2

    Take z0 to be a variable that does not appear in φ and is different from y and z. Since z does not appear in φ, the formula φ[z/y] is φ with each free y changed to z, and changing those z’s on to z0 gives the same result as changing each free y to z0 at once: (φ[z/y])[z0/z]=φ[z0/y]. So in the left derivation, line 2 is a legal ∀E (z0 does not appear in φ, so it is free for y), and it is the instance (φ[z/y])[z0/z] that the ∀I on line 3 needs. In the right derivation, line 2 is (φ[z/y])[z0/z], a legal ∀E for the same reason, and it is φ[z0/y], the instance that ∀yφ needs. At line 3 of each, z0 appears neither in the premise nor in the conclusion, and no box is open. Each direction has three lines.

  • (c) Idea. Read the goal’s quantifiers from the outside: ∀y first, so open a box flagged y0; the new goal ∀xR(x,y0) is again universal, so open a box flagged x0 inside it; the innermost goal is R(x0,y0). Two boxes, opened in the goal’s order. Now the premise’s quantifiers are removed in its order: ∀E at x0, then at y0.
1∀x∀yR(x,y)
2y0x0∀yR(x0,y)∀E 1
3R(x0,y0)∀E 2
4∀xR(x,y0)∀I 2–3
5∀y∀xR(x,y)∀I 2–4

The box flagged y0 immediately contains the box flagged x0, so it has no line of its own before the inner box; following the convention after Definition 4.4.1, line 5 cites it as 2–4, from the first line of the inner box. Its last line is line 4, a line of the outer box itself. At line 4, x0 is in no premise, the only open box (flagged y0) has no assumption, and x0 does not appear in ∀xR(x,y0). At line 5 the same holds for y0.

Nothing in (c) used that the formula under the quantifiers is R(x,y). For any formula ψ and different variables x and y, with flags x0 and y0 that are different from each other and from x and y and that do not appear in ψ, the same five lines derive ∀x∀yψ⊢∀y∀xψ: line 3 becomes (ψ[x0/x])[y0/y], which equals (ψ[y0/y])[x0/x], since the two substitutions put two different fresh names in for two different variables and so do not interfere. Exchanging the letters x and y gives the other direction. So two adjacent universal quantifiers may always be swapped: ∀x∀yψ⊣⊢∀y∀xψ. Section 4.1 read this off the game, where the challenger makes both moves; here it is derived.

Now the sequent at which Section 4.3 stalled, together with its converse. “For every x, P(x) and Q(x)” says the same as “P(x) for every x, and Q(x) for every x”:

Proposition 4.4.1 (Universal Quantifier and Conjunction)

∀x(P(x)∧Q(x))⊣⊢∀xP(x)∧∀xQ(x).

Idea. (⊢) The goal is a conjunction, so (Section 1.3) two subgoals, ∀xP(x) and ∀xQ(x). Each is universal, so each gets its own flagged box, and inside each, ∀E at the flag and ∧E give the box’s last line. (⊣) The goal is universal, so one flagged box, with the goal P(x0)∧Q(x0); take the premise apart by ∧E and instantiate each half at x0.

Proof. (⊢)

1∀x(P(x)∧Q(x))
2x0P(x0)∧Q(x0)∀E 1
3P(x0)∧E 2
4∀xP(x)∀I 2–3
5x0P(x0)∧Q(x0)∀E 1
6Q(x0)∧E 5
7∀xQ(x)∀I 5–6
8∀xP(x)∧∀xQ(x)∧I 4, 7

At lines 4 and 7, x0 appears neither in the premise nor in the conclusion of that line, and no box is open. The second flagged box reuses the name x0. That is allowed: the side condition at line 7 asks only about the premise, the open assumptions, and ∀xQ(x), and the earlier box, with its lines 2 and 3, closed at line 3 and is not available at line 7. (A fresh y0 would do equally well.)

(⊣)

1∀xP(x)∧∀xQ(x)
2∀xP(x)∧E 1
3∀xQ(x)∧E 1
4x0P(x0)∀E 2
5Q(x0)∀E 3
6P(x0)∧Q(x0)∧I 4, 5
7∀x(P(x)∧Q(x))∀I 4–6

At line 7, x0 is in neither the premise nor the conclusion, and no box is open. The two derivations prove the two sequents, as claimed.

The first derivation is the answer to Section 4.3’s stall: its lines 1–4 derive ∀xP(x) from ∀x(P(x)∧Q(x)). The proposition says that ∀ distributes over ∧. Section 4.9 collects it with the other laws that move a quantifier across a connective, and there the corresponding statement for ∀ and ∨ holds in one direction only.

4.4.4. Every element of the empty set

Section 4.1 read ∀x∈∅φ as true whatever φ says, because there is no element to test, and promised a derivation. Section 2.1 met the same phenomenon for implications: ¬P⊢P→Q (Proposition 2.1.2, the derived rule written VI). With ∀I the two meet.

Let A be a set with no elements. What that means is the premise ∀x¬(x∈A): no object is in A. From it, “every element of A is greater than 5”, which by Definition 4.1.1 is ∀x(x∈A→x>5), has a four-line derivation:

1∀x¬(x∈A)
2x0¬(x0∈A)∀E 1
3x0∈A→x0>5VI 2
4∀x(x∈A→x>5)∀I 2–3

The goal is universal, so a flagged box; the box’s goal x0∈A→x0>5 is an implication whose hypothesis is refuted by the instance of the premise at x0, and VI turns the refutation into the implication. Line 3 uses VI as a derived rule (Definition 2.3.1), with the formulas x0∈A and x0>5 in place of the letters P and Q. This is allowed because the derivation of Proposition 2.1.2 uses only ¬E, ⊥E and →I, whose schemas apply to any formulas (Definition 1.2.1), and it has no flagged box, so pasting it into the box flagged x0 gives a derivation in the rules themselves. From now on, any derived rule of Chapter 2 is used in this way on first-order formulas. Nothing in the derivation used x>5: with x<5, or with any formula in place of x>5 in which x0 does not appear, the same four lines go through. So, from the premise that A is empty, “every element of A has property φ” is derivable for every φ, as Section 4.1 said; the set ∅ is the case A=∅. One more flagged box gives a statement about upper bounds:

1∀x¬(x∈A)
2u0x0¬(x0∈A)∀E 1
3x0∈A→x0≤u0VI 2
4∀x(x∈A→x≤u0)∀I 2–3
5∀u∀x(x∈A→x≤u)∀I 2–4

Over R, line 5 says that every real number u is an upper bound of A: every element of A is at most u. For an empty set this holds for every u, because there are no elements to check. At line 4, x0 appears in neither the premise nor ∀x(x∈A→x≤u0), and the only open box (flagged u0) has no assumption. The name u0 does appear in that conclusion, which is allowed, since the condition at line 4 is about x0 only. At line 5, u0 appears in neither the premise nor ∀u∀x(x∈A→x≤u), and no box is open.

4.4.5. How this rule looks in a mathematical proof

Here is the opening proof as a derivation, with the general fact about squares as a premise and the algebra as one line justified “arith” (Section 4.3).

1∀y(y2≥0)
2x0(x0−1)2≥0∀E 1
3x02−2⋅x0+1≥0arith 2
4∀x(x2−2⋅x+1≥0)∀I 2–3
Line Sentence
box x0 “Let x∈R.”
2 “Then (x−1)2≥0, since a square is never negative.” (∀E at x0−1)
3 “That is, x2−2x+1≥0.” (arith)
4 “Since x was arbitrary, x2−2x+1≥0 for every real x.” (∀I)

Lines 2 and 3 are the derivation of Section 4.3 with the flag x0 in place of the constant a. What changed is only the name: a was a constant that the language fixed, so nothing could be generalized over it; x0 is a variable that no premise mentions, so line 4 may generalize. The written proof uses the same letter x for the flag and for the bound variable, and says “let x∈R” where the derivation opens the box. Readers of mathematics are expected to supply the box.

Since the domain is R, “let x∈R” asks nothing of x. A proof that says “let ε>0” does ask something, and its box opens with the assumption ε0>0; Section 4.8 gives the bounded form of the rule that handles it.

4.4.6. Common errors

Three errors with ∀I are common enough to name now. The first of them opens Chapter 5’s catalogue of steps that are not allowed.

  • Checking instances. Verifying φ at x=0,1,2, or at a thousand values, proves φ[0/x], φ[1/x], φ[2/x], and so on, and not ∀xφ. The rule needs one proof about a fresh name, not many proofs about particular terms. Exercise C2 shows how plausible this error can look.
  • Generalizing over a name in an open assumption. Inside “suppose x0>0”, the name x0 is not arbitrary. The second derivation of “Why each clause” shows where this leads.
  • Generalizing over a name in a premise. A premise about x0, such as “x0 is even”, makes x0 special. The first derivation of “Why each clause” shows it.

Warning. “Let x be arbitrary” is a promise about every line that follows, and the most common way to break it is an assumption made on the way. “Let n∈N and suppose n is even. Then n2 is even. Since n was arbitrary, every n2 is even” is false at n=1. What the argument proves is “for every n, if n is even, then n2 is even”: the →I box on “n is even” must close before the ∀I step, so that the assumption is no longer open.

Quick check. In the derivation below, line 6 is justified by ∀I. Is it legal? If not, which clause of the side condition fails, and can you find a reading in which the premises are true and line 6 is false?

1R(c,y0)
2∀x∀y(R(x,y)→R(y,x))
3y0∀y(R(c,y)→R(y,c))∀E 2
4R(c,y0)→R(y0,c)∀E 3
5R(y0,c)→E 4, 1
6∀yR(y,c)∀I 3–5

Solution. No. Lines 1–5 are legal; in particular line 5 may cite line 1, which lies in no box. But at line 6 the flag y0 appears in the premise on line 1, so the first clause fails: line 5 was proved about y0 using a fact about y0, and y0 was not arbitrary. A reading: over Z, let R(x,y) be x≠y, which is symmetric, so premise 2 is true; let c name 0 and y0 name 1, so premise 1, 0≠1, is true. Line 6 says that every integer differs from 0, which is false at y=0.

4.4.7. A foreshadowing

The rules for ∀ are now complete, and a natural sequent is still out of reach: ∀xP(x)⊢∃xP(x). If everything has P, then something has P, since the domain is not empty. But the goal is an existential statement, and no rule yet produces an ∃. Section 4.5 gives the rule for proving “there exists”, and this sequent is its first example.

4.4.8. What the rule sounds like

The table gains a row for ∀I, below the row for ∀E from Section 4.3.

Rule You write Lean
∀E “In particular, for x=t, …”; “applying the hypothesis to t, …” h t (or specialize h t)
∀I “Let x be arbitrary. … Since x was arbitrary, …” intro x

Other forms of the opening phrase are “let x∈R”, “fix ε>0”, “take any n∈N”; the closing phrase is often left out, since the reader knows that a proof which began by fixing x proves a statement about every x. In Lean, intro x turns the goal forall x, P x into the goal P x with a new name x in context, about which nothing is assumed: Lean’s version of opening the flagged box. The same tactic opened the →I box in Section 1.5, and the two boxes are, to Lean, one kind of thing.

4.4.9. The search method: one more step

  • A goal ∀xφ. Open a flagged box with a fresh name x0, one that appears in no premise, in no open assumption, and not in the goal. The new goal, the box’s last line, is φ[x0/x].

This step is safe, in the sense of Section 2.6. If ∀xφ can be derived at all, then so can φ[x0/x], by one more ∀E at x0; so opening the box never closes off a derivation that would otherwise exist. Take the step as soon as the goal is universal, and choose a flag that appears nowhere yet, so that the side condition cannot fail.

1P(x0)
2x0P(x0)R 1
3∀xP(x)∀I 2–2
4P(x0)→∀xP(x)→I 1–3
  • (a) Write the schema of ∀I with its side condition.
  • (b) For each of D1, D2, D3, decide whether its ∀I line is legal. If not, name the clause of the side condition that fails.
  • (c) True or false, with a reason: “fresh” means that x0 appears nowhere in the derivation outside its flagged box.
  • (d) In the derivation of the (⊢) half of Proposition 4.4.1, the name x0 flags two boxes. Why does the side condition allow this?

Solution.

  • (a) A box flagged x0, with no assumption, from line i to line j, whose last line j is φ[x0/x], a line of the flagged box itself; then line k: ∀xφ, justified “∀I i–j”. At line k the name x0 appears in no premise, in no assumption of a box still open at line k, and not in ∀xφ (Definition 4.4.1).

  • (b) D1: not legal. At line 5 the flag x0 appears in the premise on line 2 (the first clause). Over Z, with P(x) and Q(x) both read as “x is even” and x0 naming 2, both premises are true and line 5, “every integer is even”, is false.

    D2: legal. Line 2 mentions x0 outside the box, and that is allowed: it is neither a premise nor an assumption, and the conclusion ∀xR(x,x) does not contain x0. Line 3 cites line 2, which is available, since no box contains it. (It is also correct in meaning: line 2 came from a premise that says nothing about x0, so it holds whatever x0 names.)

    D3: not legal. At line 3 the assumption P(x0) on line 1 is still open, and x0 appears in it (the second clause). Line 4, which closes the box legally, then says “if x0 has P, everything has P”, false over Z with P “even” and x0 naming 2.

  • (c) False. The side condition asks about the premises, the assumptions of boxes still open at the ∀I line, and the conclusion of that line. Other lines may mention x0, as line 2 of D2 does, and a closed flagged box may share its name with a later one.

  • (d) At line 7, which closes the second box, the side condition asks whether x0 appears in the premise (no), in an assumption of an open box (no box is open), and in ∀xQ(x) (no). The first box’s lines mention x0, but they are neither premises nor open assumptions, and the box closed at line 3, so its lines are not even available at line 7.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: A chain, for every x)

Give a derivation of ∀x(P(x)→Q(x)), ∀x(Q(x)→R(x))⊢∀x(P(x)→R(x)), and check the side condition at its ∀I line.

Solution. The goal is universal, so open a box flagged x0; the new goal P(x0)→R(x0) is an implication, so open a →I box on P(x0); the new goal is R(x0). Now Section 4.3’s exercise B1 runs inside the boxes, with x0 for a.

1∀x(P(x)→Q(x))
2∀x(Q(x)→R(x))
3x0P(x0)
4P(x0)→Q(x0)∀E 1
5Q(x0)→E 4, 3
6Q(x0)→R(x0)∀E 2
7R(x0)→E 6, 5
8P(x0)→R(x0)→I 3–7
9∀x(P(x)→R(x))∀I 3–8

The flagged box immediately contains the →I box, so line 9 cites it from line 3. At line 9: x0 appears in neither premise; the assumption P(x0) mentions x0, but its box closed at line 7, so it is not open at line 9; and x0 does not appear in ∀x(P(x)→R(x)). The order of the boxes matters: the →I box is inside the flagged box and closes first.

Exercise (B2: Contraposition under a quantifier)
  • (a) Give a derivation of ∀x(P(x)→Q(x))⊢∀x¬Q(x)→∀x¬P(x).
  • (b) Try to derive the converse, ∀x¬Q(x)→∀x¬P(x)⊢∀x(P(x)→Q(x)). Say where the search stalls, and which side condition stops it.

Solution.

  • (a) The goal is an implication: a →I box on ∀x¬Q(x), with goal ∀x¬P(x). That is universal: a box flagged x0, with goal ¬P(x0). That is a negation: a ¬I box on P(x0), with goal ⊥. Inside, instantiate both universal lines at x0 and look for a clash: P(x0) and the instance of the premise give Q(x0), and the assumption ∀x¬Q(x) at x0 gives ¬Q(x0).
    1∀x(P(x)→Q(x))
    2∀x¬Q(x)
    3x0P(x0)
    4P(x0)→Q(x0)∀E 1
    5Q(x0)→E 4, 3
    6¬Q(x0)∀E 2
    7⊥¬E 5, 6
    8¬P(x0)¬I 3–7
    9∀x¬P(x)∀I 3–8
    10∀x¬Q(x)→∀x¬P(x)→I 2–9

    At line 9, the open box is the →I box of line 2, whose assumption ∀x¬Q(x) does not contain x0; the premise and the conclusion do not contain it either. So the side condition holds, even though the flagged box sits inside an open box.

  • (b) The goal is universal, so open a box flagged x0, then a →I box on P(x0), with goal Q(x0). The only premise is an implication, ∀x¬Q(x)→∀x¬P(x). Using it needs its hypothesis ∀x¬Q(x), which nothing supplies; and its conclusion, ∀x¬P(x), would clash with P(x0) and close the case by ⊥E. So everything turns on deriving ∀x¬Q(x). The goal Q(x0) is atomic and nothing produces it, so the method’s last resort is an RAA box on ¬Q(x0). Inside, the available facts about Q are ¬Q(x0), and to reach ∀x¬Q(x) from it we would close a flagged box over x0. The side condition forbids it: x0 appears in the open assumptions P(x0) and ¬Q(x0). A fresh name y0 does not help either, since nothing tells us anything about ¬Q(y0). The search stalls at the second clause of the side condition.

A stalled search proves nothing by itself; a cleverer derivation might exist. Section 4.11 settles the question with a structure in which the premise is true and the conclusion false, and shows that the converse does not follow.

Exercise (B3: A number is at most its absolute value, for every number)

Example (A number is at most its absolute value) (Section 1.7) derived |x|>x∨|x|=x for one fixed real x from three premises about that x: x≥0∨x<0, x≥0→|x|=x, and x<0→|x|>x. Turn it into a derivation of ∀x(|x|>x∨|x|=x) from premises that are universal statements. Explain why the premises of Example (A number is at most its absolute value), copied with x0 in place of x, will not do.

Solution. The goal is universal, so the derivation of Section 1.7 must run inside a box flagged x0. Its three premises mention the number it is about. So here the premises are the three facts for every x, and inside the box ∀E at x0 gives each fact about x0.

1∀x(x≥0∨x<0)
2∀x(x≥0→|x|=x)
3∀x(x<0→|x|>x)
4x0x0≥0∨x0<0∀E 1
5x0≥0→|x0|=x0∀E 2
6x0<0→|x0|>x0∀E 3
7x0≥0
8|x0|=x0→E 5, 7
9|x0|>x0∨|x0|=x0∨I 8
10x0<0
11|x0|>x0→E 6, 10
12|x0|>x0∨|x0|=x0∨I 11
13|x0|>x0∨|x0|=x0∨E 4, 7–9, 10–12
14∀x(|x|>x∨|x|=x)∀I 4–13

Lines 7–13 are the derivation of Example (A number is at most its absolute value) with x0 for x, its premises replaced by lines 4–6. At line 14, x0 appears in none of the premises, no box is open (the two case boxes closed at lines 9 and 12), and x0 is not in the conclusion. The derivation has 14 lines.

If instead the premises were x0≥0∨x0<0, x0≥0→|x0|=x0 and x0<0→|x0|>x0, the box could reach line 13 just as well, but line 14 would break the first clause: x0 would appear in a premise. And rightly so. Premises about x0 are facts about one number, and a derivation from them proves something about that number only. The universal premises are true facts about every real number, and that is what entitles the conclusion to speak about every real number.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: How fresh is fresh?)
  • (a) Give a derivation of ∀x∀yR(x,y)⊢R(x0,x0)∧∀xR(x,x) in which the line R(x0,x0) is written before a flagged box that uses the same name x0. Check every clause of the side condition at the ∀I line.
  • (b) A stronger condition is sometimes proposed: “x0 occurs nowhere in the derivation outside its box”. Does your derivation meet it? Explain why the conclusion of your derivation is nonetheless correct, so that the stronger condition forbids more than it needs to.
  • (c) Could the last line of the flagged box in (a) be obtained by R, repeating the earlier line R(x0,x0)? Is the result a derivation?

Solution (Hint 1). The goal is a conjunction. Derive the left conjunct first, by two ∀E lines outside any box.

Solution (Hint 2). For the right conjunct, open a box flagged x0 and instantiate the premise at x0 again inside it. At the ∀I line, list the premises, the open assumptions and the conclusion, and look for x0 in each.

Solution.

  • (a)
    1∀x∀yR(x,y)
    2∀yR(x0,y)∀E 1
    3R(x0,x0)∀E 2
    4x0∀yR(x0,y)∀E 1
    5R(x0,x0)∀E 4
    6∀xR(x,x)∀I 4–5
    7R(x0,x0)∧∀xR(x,x)∧I 3, 6

    The side condition at line 6. Premises: the only premise is line 1, ∀x∀yR(x,y), and x0 does not appear in it. Open assumptions: no box is open at line 6, so there are none. Conclusion: ∀xR(x,x) does not contain x0. The last line of the box, line 5, is R(x,x)[x0/x] and is a line of the box itself. So line 6 is legal, and the derivation has 7 lines. Lines 2, 3 and 7 mention x0 outside the box, and the side condition does not ask about them.

  • (b) No: lines 2, 3 and 7 mention x0 outside its box. Yet the conclusion is correct. The reason the side condition looks only at premises and open assumptions is that these are the only lines taken without proof, so they are the only lines that can single out what x0 names; every other available line is derived from them. Lines 2 and 3 were derived from a premise that does not mention x0; they hold whatever x0 names. So the argument in the box really is about an arbitrary object, and ∀xR(x,x) follows. The stronger condition would reject this derivation, although renaming the box’s flag to a new letter z0 (lines 4–5 becoming ∀yR(z0,y) and R(z0,z0)) gives a derivation that meets it and has the same last line. A condition that rejects a correct derivation for the spelling of a name forbids more than safety needs.

  • (c) Yes. R may copy into the box any line available there, and line 3 lies in no box, so it is available at every later line. With line 5 justified “R 3” (and line 4 deleted), the box is a single line R(x0,x0), and the side condition at the ∀I line is checked exactly as in (a): nothing in it looks at where the box’s last line came from. The derivation is legal, and it is correct for the reason in (b): line 3 was derived from a premise that says nothing about x0.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: the left conjunct by two ∀E lines at x0, outside any box. - 2: a flagged box with ∀E at the flag and ∀I citing it. - 2: the side condition checked clause by clause: premise, open assumptions, conclusion. - 2: (b) explains that lines outside the box that do not rest on premises or assumptions about x0 cannot make x0 special. Alternatively, 2 for showing that renaming the box’s flag to a new letter gives a derivation that meets the stronger condition and has the same last line. - 2: (c) correct answer with the availability check for the repeated line. Fatal errors (0 for the step): claiming that line 3 makes line 6 illegal; checking the side condition against lines that are not premises, open assumptions or the conclusion.

Exercise (C2: Find the error)

A student offers this proof.

Claim. For every real number x, x2−2x+1≥0.

Proof. (1) Let x be a real number. (2) At x=0: 0−0+1=1≥0. (3) At x=1: 1−2+1=0≥0. (4) At x=2: 4−4+1=1≥0. (5) So x2−2x+1≥0, and since x was arbitrary, the claim holds for every real x.

  • (a) Is the claim true?
  • (b) Does the argument prove it? Name the step that fails and the rule it misuses.
  • (c) Show that the step can lead to a false conclusion: give a polynomial p for which the same checks at x=0,1,2 succeed and p(x)≥0 fails for some real x.
  • (d) Point to a correct proof.

Solution (Hint 1). Write lines (2)–(4) as formulas. Which terms do they mention?

Solution (Hint 2). Write the student’s proof as a derivation: open a box flagged x0 for line (1), and write lines (2)–(4) as lines inside it. What would line (5) have to cite?

Solution.

  • (a) Yes. For every real x, x2−2x+1=(x−1)2≥0, since a square is never negative.

  • (b) No. Lines (2)–(4) are true, and line (1) opens a flagged box, “let x be arbitrary”. The error is in line (5). With φ the formula x2−2⋅x+1≥0, lines (2)–(4) establish φ[0/x], φ[1/x] and φ[2/x]: statements about the constants 0, 1, 2. None of them is φ[x0/x] for the flag. Inside the box, nothing about the arbitrary x was ever proved. The first sentence of (5), “so x2−2x+1≥0”, has the right form: it is φ[x0/x], with the student’s x as the flag. But no rule writes it: it is not a premise, an assumption or a repetition, and no rule takes φ[0/x], φ[1/x] and φ[2/x] to φ[x0/x]. So the closing “since x was arbitrary” applies ∀I to a box whose last line was never derived: the lines the box legally derives are all instances at constants, not the instance at the flag. Line (1) was idle: the argument never used the x it introduced.

  • (c) Take p(x)=x(x−1)(x−2). The same checks succeed: p(0)=0≥0, p(1)=0≥0, p(2)=0≥0. But p(−1)=(−1)(−2)(−3)=−6<0, so “p(x)≥0 for every real x” is false. So the pattern of (5), concluding “for every x” from checks at a few constants, can lead from true checks to a false conclusion. It is not an instance of ∀I, as (b) showed. (Section 4.11 states, and the optional Section 4.12 proves, that legal steps never lead from true premises to a false conclusion.) The claim in (a) is true for a different reason, one that the argument never gave.

  • (d) The derivation under “How this rule looks in a mathematical proof”: inside the box flagged x0, ∀E applies “squares are not negative” at x0−1, an arith line rewrites it as x02−2x0+1≥0, and that line is φ[x0/x], so ∀I applies.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: (a) the claim is true, with a reason (it is a square). - 3: (b) names step (5) and ∀I; says that lines (2)–(4) are instances at constants, not the instance at the flag. - 3: (c) a polynomial that passes the three checks and fails somewhere, with both computations shown. - 2: (d) a correct proof, generalizing over a statement proved about the arbitrary x. Fatal errors (0 for the step): answering (b) with “the claim is false”; a “counterexample” in (c) that fails one of the three checks or is non-negative everywhere.
