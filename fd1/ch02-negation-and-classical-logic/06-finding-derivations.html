2.6  How to Find a Derivation

We now have every rule for the connectives. Checking a derivation is mechanical, as Definition 1.4.2 makes it: for each line, which rule, which lines, and are they available. Finding one is not, since an assumption may be any statement at all. Yet every Idea in Chapters 1 and 2 found its derivation with the same handful of moves. This section introduces no new rule. It collects those moves into one method, puts them in order, says why the order is right, and then watches the method work: on four searches that succeed, one that fails, and one mathematical goal. It ends with every rule in one place.

2.6.1. The moves so far

Each section since Section 1.2 has added a move, either to the list read off the goal (goal-directed: which introduction rule builds the goal’s main connective?) or to the list read off the premises (premise-directed: which elimination rule uses an available line’s main connective?). Here they are together, with the section where each first appeared.

resource φ→ψ fire it on φ by →E; if φ is missing, make it a subgoal Section 1.3

Eleven moves is a lot to hold in mind at a blank page. The method below turns them into an order of attack. To state it we need a name for a derivation that is only half written.

2.6.2. Partial derivations

A search builds a derivation from the outside in: the last line first, then the boxes the goal asks for, then the lines in between. What exists halfway is not yet a derivation, but it is close to one.

Definition 2.6.1 (Partial Derivation)

A partial derivation is a list of lines laid out as a derivation (Definition 1.4.2), except that some stretches of lines are still missing. Each missing stretch is a gap, drawn as a row ⋮. The line just below a gap is its current goal; it is already written, but it has no justification yet, since the lines of the gap are to supply one. The lines available at the current goal, in the sense of Definition 1.4.2, are the gap’s resources.

In words: every line that has been written is a premise, an assumption, a current goal, or a line justified by a rule from lines and boxes available to it; the boxes are nested and closed as in any derivation; only the gaps are missing. A search starts with a single gap, below the premises and above the conclusion, and each step replaces a gap by lines, boxes, and smaller gaps. It ends when no gap is left, and then the partial derivation is a derivation.

The degenerate partial derivation is the starting point of every search: the premises, one gap, and the goal. Its resources are the premises. The snapshots in the worked searches below are all partial derivations; a line carries the number it will have in the finished derivation, so that snapshots can be compared, and a line whose place is not yet fixed carries no number. On paper you would number the lines at the end.

Nonexample. Take a snapshot and leave one of its boxes open at the bottom, so that the conclusion sits inside the box. That is not a partial derivation, even though every line is correct so far: Definition 1.4.2 asks that every box close and that the last line lie outside every box, and a gap cannot repair that, since filling a gap adds lines only above its current goal.

Quick check. In the second snapshot of Example (Splitting the goal, then using the premise) below, the second gap has the current goal ⊥ on line 11. List its resources.

Solution. Lines 1, 7, 8, and 9: the premise P→¬Q, the line ¬(P∧Q), and the assumptions Q and P of the two boxes that contain the gap. Line 7 lies in no box and above the gap, so it is available, though this gap will not need it. Lines 2–6 are not resources: they lie in a box that has closed.

2.6.3. The method

Work from both ends: let the goal choose the boxes, let the premises choose the eliminations, and meet in the middle.

Algorithm. Searching for a derivation of Γ⊢φ.

  1. Set up. Write the premises Γ at the top and φ at the bottom, with one gap between.
  2. Goal-directed step. Take the main connective of the current goal (Definition 1.1.2) and set up its introduction rule:
    • ψ∧χ: two gaps, with goals ψ and χ (Section 1.2);
    • ψ→χ: a box opened with ψ; inside it, the new goal is χ (Section 1.4);
    • ¬ψ: a box opened with ψ; inside it, the new goal is ⊥ (Section 2.2);
    • ψ↔χ: two boxes, one from ψ to χ and one from χ to ψ (Section 2.4);
    • ψ∨χ: postpone; do not choose a side yet (Section 1.5), unless the disjunction is being manufactured for a clash with its own negation (step 4), when either side may be chosen (see below).
    Repeat while the current goal is a ∧, →, ¬, or ↔.
  3. Premise-directed step. Take the main connective of a resource and apply its elimination rule: ∧E freely (Section 1.2); →E and ↔E when the other statement they need is a resource, or can be made a new goal (Sections 1.3 and 2.4); ∨E by opening two boxes, one per disjunct, each ending in the current goal (Section 1.6). A derived rule may do the work of several lines (Section 2.3): a resource ψ→χ with ¬χ available gives ¬ψ by MT; a resource ψ∨χ with ¬ψ available gives χ by DS.
  4. Clash. If the current goal is ⊥, look for a pair χ, ¬χ among the resources, or make χ the new goal for some resource ¬χ and manufacture it, starting again at step 2 for that goal. Whatever the current goal, if the resources clash, write ⊥ and then the goal by ⊥E: an impossible case closes itself (Section 2.1).
  5. Stuck. Use RAA: open a box with the negation of the current goal, whose new goal is ⊥; if that gives nothing, try the same with a goal further out whose box contains the stalled gap. Or use LEM on a well-chosen statement χ, and split on χ∨¬χ by ∨E. Then return to step 3 (Section 2.5).

When no gap is left, number the lines and check the derivation as Definition 1.4.2 says.

The steps are listed in the order in which to try them, and the order has reasons.

The goal-directed steps lose nothing. Suppose the goal ψ→χ is derivable from the resources Γ at all, by some derivation D. Then χ is derivable from Γ together with ψ: add ψ to the premises of D (Definition 1.2.3 asks only that the premises belong to the list), and append the line χ by →E, citing the last line of D and the premise ψ. So opening a box on ψ never turns a derivable gap into an underivable one. With ∧E, ¬E, or ↔E as the appended line, the same argument covers ∧, ¬, and ↔. That is why step 2 may be applied without thinking.

Choosing a side of ∨ can lose everything. A disjunction can be derivable when neither side is. From P∨Q the goal Q∨P is derivable (Example (First derivations with “or”)), but P∨Q does not say which side holds, so a last line ∨I from Q, or from P, leaves a gap that cannot be filled (Chapter 3 makes “cannot” exact, Corollary 3.5.3). Choosing a side is safe only once the resources decide it, typically inside a case of ∨E, or when the disjunction is being manufactured for a clash (step 4) with a resource that is its negation: then the resources derive ⊥ as soon as they derive the disjunction, and from ⊥ every statement, either side included, by ⊥E.

Step 5 also loses nothing, and still comes last. If φ is derivable from the resources, then ⊥ is derivable from them together with ¬φ, by one appended ¬E; so an RAA box is always safe. A split on χ∨¬χ is safe too, since each case only adds a resource. Step 5 comes last because the goal ⊥ gives step 2 nothing to work with, because a good χ must be chosen, and because a derivation that did not need the classical step reads worse with it (the costume of Section 2.5).

Quick check. For each goal, with no premises, name the first rule the method aims for and the box or gaps it sets up.

  • (a) ¬(P∧¬P)
  • (b) (P∨Q)→(Q∨P)
  • (c) (P→Q)∧(Q→(P→Q))
  • (d) P∨¬(P∧Q)

Solution.

  • (a) ¬I: a box opened with P∧¬P, goal ⊥. (b) →I: a box opened with P∨Q, goal Q∨P; that goal is a disjunction, so it is postponed, and step 3 uses the new resource P∨Q by ∨E. (c) ∧I: two gaps, with goals P→Q and Q→(P→Q), each then a box. (d) Step 2 postpones the disjunction, and with no premises steps 3 and 4 have nothing to use, so the search is at step 5 at once. LEM on P works: in the case P, ∨I gives the left side; in the case ¬P, a ¬I box on P∧Q clashes (its ∧E gives P) and gives ¬(P∧Q), and ∨I gives the right side.

2.6.4. Splitting the goal

We now run the method. The first search uses only steps 2, 3, and 4. It is the full version of the chapter’s main move, read the skeleton off the goal, then work from both ends, which comes back twice in the exercises with parts left blank.

Find a derivation of P→¬Q⊢¬(P∧Q)∧(Q→¬P).

Notice how little thinking the search needed. Step 2 is mechanical, and it did most of the work: by the time the premise was consulted, each gap was small, with the goal ⊥ and two or three resources. That is the typical shape of a successful search: the goal fixes the skeleton, and the premises fill in the flesh.

2.6.5. Cases must end in the goal

When the goal gives nothing, the premises must. The next search is driven by a disjunction among the premises.

Example (Proof by cases, found by the method)

Find a derivation of P∨Q, P→R, Q→R⊢R.

Solution. The goal R is a letter, so step 2 has nothing to do. Step 3 looks at the resources. →E on P→R needs P, and making P a new goal is hopeless: from P∨Q we know that one of P, Q holds, not which. The disjunction itself is the resource to use, by ∨E, with two boxes that each end in the current goal R:

This is the pattern that Section 1.6 called the skeleton of almost every proof by cases (compare Example (Cases on a hypothesis are two implications), which packages the same two boxes into one implication). The method found it without being told.

Why the boxes end in the current goal. ∨E (Definition 1.6.1) gives, after the two boxes, their common last line and nothing else. A box that ends in anything but the current goal leaves that goal still to be reached, and two boxes that end in different statements cannot be combined at all. The word current matters: had the goal been S→R, step 2 would first open a box on S, the current goal inside it would be R, and the two case boxes, opened inside the box of S, would end in R, not in S→R.

2.6.6. Postponing a disjunction

The third search has a disjunction as its goal. It shows why step 2 postpones one, and how a dead end can name the statement to split on.

Example (Choosing a side too early)

Find a derivation of P→(Q∨R)⊢(P→Q)∨(P→R).

Solution. The tempting start. The goal is a disjunction, and it is tempting to choose a side at once, say P→Q, and finish with ∨I. The new goal is an implication, so we open a box on P; then →E gives Q∨R, and ∨E splits into the cases Q and R, each to end in the current goal Q. The first case ends at once. The second is a dead end:

In the case R the resources are P→(Q∨R), P, Q∨R, and R, and none of them yields Q: no resource has Q as a conclusion except Q∨R, which is already split, and nothing clashes. Choosing the right side instead fails in the mirror image, in the case Q. In fact neither side follows from the premise alone: with P and Q true and R false the premise is true and P→R is false, and with P and R true and Q false the premise is true and P→Q is false. (Chapter 3 turns such rows into proofs that no derivation exists: Corollary 3.5.3.)

The diagnosis. Each case of the dead end knew which side it wanted: the case Q wanted P→Q, and the case R wanted P→R. But those cases were opened inside the box of P, too late to choose the side of a disjunction that stands outside it. The side must be decided before any box is opened, and the premise does not decide it. Steps 2–4 are used up: we are at step 5.

The dead end also names the statement to split on. If Q holds, the left side is easy. If Q fails, then from P the premise gives Q∨R with its left side refuted, and DS gives R. So we use LEM on Q, and each case gets the whole goal:

2.6.7. When nothing else works

The fourth search reaches step 5 in the other form, RAA, and then needs steps 2 and 4 inside the box.

Example (A search that reaches step 5 at once)

Find a derivation of ¬(P↔Q)⊢P∨Q.

Solution. Steps 1–4. The goal is a disjunction, so step 2 postpones it. The one resource is a negation, and a negation can be used only in a clash (step 4), which needs the goal ⊥; the goal is P∨Q. Neither side is in hand: the premise says only that P and Q differ, not which one holds. Steps 2–4 have nothing to offer.

What the method did. Step 5 came first, because nothing else applied, and after it steps 2 and 4 did the rest: step 4 chose which clash to aim for, step 2 read the two boxes off P↔Q, and step 4 closed each box with a manufactured clash and ⊥E. In words: suppose neither P nor Q holds; then each of them implies the other, since neither can hold at all; so P↔Q, against the premise. The assumption of line 2 is used twice, at lines 5 and 9. A split by LEM on P also works, but less well: in the case ¬P the goal side Q is a letter that the resources do not give, and the search is stuck there a second time.

The derivation of Peirce’s law in Section 2.5 (Proposition 2.5.4) was found by the same method, and it adds one detail to step 5. There, step 2 opened a box with the goal P, step 3 made P→Q a subgoal, step 2 opened a box on P with the goal Q, and the search stalled at Q. Assuming ¬Q at the stalled gap gives nothing new: a clash with ¬Q needs Q, the stalled goal itself. Assuming ¬P one level out does, because the new resource ¬P is available in every gap inside its box, including the stalled one. That is why step 5 says to try a goal further out when the current one gives nothing.

2.6.8. A search that fails

The method is not a machine that always succeeds. Here it fails, and the failure is instructive.

Try P→Q⊢Q→P. Step 2 opens a box on Q with the goal P:

1P→Q
2Q
⋮
P
Q→P→I

The goal P is a letter. Step 3: →E on line 1 needs P, the goal itself. Step 4: nothing clashes, and there is no negation among the resources to aim at. Step 5, RAA: assume ¬P; the only negation among the resources is now ¬P, and a clash with it needs P, the goal again. LEM on P: the case ¬P needs P again. LEM on Q: the case ¬Q clashes with line 2, and the case Q is where we started. Step 5 at the goal further out, an RAA box on ¬(Q→P) around the whole search: a clash with it needs Q→P, the goal again, and step 2 opens the same box on Q with the same goal P. Every route returns to one gap, “P from P→Q and Q”. That is affirming the consequent (Section 1.3), the step that would repair Argument B.

But notice what we have not shown. A search that fails proves nothing about derivations in general. An assumption may be any statement at all, by the assumption clause of Definition 1.4.2, so there are infinitely many derivations to consider, and a cleverer one might exist. To show that no derivation of Q→P from P→Q exists, we need a different tool.

That is the question this chapter leaves open. We show that something is derivable by exhibiting a derivation; how could we ever show that something is not? Chapter 3 answers it (Corollary 3.5.3). It gives each statement a truth value and proves that the rules never lead from true premises to a false conclusion; the assignment with P false and Q true, which makes P→Q true and Q→P false, then settles this search for good.

2.6.9. A mathematical goal

The method reads the frame of a proof off the goal whether the statements are letters or facts about numbers. In ordinary mathematics its payoff is that the first and last sentences of a proof, and its division into parts, are fixed before any mathematics is done.

Example (Building a skeleton)

Let m and n be integers. Prove that if mn is odd, then m is odd and n is odd.

Solution. The skeleton. The main connective is “if … then”, so step 2 opens a →I box: “Assume mn is odd. … Hence m is odd and n is odd.” The new goal is a conjunction, so step 2 again: two separate proofs, one that m is odd and one that n is odd. Each is a letter as far as logic can see, and the one resource, “mn is odd”, cannot be taken apart. So step 3 brings in facts we can compute with: “if m is even then mn is even” is one, and CP (Proposition 2.3.2) turns it into “if mn is not even then m is not even”, which the resource can feed. Then “every integer is even or odd” and DS finish.

The proof. Assume mn is odd. We show that m is odd and that n is odd.

First, if m is even, say m=2k with k an integer, then mn=2(kn) is even. By the contrapositive (Proposition 2.3.2), if mn is not even, then m is not even. Now mn is odd, so it is not even, since no integer is both; hence m is not even. Since every integer is even or odd, m is odd, by Proposition 2.3.3.

Exchanging the roles of m and n in the previous paragraph shows that n is odd. Hence m and n are both odd.

What the method did. It fixed the first sentence, the two paragraphs, and the last sentence; the only computation is mn=2(kn). No step uses RAA: the classical-looking move from “m is not even” to “m is odd” is disjunctive syllogism on the fact that every integer is even or odd. (The phrase “say m=2k” names a witness, which is a rule for “there exists” from Chapter 4.)

Warning. Do not assume what you want to prove. To derive a goal φ, it is tempting to open a box with φ. The box reaches φ at once, but after it closes it gives φ→φ, not φ, and its assumption is gone. An assumption is useful when the goal asks for it: the hypothesis of an implication (step 2), the negated goal of RAA (step 5), a disjunct of a resource (step 3). Opening the box on the conclusion of an implication is the converse error of Argument B, diagnosed in Section 1.4.

2.6.11. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

For (a)–(d), name the goal’s main connective, the first rule the method aims for, and the first sentence of a proof. For (e)–(f), name the elimination rule the premise invites, and what it needs.

  • (a) For real x: if x3<0 then x<0.
  • (b) There is no integer n with 2n=7.
  • (c) For real x and y: |xy|=|x||y| and |x+y|≤|x|+|y|.
  • (d) For an integer n: n is odd if and only if n2 is odd.
  • (e) The premise (P∧Q)→R, with the goal R and the resources P and Q.
  • (f) The premise ¬(P∨Q), with the goal ⊥ and the resource Q.

Solution.

  • (a) →; →I: “Assume x3<0.” (Starting “Suppose x≥0” inside that frame is an RAA box on the goal x<0, not the contrapositive; a proof through the contrapositive replaces the frame, proving “if x≥0 then x3≥0” and then applying CP.)

  • (b) ¬, since the claim is “not: there is an integer n with 2n=7”; ¬I: “Suppose n is an integer with 2n=7.” (Naming n uses a rule for “there exists”, from Chapter 4.)

  • (c) ∧; ∧I: “We prove the two statements in turn.”

  • (d) ↔; ↔I: “(⇒) Assume n is odd.”

  • (e) →E, which needs the hypothesis P∧Q: make it a new goal, reached by ∧I from P and Q; then →E gives R.

  • (f) ¬E, which needs P∨Q beside ¬(P∨Q): make P∨Q the new goal (step 4), reached by ∨I from Q.

Exercise (C3: Exit check: search)

Find a derivation of ¬(¬P∧Q)⊢P∨¬Q by the method of this section. Write one sentence for each step of the method you take, and point to the classical step of your derivation.

Solution. The search.

  1. Step 1. The premise ¬(¬P∧Q) on top, the goal P∨¬Q at the bottom, one gap.
  2. Step 2. The goal is a disjunction, so it is postponed: the premise does not say which side holds.
  3. Step 3. Nothing can be eliminated. The premise is a negation, so it can be used only in a clash (step 4), against ¬P∧Q, and we do not have it.
  4. Step 4. The goal is not ⊥, and nothing clashes. We are stuck.
  5. Step 5. We assume the negation of the goal, ¬(P∨¬Q), with the new goal ⊥.
  6. Step 4, inside the box. The new assumption clashes with P∨¬Q, which ∨I builds from either side. The side P is a letter that nothing gives; the side ¬Q is a negation, which step 2 can build. So ¬Q becomes the goal: a box on Q, with the goal ⊥. Choosing a side is safe here, since the disjunction is manufactured for a clash with a resource that is its negation (see the paragraph on choosing a side of ∨, after the method).
  7. Step 4, in the box on Q. The resources are the premise, the assumption of step 5, and Q. We aim at the premise: ¬P∧Q becomes the goal, and step 2 splits it. Its half Q is a resource; its half ¬P is a negation: a box on P, with the goal ⊥.
  8. Step 4, in the box on P. ∨I builds P∨¬Q from P, which clashes with the assumption of step 5. Now the boxes close from the inside out: ¬P, then ¬P∧Q, which clashes with the premise; then ¬Q, then P∨¬Q, which clashes with the assumption of step 5; and RAA closes the outer box.

R. Review

Exercise (R1)

For real numbers a and b, let max(a,b):=a and min(a,b):=b if a≥b, and max(a,b):=b and min(a,b):=a if a<b. Prove that max(a,b)+min(a,b)=a+b.

Solution. Since a≥b or a<b, and these two cases cover every pair of real numbers, we argue by cases (∨E, Definition 1.6.1), as in Example (The absolute value of a product).

Case a≥b. Then max(a,b)=a and min(a,b)=b, so max(a,b)+min(a,b)=a+b.

Case a<b. Then max(a,b)=b and min(a,b)=a, so max(a,b)+min(a,b)=b+a=a+b.

In either case the equation holds, which proves it. Both cases end in the same statement, the goal, as ∨E requires.

Exercise (R2)

Let x be a real number. Write N for “x<0”, G for “x>1”, and S for “x=7”. Translate the argument below into a sequent in these letters, and derive it.

In both boxes ∨I builds N∨G, which clashes with the premise; in the second box ⊥E then gives S.

Exercise (R3)

Is (P→Q)→R⊢P→(Q→R) derivable? And is its converse, P→(Q→R)⊢(P→Q)→R?

Solution. The first is derivable, by the search method of this section. The goal gives a box on P, then a box on Q, with the goal R. The premise gives R by →E once we produce P→Q, a new goal: a box on P whose last line repeats Q.

The repetition at line 5 puts Q inside the box of line 4, as →I requires (clause (D3) of Definition 1.4.2). Two look-alike tools do not apply. Proposition 1.4.2 moves a conjunction in and out of a hypothesis, (P∧Q)→R⊣⊢P→(Q→R), and the premise here has P→Q, not P∧Q, in that place. And Proposition 1.4.1 chains two implications, which we do not have.

The converse is not derivable, but a search can only stall, not show it. With P and R false (and Q either way), the premise P→(Q→R) is true because P is false, while P→Q is true and R is false, so the conclusion is false. Chapter 3 proves that such a row rules out every derivation (Corollary 3.5.3): the chapter’s open question, in a new place.
