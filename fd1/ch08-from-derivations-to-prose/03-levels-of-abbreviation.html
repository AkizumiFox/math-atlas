8.3  Four Levels of One Proof

Sections 8.1 and 8.2 matched each rule with the phrases that carry it. A dictionary works one word at a time; a proof is read and written whole. This section puts the dictionary to work on two complete proofs, the irrationality of 2 and “uniformly continuous implies continuous”, and writes each of them four times: as a full derivation, as a skeleton, as prose with every rule tagged, and as the plain prose of a textbook. The four texts are one proof written for four different readers. The section names the four levels, fixes exactly which steps the last level may leave unsaid, and ends with the use of the levels that matters most to a reader working alone: when a sentence looks doubtful, rewrite it, and only it, at level 1, as a skeleton, and look.

8.3.1. One proof, four texts

The book has already printed some proofs twice. In Section 2.2 the proof of Theorem 2.2.1 appears as a paragraph with a rule in brackets after several of its steps, and then, below it, as a 13-line derivation of its propositional part. In Section 6.4, Example (Uniformly continuous implies continuous) is an 11-line derivation, followed by three sentences of prose with line numbers in brackets. In both places the derivation and the paragraph say the same thing, and the reader is asked to match them. What was not said is how many such texts there are, what each leaves out, and who each is for.

There are four, and the differences between them are not matters of taste.

A derivation, a skeleton and a paragraph are the same proof; they differ in what the reader is trusted to restore.

Definition 8.3.1 (Levels of Proof)

A proof can be written at four levels.

  • Level 0, the full derivation. A derivation in the sense of Definition 1.4.2, with the rules of Chapters 1, 2 and 5: every line is a premise, an assumption, or one rule applied to available lines. A derived rule used as one line (Definition 2.3.1), such as the bounded rules of Proposition 6.1.1, counts as one rule. Facts about numbers enter as premises, or as lines justified “arith”, each of which records one such fact applied to the lines it cites.
  • Level 1, the skeleton. The boxes and the logical lines only: every assumption, every flagged name, every line that opens or closes a box, with the rule that closes it, and every line that such a closing line cites. Each stretch of mathematics between them is compressed into one line, justified “arith”, “by def”, or by a cited result; a stretch of routine logical steps inside one box is compressed into one line justified by the names of its rules in order, such as “∀E, ∃I”.
  • Level 2, annotated prose. Sentences, each tagged in brackets with the rule or rules it carries, such as [∀I] or [∃E].
  • Level 3, plain prose. Sentences without tags, in which some steps are left unsaid. Which steps may be left unsaid is fixed by the convention that follows, not by the writer.

In words: level 0 hides no logic. Every line other than an arith line can be checked mechanically against the schema of its rule, and each arith line records one fact about numbers that can be checked by hand. Level 1 keeps the logical architecture and lets the arithmetic run in blocks. Level 2 is level 1 turned into sentences, with the architecture still labeled. Level 3 removes the labels and some of the lines, following a fixed convention.

The levels are already on the page in earlier chapters. The derivation in Section 6.4 is level 0. The paragraph after Theorem 2.2.1, with its tags [→E], [∧I], [¬E], [¬I], is level 2 for the rules Chapter 2 had; its quantifier steps carry no tags. The derivation printed below that paragraph is not quite level 0, and the reason is instructive: it fixes r, p and q in advance and leaves out the two quantifier steps, choosing p, q (and later m) and passing from one r to every r, because Chapter 2 had no rules for them yet. Section 2.2 says so in the paragraph after the derivation. With Chapter 5’s rules those steps can be written. The example below writes the choices of p and q and the step to every r; the choice of m stays inside the arithmetic fact of its line 15, since evenness is treated there as a single predicate E.

A non-example, by one change: take a level-1 skeleton and delete one of its boxes, say the box in which a name N0 is chosen, keeping the lines inside. The result is not a shorter skeleton. It is a list in which N0 appears from nowhere, and no reader can tell whether it was chosen, given, or assumed. A skeleton may compress the work inside a box; it may not compress the boxes.

Why four levels and not two? Because each serves a task. Level 0 is for settling a doubt completely: nothing in it is taken on trust. Level 1 is for planning a proof and for reading one: it is what you write on scratch paper before the first sentence (Section 8.5 turns this into a procedure), and what you reconstruct from a textbook when a proof will not come clear (Section 8.4). Level 2 is for learning, which is why this volume and Sets, Relations, and Cardinality, Chapters 1–3 use it and later volumes do not: from Analysis on the Real Line, Chapter 1 on, proofs are written at level 3, in plain prose, and a rule tag appears only where the rule is itself the point, as in a review exercise that looks back at those chapters. Level 3 is what mathematicians read and write.

Warning. A skeleton is not a finished proof. A level-1 text with lines such as “|x0−y|<δ0→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0, arith” is a plan: it says that a computation goes there, and it can hide a computation that does not work. For f(x)=x2 on R, a skeleton of “f is uniformly continuous” with one line “arith” at that point looks exactly like a correct skeleton for f(x)=3x+1, where δ=ε/3 works because |(3x+1)−(3y+1)|=3|x−y|; the claim is false for the first function (Example (The square function on the real line)), and only writing out the arithmetic shows where it fails. Expand every “arith” line before calling the proof done.

8.3.2. Which steps may be silent

Level 3 leaves things out. If it left out whatever a writer happened to find obvious, a reader could never tell an omission from a gap. So the book fixes the omissions once, by rule. The principle behind the list: a step may go unsaid when a reader who knows the rules can restore it uniquely from what is written. A step that creates something (a name, a witness, a case split), assumes something, or cites something cannot be restored that way, so it is always written.

Silent steps are the ones whose result is forced by the lines around them; every choice, assumption and citation is spoken.

Definition 8.3.2 (Silent Steps)

In level-3 prose the following steps may be silent, that is, left unwritten:

  • ∧I when both conjuncts are displayed, and ∧E;
  • R;
  • ∨I;
  • ⊥E, once the contradiction or the empty case has been stated;
  • the name of DS (not the fact it uses);
  • the exchange of the two members of a disjunction or of a conjunction (Example (First derivations with “or”), Proposition 1.2.1), made only to match the schema of a rule, with no citation;
  • ↔E with a definition (“that is”, “by definition”, or nothing);
  • ∀E at the obvious term;
  • →E whose implication is a line already in the proof that is neither a hypothesis of the statement, nor a cited result, nor an instance of one (for instance, an instance of the property of a chosen name), when its antecedent is displayed or has just been assumed;
  • =I and =E inside a chain of equalities or inequalities;
  • the phrase “it suffices to show”, when the claim of the cut is displayed and then used;
  • the closing lines of →I (“hence if P then Q”), of ∀I (“since x was arbitrary”), of ∨E (“in either case”) and of ∃E (the conclusion repeated outside the box of the chosen name).

The following are never silent:

  • the openings of →I, ¬I, RAA and ∀I, and of ∃E with a fresh name;
  • the conclusions of ¬I and of RAA (“hence ¬P”, “hence P”);
  • a ¬¬E or LEM step, written as an explicit case split or as an explicit removal of a double negation (Proposition 2.5.1 (b); “P must hold, since …”), with the flag of Section 8.7 when the step is genuinely non-constructive;
  • the case split of ∨E, with the reason the cases exhaust;
  • the clash of ¬E: both members of the clashing pair are named;
  • the witness of ∃I, with what it depends on;
  • an instance of ∀E at a term that is not the obvious one;
  • every citation, and so every →E whose implication is a hypothesis of the statement or a cited result, or an instance of one;
  • both directions of ↔I, and both halves of an ∃! proof;
  • the base case and the step of an induction, with the induction hypothesis stated;
  • the announcement of a contrapositive;
  • the negated form, after De Morgan (Proposition 2.5.5) or a quantifier negation (Proposition 6.2.1, Proposition 6.2.3);
  • the claim of a cut;
  • a “without loss of generality”, together with the symmetry that licenses it.

The two lists collect the last column of the dictionary in Sections 8.1 and 8.2 in one place, together with the requirements stated in the text under its tables: the reason a case split exhausts and the flag on a genuinely non-constructive step (Section 8.1), and chains of inequalities as well as equalities (Section 8.2). The dictionary gives each row its phrase and an example, and this definition gives the reader one list to check against. Two items are made explicit here that the dictionary leaves implicit. The first is the →E whose implication is a line already in the proof rather than a citation, as in “for every n≥N” after N is chosen. The second is the closing line of ∃E, which the dictionary’s row for ∃E leaves implicit: when a proof says “choose N such that …” and goes on to prove something that does not mention N, the ∃E line that carries that conclusion outside the box of N is never written in prose. What is written is the choice, with its fresh name.

Read the two lists against the principle. Everything in the first list is forced. ∧E has one premise and two possible conclusions, and the next sentence shows which was used. The closing line of a box is determined by the box. ∀E at the obvious term x, when x is the name in sight, is forced by the sentence that uses it. Everything in the second list carries information that the surrounding lines do not determine. Which name is chosen, and that it is fresh; which witness, and what it depends on; which cases, and why they are all the cases; which result is cited. A reader who meets “Then p is even” with no citation has to guess what licensed it, and a guess is not a check.

A small example. In “since x∈(0,1), in particular x>0”, the step from the conjunction “0<x and x<1” to its first conjunct is ∧E, and the unwinding of x∈(0,1) into that conjunction is ↔E with the definition of an interval. Both may be silent, and here the writer kept the phrase “in particular” anyway, which is allowed: the convention says what may be omitted, never what must be. On the other side, “suppose r2=2” can never be omitted from the proof of Theorem 2.2.1: without it, the reader does not know that a box has opened, and the final “r2≠2” has no visible source.

Warning. Level 3 is not level 0 with steps missing at random. A proof that skips the reason a case split is exhaustive, or chooses δ without saying what it depends on, or uses a lemma without naming it, is not “written at level 3”: it has a gap, because those steps are in the never-silent list. Conversely, a proof that omits “since ε was arbitrary” has no gap. When you judge whether a written proof is complete, judge it against Definition 8.3.2, not against your sense of what is obvious.

8.3.3. The irrationality of the square root of two, four times

The first example is the proof of Theorem 2.2.1, now with every quantifier step written. It uses the fact that Section 2.2 calls (L): every rational number is p/q for integers p, q with q≠0, not both even. Write E(x) for “x is even”. The proof uses only two clauses of (L), and in a convenient form: r=p/q with q≠0 gives p=r⋅q, which avoids division; and “not both even” is ¬(E(p)∧E(q)). The clause q≠0 is then not needed at all, since q=0 would force p=0, and both would be even. It also uses Theorem 2.1.3, which we write as the formula ∀n∈Z(E(n⋅n)→E(n)).

Example (The irrationality of the square root of two at four levels)

Write the proof of Theorem 2.2.1, that no rational number has square 2, at levels 0, 1, 2 and 3 of Definition 8.3.1, and match the levels line by line.

Idea. The move this example shows in full is skeleton first: before any arithmetic, write the first and the last line of every box. The goal ∀r∈Q¬(r⋅r=2) dictates the outer box (∀I, “let r∈Q”) and the next one (¬I, “suppose r2=2”, ending in ⊥). Inside, (L) instantiated at r0 is an existential, so its ∃E boxes open at once, with fresh names p0, q0, and they too end in ⊥. Only then is there something to compute: the arithmetic fills the innermost box, and it has a target, the clash with “not both even”. Sections 8.5 and 8.6 come back to this move twice, with parts of the proof left blank.

Before reading on: the level-3 proof below has four sentences. Which lines of the level-0 derivation does its second sentence, “By (L), write r=p/q …”, stand for?

Level 2.

Let r∈Q [∀I: the box of r0], and suppose r2=2 [assumption for ¬I]. By (L), there are integers p and q with p=rq, not both even; choose such p and q [∀E at r, then ∃E twice, with fresh names]. Then p2=r2q2=2q2 [arith], so p2 is even [arith], and by Theorem 2.1.3 p is even [∀E at p, →E]. Writing p=2m, we get 4m2=2q2, so q2=2m2 is even [arith], and by the same theorem q is even [∀E at q, →E]. So p and q are both even [∧I], while they were chosen not both even: a contradiction [¬E; then ∃E twice, since ⊥ mentions neither p nor q]. Hence r2≠2 [¬I]. Since r was arbitrary, no rational number has square 2 [∀I].

Level 3.

Let r be rational, and suppose r2=2. By (L), write r=p/q with integers p, q, not both even. Then p2=2q2, so p2 is even, and p is even by Theorem 2.1.3; writing p=2m gives q2=2m2, so q is even by the same theorem. Thus p and q are both even, a contradiction; hence r2≠2.

The answer to the prompt: “By (L), write r=p/q with integers p, q, not both even” stands for line 5 (∀E of premise 1 at r0) and for the openings of the two ∃E boxes, lines 6–9. It also displays the conjunct, in the form r=p/q, on its own, so the ∧E of line 10 that extracts it has nothing left to say and can be silent.

Look at what the four texts share. Every level has the box of r, the box of the assumption r2=2, the choice of p and q, two uses of the lemma, and a contradiction followed by “hence r2≠2”. That common part is the proof; the rest is how much of the routine work each reader is trusted to do. The skeleton is the place where you can see it most clearly: fifteen lines, of which only lines 9 and 10 carry the mathematics; every other line is a decision about logic.

Quick check. Which lines of the level-0 derivation in Example (The irrationality of the square root of two at four levels) have no counterpart in the level-3 text, and which item of Definition 8.3.2 lets each go?

Solution. Eight lines. Line 5 is ∀E of premise 1 at the obvious term r0, the name in sight; “By (L)” cites premise 1, and the instance is not written. Lines 10 and 19 are ∧E. Lines 13 and 16 are ∀E of the lemma at the obvious terms p0 and q0; the citations “by Theorem 2.1.3” and “by the same theorem” remain, and they are the →E lines 14 and 17. Lines 21 and 22 are the closing lines of the two ∃E boxes. Line 24 is the closing line of ∀I: level 3 never says “since r was arbitrary”. Every other line has a phrase: lines 1 and 2, the premises, are the citations “By (L)” and “by Theorem 2.1.3”; lines 3, 4, 6–9 the openings; lines 11, 12, 15 the arithmetic; lines 14, 17 the citations; lines 18 and 20 the clash, “both even, a contradiction”, which names the pair through “not both even” two sentences earlier; and line 23, “hence r2≠2”, the conclusion of ¬I, which is never silent.

8.3.4. Uniform continuity, four times

The second example has no arithmetic at all. That makes it a good test of the levels: what is left when there is nothing to compress?

Example (Uniformly continuous implies continuous, at four levels)

Write the proof of Example (Uniformly continuous implies continuous), that a function uniformly continuous on A is continuous at every point of A, at levels 0, 1, 2 and 3, and say where each level records what δ depends on.

Solution. Level 0 is the derivation of Example (Uniformly continuous implies continuous), copied here with the same line numbers. Line 1 is uniform continuity (UC), line 11 is continuity at every point (C).

1∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε)
2x0x0∈Aasm, for ∀I
3ε0ε0>0asm, for ∀I

5δ0δ0>0asm, for ∃E
6∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ0→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε0)asm, for ∃E
7∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ0→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)∀E 6, 2
8∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)∃I 5, 7
9∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)∃E 4, 5–8

Level 1. Every line of level 0 other than the premise opens or closes a box, or is cited by a closing line, except line 7: lines 7 and 8, which specialize the chosen δ0 to the point x0 and repackage the result, are the one stretch of work inside a box. They become one line.

1∀ε>0∃δ>0∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε)
2x0x0∈Aasm, for ∀I
3ε0ε0>0asm, for ∀I
4∃δ>0∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε0)∀E 1, 3
5δ0δ0>0asm, for ∃E
6∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ0→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε0)asm, for ∃E

8∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)∃E 4, 5–7
9∀ε>0∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε)∀I 3–8
10∀x∈A∀ε>0∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε)∀I 2–9

Level 2.

Let x∈A [∀I] and let ε>0 [∀I]. By uniform continuity, applied at ε [∀E], there is δ>0 such that |u−v|<δ implies |f(u)−f(v)|<ε for all u,v∈A; choose such a δ, which depends on ε only [∃E]. Taking u=x [∀E], every y∈A with |x−y|<δ has |f(x)−f(y)|<ε. So this δ works for x and ε [∃I; then ∃E closes, since “some δ works” does not mention the chosen one]. Since ε and x were arbitrary, f is continuous at every point of A [∀I twice].

As in Section 6.4, the prose renames the bound variables of UC to u, v, so that x can name the fixed point.

Level 3.

Let x∈A and ε>0. By uniform continuity, choose δ>0 (depending on ε only) such that |f(u)−f(v)|<ε for all u,v∈A with |u−v|<δ. In particular |f(x)−f(y)|<ε for every y∈A with |x−y|<δ, so this δ works for x and ε.

Where the dependence of δ is recorded. At level 0, in two places: the box of δ0 lies inside the boxes of x0 and ε0, so δ0 may depend on both; and the line it comes from, line 4, mentions ε0 and not x0, so it does depend only on ε0 (Section 6.4). At level 1 the record is the same, lines 3 to 5 being unchanged. At level 2 it is the clause “which depends on ε only”, placed at the ∃E tag, after the ∀E at ε that produced it. At level 3 it is the parenthesis “(depending on ε only)”, and nothing else: the parenthesis is the whole of the box structure that survives.

Here the gap between level 0 and level 1 is one line, because there is no arithmetic to compress. The large step is from level 1 to level 3: ten lines become three sentences. What the level-3 text keeps is exactly the never-silent part of Definition 8.3.2: the openings of the two ∀I boxes (“let x∈A and ε>0”), the citation and the choice with its fresh name (“by uniform continuity, choose δ”), the dependence of the witness, and the witness itself (“this δ works”). The ∀E at u=x is the obvious instance and is reduced to “in particular”. The ∃E closing line 9 and the two ∀I closing lines 10 and 11 of level 0 are silent.

8.3.5. Expand to check

The levels are not only a way to write. They are a way to check. When a sentence of a level-3 proof leaves you uneasy, rewrite that stretch, and only that stretch, at level 1. Either every line of the expansion is legal, and the doubt is settled, or one line breaks a rule, and you have found the error and its exact address.

Here is a sentence that sounds like the level-3 proof above run backwards, from continuity to uniform continuity.

Let ε>0. For each x∈A, continuity at x gives some δ>0 such that |f(x)−f(y)|<ε for every y∈A with |x−y|<δ. Hence, choosing δ as above for every x, we have |f(x)−f(y)|<ε whenever x,y∈A and |x−y|<δ, so f is uniformly continuous on A.

Expand it. “Let ε>0” opens a box for ε0. “For each x∈A” opens a box for x0. “Continuity at x gives some δ” is ∀E twice and the opening of an ∃E box for δ0. At level 1, with the line the sentence needs as line 7, its rule marked with a question mark:

1∀x∈A∀ε>0∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε)
2ε0ε0>0
3x0x0∈A
4∃δ>0∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)∀E twice 1, 3, 2
5δ0δ0>0
6∀y∈A(|x0−y|<δ0→|f(x0)−f(y)|<ε0)
7∀x∈A∀y∈A(|x−y|<δ0→|f(x)−f(y)|<ε0)∀I? 3–6

Line 7 is what the phrase “choosing δ as above for every x” needs: one δ0 that works for all x and y, so that ∃I can then produce UC’s ∃δ inside the box of ε0. But line 7 would be a ∀I over x0, and no such step is legal here. The box of x0 opened at line 3 is still open, so ∀I cannot cite it; and a new box with the flag x0, opened inside the box of δ0, breaks the side condition of ∀I (Definition 5.4.1), because x0 appears in the open assumptions on lines 3 and 6. The phrase “for every x” has generalized over a name on which the chosen δ0 depends. This is the first blocked line of Example (The search from C to UC), met there in the search; here it was found by expanding one English sentence. Section 8.6 catalogues the error, and Example (The square function on the real line) shows that no repair is possible: for x2 on R the conclusion is false.

The expansion took six lines, and it was enough to expand the one doubtful stretch. That is the general pattern: you never need to rewrite a whole proof at level 0 to check it. Find the sentence you do not trust, find the boxes open at that sentence, and write the few lines it stands for.

8.3.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

Parts (a)–(c) are about Example (Uniformly continuous implies continuous, at four levels), parts (d) and (e) about Example (The irrationality of the square root of two at four levels).

  • (a) Which lines of the level-0 derivation does the level-3 sentence “Let x∈A and ε>0” stand for? Which of them open a box and which close one, and which are written at level 3?
  • (b) Where is the dependence of δ recorded at each of the four levels?
  • (c) State the idea of the proof in two sentences.
  • (d) At which line of each of the four levels is the “not both even” clause of (L) used to reach the contradiction?
  • (e) True or false, with a reason: at level 3, the sentence “hence r2≠2” may be left out of the proof of Theorem 2.2.1, since the reader has just been told that a contradiction was reached.

Solution. (a) Lines 2, 3, 10 and 11. Lines 2 and 3 open the flagged boxes of x0 and ε0, with their bounds x0∈A and ε0>0; lines 10 and 11 close them by ∀I. The openings are written (“let”); the closings are silent at level 3 (Definition 8.3.2), and at level 2 they are the sentence “since ε and x were arbitrary”.

(b) At level 0 and level 1: the box of δ0 sits inside the boxes of x0 and ε0, and the line it comes from (line 4) mentions ε0 only. At level 2: the clause “which depends on ε only” at the ∃E tag. At level 3: the parenthesis “(depending on ε only)”.

(c) Uniform continuity at ε provides one δ that serves all pairs of points of A at once, and it provides it before any point is named. Continuity at a point x asks only for the pairs whose first point is x, so the same δ works.

(d) Level 0: the clause is line 9’s second conjunct, taken out by ∧E at line 19 and used in the ¬E of line 20. Level 1: it is the second conjunct of the assumption on line 8, used at line 11. Level 2: “while they were chosen not both even: a contradiction”. Level 3: “Thus p and q are both even, a contradiction”, where the clash refers back to “not both even” in the sentence that chose p and q.

(e) False. “Hence r2≠2” is the conclusion of ¬I, which Definition 8.3.2 puts among the steps that are never silent. A contradiction alone does not say which assumption it refutes; in this proof the boxes of p, q and of r2=2 are all open when it is reached, and the sentence says which box closes and what it gives.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Some-all gives all-some, on the natural numbers)

Read Proposition 5.6.2 over the domain N, with R(x,y) as “x≤y”: from ∃x∀y(x≤y), conclude ∀y∃x(x≤y). The premise is true, with the witness x=0. Write the proof at levels 1, 2 and 3. Your proof must use the premise, although this instance of the conclusion also has a one-line proof, with x=y.

Solution. Level 1. The proof has no arithmetic, so the skeleton is the level-0 derivation of Proposition 5.6.2 with R(x,y) read as x≤y:

1∃x∀y(x≤y)
2y0x0∀y(x0≤y)asm, for ∃E

4∃x(x≤y0)∃I 3
5∃x(x≤y0)∃E 1, 2–4
6∀y∃x(x≤y)∀I 2–5

Level 2.

Let y∈N [∀I]. By hypothesis, some natural number is at most every natural number; choose such a number x0, which depends on nothing, since the hypothesis mentions no y [∃E, x0 fresh]. In particular x0≤y [∀E at y], so some natural number is at most y [∃I with witness x0; then ∃E closes, since this does not mention x0]. Since y was arbitrary, every natural number has a natural number at most it [∀I].

Level 3.

Let y∈N. By hypothesis, choose x0∈N with x0≤z for every z∈N; the hypothesis does not mention y, so x0 does not depend on y. In particular x0≤y, so x0 is a natural number at most y.

The level-3 text renames the bound variable of the hypothesis to z, so that y can name the fixed number. The witness x0 was chosen inside the box of y0, but it comes from line 1, which mentions no y; so it does not depend on y. The one-line proof, “y≤y, so take x=y”, is ∃I with the witness y, which does depend on y. Both prove the conclusion; only the first is an instance of Proposition 5.6.2, and only the first shows that a single x serves every y.

Exercise (B2: Back from prose to a skeleton)

Here is a level-3 proof.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Arbitrarily close numbers, at two levels)

Consider Proposition 6.2.6: for real numbers a and b, if |a−b|<ε for every ε>0, then a=b.

  • (a) Write its proof at level 1, as a skeleton in which school facts about |⋅| and < are lines justified “arith”.
  • (b) Write it at level 3.
  • (c) Which lines of your skeleton are silent at level 3? Which instance of ∀E must the level-3 text name, and why?

Solution. (a) The statement is an implication, so the outer box is →I; the goal a=b is atomic, and the proof in Section 6.2 opens an RAA box on a≠b.

1∀ε>0(|a−b|<ε)asm, for →I
2¬(a=b)asm, for RAA
3|a−b|>0arith 2
4|a−b|<|a−b|∀E 1, 3
5¬(|a−b|<|a−b|)arith
6⊥¬E 4, 5
7a=bRAA 2–6
8∀ε>0(|a−b|<ε)→a=b→I 1–7

Line 4 is the bounded ∀E at the term |a−b|, whose bound |a−b|>0 is line 3. Line 5 is the school fact that no real number is less than itself.

(b)

Suppose |a−b|<ε for every ε>0, and suppose, for a contradiction, that a≠b. Then |a−b|>0, so the hypothesis applies with ε=|a−b| and gives |a−b|<|a−b|; but no real number is less than itself. Hence a=b.

(c) Only line 8, the closing line of →I, is silent: “hence if … then a=b” is not written. Line 5 is not silent: it is one member of the clashing pair of the ¬E on line 6, and level 3 names it (“no real number is less than itself”). The instance of line 4 must be named, ε=|a−b|, because it is not the obvious term: no name in sight is the obvious candidate for ε, and the whole proof turns on choosing this one. Saying only “by the hypothesis” would leave the reader to find the instance, and that instance is the idea of the proof. The RAA opening, “suppose, for a contradiction, that a≠b”, and its conclusion, “hence a=b”, are also never silent.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: (a) the →I box on the hypothesis, and inside it a box opened on a≠b. - 2: (a) the box closed by RAA (¬I alone would give only ¬¬(a=b)), with ⊥ as its last line. - 2: (a) the ∀E at |a−b|, citing a line |a−b|>0. - 2: (b) a level-3 text with the opening of the contradiction, the instance, the clash and “hence a=b”, and no tags. - 2: (c) the silent line identified, and the instance ε=|a−b| said to be named because it is not the obvious term. - Other routes: a ¬I box on a≠b followed by ¬¬E, or a split on |a−b|>0 or |a−b|=0 (arith) whose first case reaches ⊥ by the same ∀E and clash and then a=b by ⊥E, while the second gives a=b by arith, earns the 4 points of the first two lines, provided the ¬¬E line is written explicitly, or the disjunction of the split is justified (from |a−b|≥0, or by trichotomy). In the split, the case assumption |a−b|>0 is the line the ∀E cites for the third line, and for (b) and (c) the opening of the ¬I box or of the split stands in for the opening of the contradiction. Fatal errors (0 for the step): the box closed by ¬I with the conclusion a=b; the hypothesis applied at an ε not known to be positive.
