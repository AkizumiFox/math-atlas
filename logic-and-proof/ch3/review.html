Chapter 3

Chapter Review

Summary

The search method reads the boxes off the goal and the elimination rules off the premises. Its goal-directed steps and its last-resort classical step never turn a gap that can be filled into one that cannot (Section 3.1, “Why the order loses nothing”). Postponing a disjunction, and four refinements, make the search shorter. Every task of Section 3.2 is a question about derivability. Every question about derivability is in turn a question about whether a list is inconsistent, that is, whether it derives ⊥ (Proposition 3.2.2). So deriving φ from Γ is the same as showing that the counterexample set Γ,¬φ is inconsistent. An inconsistent list derives every statement (Proposition 3.2.1). An argument in prose becomes a sequent once its unstated facts are written down, and the library of Section 3.3 sorts the standard sequents by what they are for.

What is left open: every method of the chapter can show that something is derivable, and none can show that something is not. A claim that breaks, or a search that stalls, has not been shown underivable. Chapter 4 answers that question.

Proof methods in this chapter

  • Work from both ends. Let the goal choose the boxes, let the premises choose the elimination rules, and keep the classical rules for when nothing else works. Introduced in Section 3.1.
  • Find the goal among the resources. If the current goal is already on an available line, cite it, or copy it by R. Introduced in Section 3.1.
  • Derive what needs nothing new before opening a box. A line written outside the boxes is available inside all of them. Introduced in Section 3.1.
  • Look first for a clash partner already on a line. When the goal is ⊥, aim at a statement whose negation you already have. Introduced in Section 3.1.
  • No detours. Never build a line by an introduction rule and then take it apart at once by the matching elimination rule. Introduced in Section 3.1.
  • Let the task choose the search. A theorem starts from the goal alone, an equivalence needs two derivations, and an inconsistent list needs a clash. Introduced in Section 3.2.
  • Try to break it first. Before searching, try to make every statement of the counterexample set true. If every attempt is forced into a clash, the attempt is usually a derivation read backwards. The statements it was forced to accept suggest the boxes, and its clash suggests the line ⊥. Introduced in Section 3.2.
  • Name the unstated fact. An argument in prose leaves some premises unsaid. Write each one down and check that it is true. Introduced in Section 3.3.
  • Two columns. Write the proof beside the derivation it follows, and see which lines the prose leaves out. Introduced in Section 3.3.

Terms

  • consistent list: a list from which ⊥ is not derivable (Definition 3.2.1).
  • counterexample set (from Section 1.1): the premises together with the negated conclusion (Definition 1.1.3).
  • current goal: the written line below a gap (Definition 3.1.1).
  • gap: a stretch of a partial derivation still missing (Definition 3.1.1).
  • inconsistent list: a list from which ⊥ is derivable (Definition 3.2.1).
  • partial derivation: a derivation with gaps still to fill (Definition 3.1.1).
  • provably equivalent (from Section 2.4): each derivable from the other (Definition 2.4.2).
  • resources: the lines available at a gap’s current goal (Definition 3.1.1).
  • theorem (of logic): a statement derivable from no premises, ⊢φ (Definition 1.2.3).
