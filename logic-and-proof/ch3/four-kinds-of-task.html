Four Kinds of Task

The method of Section 3.1 answers one kind of question: here are premises, here is a conclusion, find a derivation. Mathematics asks others as well:

  • prove a statement from nothing;
  • show that two statements say the same thing;
  • show that some conditions cannot all be met at once;
  • decide whether a conclusion follows at all, when nobody says whether it does.

Each of these changes where a search starts and what counts as its end. This section takes the four tasks in turn, and shows that underneath they are one question.

3.2.1 Four tasks, one method

Let the task choose the search. The kind of task decides where the search starts. The four tasks use the same rules and the same method; what differs is the first step and the place where the search ends.

Task The claim Where the search starts What ends it
theorem ⊢φ the goal-directed step (step 2) on the goal; every resource is an assumption the goal asks for the goal, reached outside every box
equivalence φ⊣⊢ψ (Definition 2.4.2) two searches, one from each side two derivations
inconsistency Γ⊢⊥ the clash step (step 4): the goal is ⊥; look for a clash partner already on a line ⊥, outside every box
derive or break Γ⊢φ? (verdict withheld) try to break it first a derivation, or statements that make the counterexample set true

Only the last task leaves open which way it ends. The other three state their claim: a failed search leaves the question open, as Section 3.1’s failed search did, and a break ends the task against the claim.

Quick check. Which task is each? (i) ⊢P∨¬P. (ii) P∧Q⊣⊢Q∧P. (iii) Show that P, ¬P∨Q and ¬Q cannot all be accepted together. (iv) Does P→Q, Q⊢P hold?

Solution.

  • (i) A theorem. (ii) An equivalence. (iii) An inconsistency: the claim P, ¬P∨Q, ¬Q⊢⊥. (iv) Derive or break; it breaks, since it is affirming the consequent (Section 1.3).

3.2.2 Theorems

A theorem of logic is a statement derivable from no premises, ⊢φ (Definition 1.2.3 with the list empty).

Example (A statement that implies a contradiction)

Find a derivation of ⊢(P→(Q∧¬Q))→¬P.

In words: a statement that implies a contradiction is false. Every proof that begins “suppose P” and ends “which is impossible” has this shape.

Solution. There are no premises, so setting up (step 1) writes only the goal. The goal-directed step opens a box on P→(Q∧¬Q) with the goal ¬P, and then, inside it, a box on P with the goal ⊥:

A theorem has no premises, so the goal does all the early work. Once proved, it is a derived rule with nothing above the line (Definition 2.3.1): any derivation, from any premises, may write an instance of it as a line, as derivations already write LEM.

The direction (⇐) reached the stuck step, because its goal was a disjunction with no side in hand. When one direction is easy, do it first: it checks that the statements are worth comparing, and its lines often suggest the other direction’s split.

3.2.4 Inconsistent lists

Sometimes the task is not to reach a conclusion but to show that some statements cannot be accepted together:

  • the conditions of a puzzle that no number meets;
  • the hypotheses of a lemma that contradict each other;
  • the assumption of a proof by contradiction, together with what is given.

Section 2.5 (Exercise (B5: Derive or break, used later)) already read an RAA derivation as a derivation of ⊥ from the counterexample set. A derivation of ⊥ does this job.

Take the list P, ¬P∨Q, ¬Q from the start of the section. Split ¬P∨Q by ∨E: the case ¬P clashes with P, and the case Q clashes with ¬Q. Both cases end in ⊥, so a derivation from these three premises ends with ⊥, outside every box. Lists like this one get a name.

Definition 3.2.1 (Inconsistent and Consistent Lists)

Let Γ be a list of statements φ1,…,φn, where n≥0. The list Γ is inconsistent if Γ⊢⊥: some derivation whose premises all belong to Γ has ⊥ as its last line. It is consistent if it is not inconsistent, that is, if no derivation with premises in Γ ends with ⊥.

In words: a list is inconsistent when a derivation from it reaches ⊥ outside every box. The definition speaks only of derivations, and says nothing about whether the statements are true. The list P,¬P is inconsistent, by one ¬E line, and so is any list with ⊥ as a member. To show a list inconsistent, exhibit a derivation. To show one consistent, one would have to rule out every derivation, and nothing in this chapter can do that.

Proposition 3.2.1 (An Inconsistent List Proves Everything)

For a list of statements Γ, the following are equivalent:

  • (i) Γ is inconsistent;
  • (ii) Γ⊢ψ for every statement ψ;
  • (iii) Γ⊢χ and Γ⊢¬χ for some statement χ.

In words: an inconsistent list proves every statement, and a list that proves some statement and its negation is inconsistent. It extends Proposition 2.1.1 from the two premises P,¬P to any inconsistent list. The proof is short, and is given in the next subsection together with the proof of the proposition there.

Quick check. Without writing a derivation, explain why P, ¬P⊢Q∧¬Q.

Solution. The list P,¬P is inconsistent (one ¬E line), so by Proposition 3.2.1, (i) ⇒ (ii), it derives every statement, Q∧¬Q included.

Example (Four conditions that no integer meets)

Let n be an integer. A puzzle asks for an n such that:

  • (1) if n is even, then n is a multiple of 4;
  • (2) n is even or a multiple of 3;
  • (3) if n is a multiple of 3, then n is even;
  • (4) n is not a multiple of 4.

With E: “n is even”, F: “n is a multiple of 4” and T: “n is a multiple of 3”, show that the list E→F, E∨T, T→E, ¬F is inconsistent.

Solution. The goal is ⊥. Look first for a clash partner already on a line (Section 3.1): line 4 is ¬F, so aim at F.

Read as a proof about n, the derivation shows that no integer meets all four conditions. By (2) and (3), n is even in either case. By (1) it is a multiple of 4, and (4) says it is not. Yet any three of the conditions can be met together: n=6 meets all but (1), n=1 all but (2), n=3 all but (3), and n=12 all but (4). The inconsistency belongs to the four together. The derivation does not say which condition to drop: logic shows that the four cannot all hold, and it does not choose among them (Section 3.1).

Quick check. Which integers between 1 and 10 meet conditions (1), (2) and (4) of Example (Four conditions that no integer meets), and which condition does each break?

Solution. 3 and 9: they are odd, so (1) promises nothing; they are multiples of 3, so (2) holds; and they are not multiples of 4. Each breaks (3).

Warning (Not allowed: claims about inconsistency). Each claim below is wrong. The words in italics name the mistake.

  • “The list is inconsistent because one of its members is false.” Mistaking truth for derivation: inconsistency is a derivation of ⊥ (Definition 3.2.1), not a false member. The one-member list “2>3” has a false member, and no derivation of ⊥ is offered.
  • “P→Q and P→¬Q contradict each other, so the list is inconsistent.” Mistaking looks for a derivation: nothing has been derived. Both members are true when P is false, and the claim of inconsistency breaks.
  • “Line 7, inside the box of the assumption P, is ⊥; so the premises are inconsistent.” Ending inside a box: that ⊥ depends on P. It shows the list Γ,P inconsistent, and so Γ⊢¬P. The last line must lie outside every box (Definition 1.4.2).
  • “A search for ⊥ stalled, so the list is consistent.” Mistaking a stall for a proof: a stalled search proves nothing (Section 3.1). Consistency is the absence of every derivation of ⊥.
  • “The list is inconsistent, so its first member is false.” Mistaking the list for a member: inconsistency belongs to the list. It says that the members cannot all be accepted together, not which one fails (compare the four conditions above).

3.2.5 The counterexample set, derived

Proving and refuting meet in one list. The counterexample set of a claim Γ⊢φ is the list Γ,¬φ (Definition 1.1.3). Breaking the claim makes that list true. Deriving the claim, it turns out, is showing that list inconsistent.

For example, take the claim P∧Q⊢P. Its counterexample set is P∧Q, ¬P, and that list is inconsistent: ∧E gives P, which clashes with ¬P.

Proposition 3.2.2 (Derivable Exactly When the Counterexample Set Is Inconsistent)

Let Γ be a list of statements and φ a statement.

  • (a) Γ⊢¬φ if and only if the list Γ,φ is inconsistent.
  • (b) Γ⊢φ if and only if the list Γ,¬φ is inconsistent.

In words: (b) says that a claim is derivable exactly when its counterexample set is inconsistent, and (a) says the same for a negated conclusion. In the proofs, the box of (a) closes by ¬I and that of (b) by RAA: Section 2.5’s distinction between the two rules, stated about whole derivations. Section 1.1 tested a form by trying to break it, that is, by making the counterexample set true. Now there is a second test on the same list: derive ⊥ from it.

Take Γ empty and φ the statement P↔¬P. Then part (a) says that the second row of Example (Three inconsistent lists, and a look-alike) and the theorem ⊢¬(P↔¬P) are the same fact. That theorem is Lemma 2.4.2.

↺Second readingThe proofs: append a line, or copy a derivation into a box

Proof. Proof of Proposition 3.2.1. (i) ⇒ (ii): a derivation of ⊥ with premises in Γ, followed by the line ψ by ⊥E, is a derivation of ψ. Its last line lies in no box. (ii) ⇒ (iii): apply (ii) to any χ and to ¬χ. (iii) ⇒ (i): the sequent χ,¬χ⊢⊥ has a one-step derivation (¬E), so by Lemma 2.3.3 (c), Γ⊢⊥.

Proof. Proof of Proposition 3.2.2. (b), (⇒): from Γ⊢φ, Lemma 2.3.3 (a) gives Γ,¬φ⊢φ. The one-line derivation of the premise ¬φ gives Γ,¬φ⊢¬φ. By Lemma 2.3.3 (c) with φ,¬φ⊢⊥, we get Γ,¬φ⊢⊥.

(b), (⇐): let D be a derivation of ⊥ with premises in Γ,¬φ. If the last line of D is a premise in Γ, then ⊥ is a member of Γ, and ⊥E gives φ. (The last line cannot be the premise ¬φ, since ¬φ is not ⊥.) Otherwise, write as premises those premises of D that belong to Γ. Open a box with the assumption ¬φ. Inside it, copy the lines of D that are not premises, in order, with their boxes, renumbering citations; a citation of the premise ¬φ now cites the assumption. Close the box after the copy of the last line of D, and write φ by RAA.

Every copied citation is still allowed. A cited premise of Γ lies above the box, in no box. The assumption is available throughout the box. The boxes of D sit inside the new box as they sat in D, so every line and every box that was available at a citing line stays available, by the scope rule for derivations. The last line of D lies in no box of D, so its copy is a line of the new box itself, as RAA requires. If D never cites ¬φ, nothing changes.

Part (a) is the same, with φ in place of ¬φ and ¬I in place of RAA. If the last line of D is the premise φ itself (possible only when φ is ⊥), nothing is copied: the box is its one assumption line, and ¬I closes it at once. In (⇒), Γ,φ⊢φ and, by weakening, Γ,φ⊢¬φ; chaining with ¬E finishes.

Uses: Definition 1.4.2 (the scope rule).

The copying reverses what Section 1.4’s Exercise (C4: The assumptions a line depends on, used later) (b) does. That exercise turns open assumptions into premises; the copying turns a premise into an open assumption.

3.2.6 One task in four forms

Every task of this section is a claim that something is derivable, and by Proposition 3.2.2 every such claim is a claim that a list is inconsistent.

Task As a claim of derivability As a claim of inconsistency It breaks when some choice of statements makes
theorem ⊢φ the list ¬φ φ false
equivalence φ⊢ψ and ψ⊢φ the lists φ,¬ψ and ψ,¬φ one of φ, ψ true and the other false
inconsistency Γ⊢⊥ the list Γ every member of Γ true
derive or break Γ⊢φ the list Γ,¬φ every member of Γ,¬φ true

An equivalence is also a theorem: φ⊣⊢ψ holds exactly when ⊢φ↔ψ. Copy each of the two derivations into a box, as the proof of Proposition 3.2.2 does, and close both boxes by ↔I. Conversely, add the premise φ to a derivation of φ↔ψ and write ψ by ↔E, and likewise for ψ.

So the four tasks are one: is this list inconsistent? Each form of the task starts the search in a different place, so the kind of task decides how to search. Each row also has a second half, given in the last column: what to do when the list might instead be made true. That is the fourth task.

3.2.7 Derive or break

Section 1.5 gave claims without saying whether they hold, under the heading “Derive it or break it”. Such a claim is a task with its own first step: decide before you search.

Try to break it first. Spend a few minutes trying to make the counterexample set true, starting where the conclusion is false (Section 1.1, “Where to look first”). Either some choice of statements works, and the claim breaks; or every attempt is forced into a clash. Then search for a derivation. The forced attempt usually suggests a plan for it. The statements the attempt had to make true suggest the assumptions of the derivation’s boxes. The clash it met suggests the derivation’s ⊥.

Example (Two claims, one connective apart)

Derive or break each claim.

  • (a) P→(Q→R), P→¬R⊢P→¬Q.
  • (b) P→(Q∨R), P→¬R⊢P→¬Q.

Solution. (a), trying to break it. The conclusion is false only with P true and ¬Q false, that is, Q true. Then the first premise needs Q→R true, so R true. The second needs ¬R true, so R false. Every choice was forced, and the forcing ends in a clash. So we search.

(a), the derivation. The attempt gives the plan. Open a box on P, the hypothesis of the conclusion, and inside it a box on Q, the statement whose negation is wanted; then aim for the clash R, ¬R. Before the box on Q opens, both premises give a line from P by →E. These are lines that need nothing new (Section 3.1).

With statements about a real number x, take P: “x>0”, Q: “x>1”, R: “x>3”, at x=2. The claim breaks.

The two claims differ in one connective. Trying to break each found its verdict, and for (a) it also found the derivation. The method of Section 3.1 would have found (a) without the attempt. On (b) the method of Section 3.1 could only search, and a stall is not a verdict; the break is.

3.2.8 Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) When is a list of statements inconsistent?
  • (b) By Proposition 3.2.2, which list is inconsistent exactly when P→Q, Q→R⊢P→R?
  • (c) True or false: every list that has ⊥ as a member is inconsistent.
  • (d) True or false: if Γ is inconsistent, so is every list Γ,ψ.
  • (e) Name the task and the first rule to use: “show that P∧Q⊣⊢Q∧P”.

Solution.

  • (a) When ⊥ is derivable from it (Definition 3.2.1).

  • (b) The counterexample set P→Q, Q→R, ¬(P→R).

  • (c) True: the one-line derivation whose only line is the premise ⊥ ends with ⊥.

  • (d) True, by Lemma 2.3.3 (a): the derivation of ⊥ from Γ has its premises in Γ,ψ.

  • (e) An equivalence: two derivations, each starting with ∧E on its premise. Each is Proposition 1.2.1, with the letters exchanged for the second.

The parts run from naming the task, through one step and a missing piece, to claims to judge and short derivations.

  • (a) Which task is “show ⊢(P∧Q)→P”?
  • (b) “Show that P→¬Q and Q→¬P are provably equivalent.” Which two derivations does this ask for?
  • (c) Write “P, P→Q and ¬Q cannot all be accepted together” as a sequent.
  • (d) A search for ⊥ from P∧¬Q, P→Q begins. Write its first three lines after the premises.
  • (e) Which list must be shown inconsistent to prove P→Q⊢¬Q→¬P by Proposition 3.2.2 (b)?
  • (f) The list Q, ¬P, ¬¬R is the counterexample set of which claims?
  • (g) “P→Q, P→¬Q is inconsistent, since its members contradict each other.” Is this correct?
  • (h) “Inside the box of the assumption P, line 6 is ⊥; so the premises are inconsistent.” Is this correct?
  • (i) “A search for ⊥ from P∨Q, ¬P stalled; so the list is consistent.” Is this correct?
  • (j) “Since P,¬P⊢⊥, the list P,¬P derives both Q and ¬Q.” Is this correct?
  • (k) Derive ⊥ from P→Q, Q→R, P, ¬R.
  • (l) Derive ⊥ from P∨Q, ¬P, ¬Q.

Solution.

  • (a) A theorem.

  • (b) P→¬Q⊢Q→¬P and Q→¬P⊢P→¬Q.

  • (c) P, P→Q, ¬Q⊢⊥.

  • (d) 3 P, ∧E 1; 4 Q, →E 2, 3; 5 ¬Q, ∧E 1. (Then 6 ⊥, ¬E 4, 5.)

  • (e) P→Q, ¬(¬Q→¬P).

  • (f) Of Q,¬P⊢¬R, with ¬¬R the negated conclusion, and of Q,¬¬R⊢P, with ¬P the negated conclusion. Q is not a negation, so it is not the negated conclusion of any claim.

  • (g) No. Both members are true when P is false, and nothing has been derived.

  • (h) No. It shows the premises together with P inconsistent, so the premises derive ¬P.

  • (i) No: a stalled search proves nothing.

  • (j) Yes: Proposition 3.2.1, (i) ⇒ (ii).

  • (k) Line 5 is Q, →E 1, 3. Line 6 is R, →E 2, 5. Line 7 is ⊥, ¬E 6, 4.

  • (l) Line 4 is P, assumption. Line 5 is ⊥, ¬E 4, 2. Line 6 is Q, assumption. Line 7 is ⊥, ¬E 6, 3. Line 8 is ⊥, ∨E 1, 4–5, 6–7.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Three theorems)

Derive each theorem, and say which step of Section 3.1’s method opens each box.

  • (a) ⊢¬(P∧¬P)∧¬¬(P∨¬P).
  • (b) ⊢((P→Q)∧(P∨Q))→Q.
  • (c) ⊢((P→Q)→Q)→((Q→P)→P).

Solution.

  • (a)
    1P∧¬Pasm, for ¬I
    2P∧E 1
    3¬P∧E 1
    4⊥¬E 2, 3
    5¬(P∧¬P)¬I 1–4
    6¬(P∨¬P)asm, for ¬I
    7Pasm, for ¬I
    8P∨¬P∨I 7
    9⊥¬E 8, 6
    10¬P¬I 7–9
    11P∨¬P∨I 10
    12⊥¬E 11, 6
    13¬¬(P∨¬P)¬I 6–12
    14¬(P∧¬P)∧¬¬(P∨¬P)∧I 5, 13

    Boxes 1 and 6 come from step 2: each goal is a negation. Box 7 comes from step 2 inside step 4. The clash step chooses to manufacture P∨¬P from its side ¬P, so that it clashes with line 6. Then the goal ¬P, a negation, opens the box. The second conjunct needed no classical rule, although P∨¬P itself does in this book’s derivation (Proposition 2.5.3).

  • (b)
    1(P→Q)∧(P∨Q)asm, for →I
    2P→Q∧E 1
    3P∨Q∧E 1
    4Pasm, for ∨E
    5Q→E 2, 4
    6Qasm, for ∨E
    7Q∨E 3, 4–5, 6–6
    8((P→Q)∧(P∨Q))→Q→I 1–7

    Box 1 comes from step 2. Boxes 4 and 6 come from step 3: the resource P∨Q is split by ∨E, both cases ending in the goal Q (the case Q is a one-line box). Lines 2 and 3 need nothing new. Read aloud: if P implies Q, and P or Q holds, then Q holds.

  • (c)
1(P→Q)→Qasm, for →I
2Q→Pasm, for →I
3¬Pasm, for RAA
4P→QVI 3
5Q→E 1, 4
6P→E 2, 5

8PRAA 3–7
9(Q→P)→P→I 2–8
10((P→Q)→Q)→((Q→P)→P)→I 1–9

Boxes 1 and 2 come from step 2. Box 3 comes from step 5: the goal P is a letter, and the resources need P→Q, which nothing in hand gives.

Exercise (B2: Two directions)

Show that (P∧Q)→R⊣⊢(P∧¬R)→¬Q.

  • (a) The search for (⇐) has reached the stage below. Name the step of the method, then complete it.
  • (b) Derive (⇒).
  • (c) Which direction used a classical rule, and why did the other not need one?
1(P∧¬R)→¬Q
2P∧Qasm, for →I
⋮
9R
10(P∧Q)→R→I 2–9

Solution.

  • (a) First the lines that need nothing new, P and Q by ∧E. Then comes the stuck step, step 5: the current goal R is a letter, no resource gives it, and nothing clashes, so assume ¬R for RAA.
    1(P∧¬R)→¬Q
    2P∧Qasm, for →I
    3P∧E 2
    4Q∧E 2
    5¬Rasm, for RAA
    6P∧¬R∧I 3, 5
    7¬Q→E 1, 6
    8⊥¬E 4, 7
    9RRAA 5–8
    10(P∧Q)→R→I 2–9
  • (b)
    1(P∧Q)→R
    2P∧¬Rasm, for →I
    3P∧E 2
    4¬R∧E 2
    5Qasm, for ¬I
    6P∧Q∧I 3, 5
    7R→E 1, 6
    8⊥¬E 7, 4
    9¬Q¬I 5–8
    10(P∧¬R)→¬Q→I 2–9
  • (c) (⇐) used RAA at line 9, because its goal R is a letter. (⇒) needed none, because its goal ¬Q is a negation, which ¬I builds. This is a claim about our two derivations only.

In words: to prove “if P and Q, then R”, it is enough to prove “if P and not R, then not Q”, a contrapositive in one hypothesis.

Exercise (B3: Derive or break, used later)

For each claim, derive it or break it. Try to break it first. To break a claim, give statements that make its counterexample set true.

  • (a) ¬P∨¬Q⊢¬(P∨Q)
  • (b) ¬(P↔Q), P⊢¬Q
  • (c) P→Q, ¬P→(R∨Q), ¬R⊢Q
  • (d) P→Q, ¬P→(R∨Q), ¬R⊢P
  • (e) (P→Q)→R, ¬R⊢P
  • (f) (P→Q)→R, ¬R⊢Q
  • (g) P∨Q, P⊢¬Q
  • (h) P∨Q, ¬(P∧Q), P⊢¬Q
  • (i) ¬(P∨Q)→P⊢P∨Q

Solution. The verdict comes first, then the derivation or the break. In every break, x is a real number.

  • (a) Breaks. Take P: “x>2”, Q: “x>0”, at x=1. ¬P is true, so ¬P∨¬Q is true; Q is true, so P∨Q is true and ¬(P∨Q) is false. The De Morgan law with this premise is ¬P∨¬Q⊢¬(P∧Q), part (c) of Proposition 2.5.5. Here the wrong connective sits inside the negation.

  • (b) Derive.

    2P

    4Pasm, for ↔I
    5QR 3
    6Qasm, for ↔I
    7PR 2

    This is the first box of Exercise (B5: Derive or break, used later) (f).

  • (c) Derive. Step 5: the goal Q is a letter, and the first two premises can be used only once P or ¬P is in hand, so LEM on P. DS matches as stated in Section 2.3, refuting the left disjunct R.

    7¬Pasm, for ∨E

    10Q∨E 4, 5–6, 7–9

  • (d) Breaks. Take P: “x>5”, Q: “x>0”, R: “x>10”, at x=1. P is false, so P→Q is true. ¬P and Q are true, so ¬P→(R∨Q) is true. ¬R is true, and P is false. Trying to break it first: with P false, the premises force Q true and R false, and nothing clashes. The tempting step goes from “P gives Q” and “Q holds” to P. Here Q holds by (c), and the step is affirming the consequent (Section 1.3).

Exercise (B4: Derive ⊥, or make every member true, used later)

For each list, either derive ⊥ from it, or give statements that make every member true at once.

  • (a) P→Q, Q→¬P, P
  • (b) P∨Q, P→¬Q, Q→¬P
  • (c) ¬(P→Q), Q∨¬P
  • (d) P→(Q∧¬Q)
  • (e) ¬(P∨Q), P∨(Q∧R)
  • (f) ¬(P∧Q), ¬(P∧¬Q), ¬(¬P∧Q)

Solution.

  • (a) Derive ⊥: 4 Q, →E 1, 3; 5 ¬P, →E 2, 4; 6 ⊥, ¬E 3, 5.

  • (b) True at once. Take P: “x>0”, Q: “x<0”, at x=1: P∨Q is true; ¬Q is true, so P→¬Q is true; and Q→¬P is true, since Q is false.

  • (c) Derive ⊥.

Adding a fourth member ¬(¬P∧¬Q) gives a list from which ⊥ can be derived: the four exclude every way for P and Q to be. Use LEM on P and, inside each case, on Q; each of the four cases builds the conjunction that one member negates, by ∧I, and clashes with it.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Chapter check: the counterexample set, used later)

Let Γ be the list P→(Q∨R), Q→S, R→S.

  • (a) Write the counterexample set of Γ⊢P→S.
  • (b) Show that it is inconsistent.
  • (c) What does Proposition 3.2.2 conclude? Give also a direct derivation of Γ⊢P→S, and compare the two.
  • (d) Replace one premise so that the claim breaks, and break it.

Solution.

  • (a) P→(Q∨R), Q→S, R→S, ¬(P→S).

  • (b) The goal is ⊥, and the clash partner ¬(P→S) is already on a line (Section 3.1), so aim at P→S.

    1P→(Q∨R)
    2Q→S
    3R→S
    4¬(P→S)
    5Pasm, for →I
    6Q∨R→E 1, 5
    7Qasm, for ∨E
    8S→E 2, 7
    9Rasm, for ∨E
    10S→E 3, 9
    11S∨E 6, 7–8, 9–10
    12P→S→I 5–11
    13⊥¬E 12, 4

  • (c) By part (b) of the proposition, Γ⊢P→S. The direct derivation is lines 1–3 and 5–12 of (b), renumbered: 11 lines. The route through the counterexample set adds the premise ¬(P→S) and one ¬E line, and gains nothing here. It helps when the goal gives the goal-directed step nothing to do, as with a letter or a disjunction with no side in hand. Then it is the stuck step, step 5.

  • (d) For instance, replace R→S by R→T. Take P: “x>0”, Q: “x>5”, R: “x<2”, S: “x>3”, T: “x<10”, at x=1. P and R are true, so Q∨R and P→(Q∨R) are true. Q→S is true, since Q is false, and R→T is true, since T is true. Last, P→S is false, since S is false. So every member of the new counterexample set is true. Any replacement that a choice of statements breaks is accepted.

What a complete answer needs. (10 points)

  • 2: the counterexample set, with ¬(P→S), not ¬P→S or P→¬S.
  • 3: the derivation of ⊥: P→S derived (by a box whose cases of Q∨R both end in S, or by cases on a hypothesis and the transitivity of implication), and ¬E outside every box.
  • 2: the conclusion drawn by the proposition, and the direct derivation.
  • 3: (d), a changed premise, with statements checked against all four members of the new counterexample set.

Slips that lose the step (0 for it): ⊥ written inside the box of P and taken as the end; a “break” in (d) that leaves a premise false.

Uses: Example (Cases on a hypothesis are two implications) (cases on a hypothesis); Proposition 1.4.1 (implication is transitive).

Exercise (C2: Challenge: Splitting an inconsistency, used later)

Let Γ be a list and φ a statement.

  • (a) Prove that if Γ,φ and Γ,¬φ are both inconsistent, then so is Γ.
  • (b) Deduce: if Γ is consistent, then at least one of Γ,φ and Γ,¬φ is consistent.
  • (c) Does (b) say which one?

Solution.

  • (a) Since Γ,φ is inconsistent, Proposition 3.2.2 (a) gives Γ⊢¬φ. Since Γ,¬φ is inconsistent, part (b) gives Γ⊢φ. So (iii) of Proposition 3.2.1 holds with χ the statement φ, and Γ is inconsistent.

  • (b) This is the contrapositive of (a): if both lists were inconsistent, Γ would be.

  • (c) No. It says that one of the two lists is consistent and gives no way to tell which. Deciding that would need a way to show a list consistent, which this chapter does not have.

Section 4.10 (optional) adds statements to a consistent list one at a time, in exactly this way.

What a complete answer needs. (10 points)

  • 4: (a) obtains Γ⊢¬φ by part (a) of the proposition or Γ⊢φ by part (b), and combines it with the other inconsistency, directly (by Cut, Lemma 2.3.3 (b)) or by the other part.
  • 3: (a) concludes by Proposition 3.2.1, by part (c) of the Cut lemma with one ¬E, or by Cut, its part (b).
  • 2: (b) as the contrapositive.
  • 1: (c) “no”.

Slips that lose the step: (b) claimed to show that a particular list is consistent.

Uses: Lemma 2.3.3 (Cut and chaining).
