Chapter 2

Chapter Review

Summary

The chapter added the rules for ¬, ⊥ and ↔, and the rule RAA. Together with Chapter 1’s seven rules they make the thirteen rules of the book’s logic. A clash gives anything (Proposition 2.1.1), and an implication whose hypothesis cannot hold is true (Proposition 2.1.2). The chapter proved that if n2 is even, then n is even (Theorem 2.1.3), and that 2 is irrational (Theorem 2.2.1). It showed that a result already proved may be used as one line of a later derivation, and why that is safe (Lemma 2.3.3). An iff is two implications, the “if” of a definition is an iff, and no statement is equivalent to its own negation (Lemma 2.4.2). With RAA the chapter derived double negation, excluded middle, De Morgan’s laws and Peirce’s law, and showed that RAA, ¬¬E and LEM are one principle in three forms (Theorem 2.5.7).

What is left open: a method for finding derivations (Chapter 3), and a way to show that a sequent has no derivation (Chapter 4). Chapter 4 also settles whether the classical rule could be done without.

Proof methods in this chapter

  • When the goal is ⊥, find a clash. Look for a statement and its negation among the available lines. Or, if a negation ¬φ is available, make φ the next goal. Introduced in Section 2.1.
  • An impossible case closes itself. In a proof by cases, a case that reaches ⊥ ends by ⊥E on the current goal. Introduced in Section 2.1.
  • To prove “not φ”, assume φ. The box opens with φ itself, without the “not”, and ends on ⊥. Introduced in Section 2.2.
  • Use a derived rule as one line. A sequent already derived can be used like a rule. Introduced in Section 2.3.
  • Two boxes for an iff. Open one box for each direction. Each box opens with one side and ends with the other. Introduced in Section 2.4.
  • When stuck, assume the negation of the goal. This is proof by contradiction. Then look for a clash. Introduced in Section 2.5.
  • A disjunction with neither side within reach: expect the classical step. Suppose the goal is a disjunction, and each side, as a goal on its own, can be broken. Then aim at the whole disjunction by RAA. Introduced in Section 2.5.

Section 3.1 gathers these methods and Chapter 1’s into one table, and all thirteen rules onto one card.

Terms

  • biconditional rules ↔I, ↔E: prove an iff by two boxes, and use it in either direction (Definition 2.4.1).
  • classical rules RAA, ¬¬E, LEM: each can be derived from each other one, so they are one principle (Theorem 2.5.7).
  • contradiction ⊥: the line a clash produces (Definition 2.1.1).
  • contradiction rules ¬E, ⊥E: a clash gives ⊥, and ⊥ gives anything (Definition 2.1.1).
  • contrapositive: ¬ψ→¬φ, derivable from φ→ψ (Proposition 2.3.2).
  • cut: a result already proved may be pasted into a derivation (Lemma 2.3.3).
  • De Morgan’s laws: how negation passes through “and” and “or” (Proposition 2.5.5).
  • derived rule: a sequent already derived, used as one line (Definition 2.3.1).
  • disjunctive syllogism DS: from φ∨ψ and ¬φ, conclude ψ (Proposition 2.3.4).
  • double negation: ¬¬φ and φ, each derivable from the other (Proposition 2.5.1).
  • ex falso: from a clash, anything follows (Proposition 2.1.1).
  • excluded middle LEM: φ∨¬φ, with no premises (Proposition 2.5.3).
  • modus tollens MT: from φ→ψ and ¬ψ, conclude ¬φ (Proposition 2.3.1).
  • negation rule ¬I: assume φ, reach ⊥, conclude ¬φ (Definition 2.2.1).
  • Peirce’s law: ((φ→ψ)→φ)→φ (Proposition 2.5.4).
  • provably equivalent, ⊣⊢: each derivable from the other (Definition 2.4.2).
  • reductio ad absurdum RAA: assume ¬φ, reach ⊥, conclude φ (Definition 2.5.1).
  • vacuously true: said of an implication whose hypothesis cannot hold (Proposition 2.1.2).
  • weakening: extra premises never spoil a derivation (Lemma 2.3.3).
