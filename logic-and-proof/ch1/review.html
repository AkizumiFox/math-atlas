Chapter 1

Chapter Review

Summary

A proof is a chain in which every line is a premise, an assumption, or one step of a rule, and checking one needs no idea. The chapter gave six rules, two each for “and”, “implies” and “or”, together with repetition. With them it derived the following:

  • “and” commutes and regroups (Proposition 1.2.1, Proposition 1.2.2);
  • implications chain (Proposition 1.4.1);
  • two hypotheses may be taken at once or one at a time (Proposition 1.4.2);
  • “or” commutes and regroups (Example (First derivations with “or”), Example (Regrouping a disjunction));
  • “and” distributes over “or” (Proposition 1.6.1);
  • from “P or Q”, “P implies R” and “Q implies S” follows “R or S”, the constructive dilemma (Example (Constructive dilemma)).

It named Argument B’s error: a box opened at the wrong end proves the converse. A form is bad when some choice of statements makes its counterexample set true. Breaking a form in this way is how a bad step is shown to be bad.

Still open: the chapter has no rules for “not” or for contradiction, and the fact Argument B set out to prove waits for them. That is Chapter 2’s question.

Proof methods in this chapter

  • Try to break it. To test a form, put statements in for the letters that make every premise true and the conclusion false. One such choice shows that the form is bad. (Section 1.1)
  • Two ways to attack an argument. Show a premise false, or show that a step’s form is bad. Either attack works without the other. (Section 1.1)
  • Read the skeleton off the goal. The goal’s main connective chooses the introduction rule: ∧ gives two subgoals, → a box, ∨ a choice to postpone. (Section 1.2)
  • Use a premise by its main connective. Its elimination rule says what you may do with it: take a conjunction apart, feed an implication its hypothesis, split a disjunction into cases. (Section 1.2)
  • Work from both ends. Premises at the top, the goal at the bottom; fill the gap from both sides. (Section 1.2)
  • Open a box, close a box. An assumption may be used exactly as long as its box is open. (Section 1.4)
  • Postpone a disjunction. Choose a side of a goal P∨Q only when that side is in hand. (Section 1.5)
  • Cases end in the current goal. Both boxes of ∨E close on the statement you are trying to reach. (Section 1.6)

Terms

  • argument form, good and bad: bad if some choice of statements breaks it (Definition 1.1.3).
  • assumption, discharged: an assumption whose box has closed (Definition 1.4.1).
  • available: citable at a line, by the scope rule (Definition 1.4.2).
  • box: a block of lines opened by an assumption (Definition 1.4.2).
  • box-free derivation: premises, then one rule step per line (Definition 1.2.2).
  • conjunction rules ∧I, ∧E: build from both sides; take apart (Definition 1.2.5).
  • counterexample set: the premises together with the negated conclusion (Definition 1.1.3).
  • derivable, Γ⊢ψ: some derivation from premises in Γ ends with ψ (Definition 1.2.3).
  • derivation: each line a premise, assumption, repetition or rule step (Definition 1.4.2).
  • disjunction rules ∨I, ∨E, proof by cases: weaken to a disjunction; split one into cases (Definition 1.6.1).
  • implication rules →I, →E: prove by a box; use by modus ponens (Definition 1.4.1).
  • main connective: the connective applied last in building a statement (Definition 1.1.2).
  • modus ponens: from φ→ψ and φ, conclude ψ (Definition 1.3.1).
  • or-introduction: from one side, conclude the disjunction (Definition 1.5.1).
  • repetition R: copy the statement of an available line (Definition 1.4.2).
  • rule schema: given lines above, one new line below (Definition 1.2.1).
  • sequent, turnstile: P1,…,Pn⊢Q, the claim that Q is derivable (Definition 1.2.4).
  • statement: a declarative sentence, either true or false (Definition 1.1.1).

Five tempting misreadings

Each claim below is a misreading that this chapter corrects. Decide whether each one is right, then check the answer.

Quick check.

  1. Let x be a real number. “If x>3, then x2>9” is false at x=1, since x>3 fails there.
  2. Let n be an integer. “n is divisible by 4 only if n is even” says that every even number is divisible by 4.
  3. In a proof that begins “Suppose x>2” and derives x2>4, the line x2>4 may be used anywhere later.
  4. “5≥0” is false, because 5≠0.
  5. Let x be a real number. From “x≥1 or x≤2” and “x≥1” it follows that x≤2 fails.

Solution.

  1. No. An implication breaks only when its hypothesis holds and its conclusion fails; at x=1 it promises nothing (Section 1.1, “When is an implication broken?”).
  2. No. “P only if Q” is P→Q: divisible by 4 implies even, which is true. The claim reads the converse, false at n=2 (Section 1.1).
  3. No. It holds only inside the supposition’s box. Outside it, only “if x>2, then x2>4” may be used, and x2>4 fails at x=0 (Section 1.4).
  4. No. 5≥0 is (5>0)∨(5=0), and an inclusive “or” needs one true side: 5>0 is true (Section 1.5).
  5. No. At x=32 both sides hold. The form “from P∨Q and P, conclude not Q” is affirming a disjunct, a bad form (Sections 1.1 and 1.6).
