Chapter 8

Chapter Review

Summary

A written proof is a derivation in sentences. Every rule has its phrases, and “let”, “suppose”, “choose” and “in either case” are the boxes, spoken (Sections 8.1–8.2). The chapter named four levels of one proof (Definition 8.3.1). One principle fixed which steps plain prose may leave unsaid (Definition 8.3.2), and a doubtful sentence is checked by expanding it to a skeleton. For reading, the chapter gave a habit, self-explanation. For writing, it gave a procedure. For checking, it gave two questions and a catalogue of fourteen errors (Example (What is not allowed)).

It ended with the one rule mathematicians have disputed. Without RAA, excluded middle has no derivation, by the optional Theorem 4.7.4. Yet excluded middle cannot be refuted (Proposition 8.7.3). An irrational number raised to an irrational power can be rational. One proof of this names no witness (Theorem 8.7.1), and another names one, using Proposition 8.7.2. The optional Section 8.8 proves, with stages of knowledge, which classical laws need the rule.

What is open is what the logic is for. Sets and Functions writes its proofs at levels 2 and 3 with these tools, and its first question is what a set is.

Proof methods in this chapter

  • Hear the rule in the phrase. “Let x∈A” opens a ∀I box, and “suppose” opens an assumption. “Choose” is ∃E, and “take” and “let δ:=…” are ∃I. “Suppose, for contradiction” is RAA or ¬I. Both boxes end in ⊥, and what the box assumes and the line written after it tell which. (Sections 8.1 and 8.2)
  • Expand to check. When a sentence is doubtful, rewrite that stretch, and only that stretch, as a skeleton, and look for the line that breaks a rule. (Section 8.3)
  • Skeleton first. Write the first and the last sentence of every box before filling any. (Section 8.3)
  • Explain each line to yourself by earlier lines, definitions and named results, and the rule that combines them, never by restating it. (Section 8.4)
  • Two columns when checking: is the line true? and does it follow, and by which rule? The first column finds where to look, and the second decides. (Section 8.6)
  • Say what depends on what. Every chosen object carries its dependencies: the names in the line it comes from, among the boxes open when it was chosen. (Section 8.2)
  • Write the reductions as a column. Each “it suffices to show” is one row with its reason. Read upwards, the column is the proof. (Section 8.3)
  • Generalize by a new name. Replace the particular object by a new letter, and keep only the lines that still hold for it. (Section 8.5)

Terms

  • BHK reading: a proof as a construction of what it claims (Section 8.7).
  • catalogue of errors, (N1)–(N14): recurring errors, each with the rule it breaks (Example (What is not allowed)).
  • costume: a direct or contrapositive proof dressed as a proof by contradiction (Example (A direct proof in costume)).
  • double inclusion: A=B shown as A⊆B and B⊆A (Example (Two inclusions)).
  • forcing (optional): what a stage of knowledge makes true (Definition 8.8.2).
  • heredity (optional): what a stage forces, every later stage forces (Lemma 8.8.1).
  • intuitionistic logic: the rules of this book without RAA (Section 8.7).
  • Kripke model (optional): stages of knowledge, with atoms that stay known (Definition 8.8.1).
  • levels of proof: derivation, skeleton, annotated prose, plain prose (Definition 8.3.1).
  • self-explanation: naming what makes each line true, never restating it (Example (Explaining a proof, well and badly)).
  • silent step: a step plain prose may leave unwritten (Definition 8.3.2).
  • skeleton: level 1, the boxes and the logical lines (Definition 8.3.1).
  • TFAE by a cycle (“the following are equivalent”): several conditions shown equivalent by a loop of implications (Example (Three conditions, one cycle)).
