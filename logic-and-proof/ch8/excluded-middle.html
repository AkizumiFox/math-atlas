8.7  The Dispute over Excluded Middle

Here is a proof that two numbers with a certain property exist which, when it is finished, still does not tell you what the numbers are. It is correct by the rules of this book. Some mathematicians, called intuitionists, reject it, for a century now and for reasons that are mathematical rather than merely fussy. This section shows the proof, says what those mathematicians ask of a proof, finds the one rule that fails to provide it, and asks what can be proved without that rule and what analysis would lose.

8.7.1. Irrational powers, with and without a witness

Here is the standard example of a proof that establishes existence without saying what exists.

Theorem 8.7.1 (An Irrational Power of an Irrational Number Can Be Rational) Optional: nothing later depends on this.

There are irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational.

Try it first (ten minutes): find such a and b explicitly, and prove that your a and b are irrational and that ab is rational.

Idea. The first candidates anyone tries are a=b=2. Then the question is whether 22 is rational, and the attempt stalls there: nothing in this book decides it. There are two ways out. The classical one splits on the undecided question with LEM. If 22 is rational, the first candidates work. If not, use it as the new a and raise it to 2 once more, which turns the exponent into 2⋅2=2. The proof below takes this way. The other way chooses an exponent that undoes the square root, and the proposition after the proof takes it.

Proof. Let s:=22, a positive real number. By excluded middle, s is rational or s is not rational [LEM], and we argue by cases [∨E].

Case 1: s is rational. Take a:=2 and b:=2. Both are irrational by Theorem 2.2.1, and ab=s is rational [∃I].

Case 2: s is not rational. Take a:=s and b:=2. Then a is irrational by the case assumption and b by Theorem 2.2.1, and ab=(22)2=22⋅2=22=2, which is rational [∃I].

In either case there are irrational a and b with ab rational. This proves the theorem.

The classical step is the first line, LEM on the statement “s is rational”. Every other step uses only rules other than RAA. The proof does not tell us which pair works. It hands over two candidate pairs, (2,2) and (22,2), and a guarantee that one of them succeeds. The next subsection says why some mathematicians do not accept such a proof. Section 2.5 described excluded middle as making a decision; here the decision about “s is rational” is made, and nobody learns its outcome.

Remark. In fact the second case holds: 22 is transcendental, so in particular irrational, by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem, proved independently by Gelfond and by Schneider in 1934. That theorem lies far beyond this book, and nothing here uses it. It also does not repair the proof above, which never needed to know the answer.

The other way out finds the witness. Since 2b=2b/2, the exponent b=2log2⁡m=log2⁡(m2) gives 2b=m for every integer m≥2. The choice m=2 gives b=2, which is rational, so m must avoid the powers of 2; the first choice left is m=3.

Proposition 8.7.2 (A Witness for an Irrational Power) Optional: nothing later depends on this.

The number log2⁡9 is irrational. Consequently a:=2 and b:=log2⁡9 are irrational numbers with ab=3.

Idea. The irrationality goes as for 2: suppose log2⁡9 is a fraction p/q, clear the denominator, and look at parity. Here the clash is quicker than for 2: a power of 2 would equal a power of 9, and one is even while the other is odd. The positivity of log2⁡9 is what makes the power of 2 even.

Proof. Suppose that log2⁡9=p/q for integers p and q with q≠0; replacing p, q by −p, −q if necessary, we may take q≥1. Since 20=1<9 and t↦2t is increasing, log2⁡9>0, so p>0, that is, p≥1. From 2p/q=9, raising both sides to the power q gives 2p=9q. The left side is even, since 2p=2⋅2p−1 with p−1≥0. The right side is odd, being a product of q odd factors. No integer is both even and odd, by (E2) of Section 2.1, so this is a contradiction. Hence log2⁡9 is not rational [¬I].

For the consequence, a=2 is irrational by Theorem 2.2.1, and b=log2⁡9 is irrational by what was just shown. Since log2⁡9=log2⁡(32)=2log2⁡3, ab=(21/2)log2⁡9=2(log2⁡9)/2=2log2⁡3=3. This proves the proposition.

This proof uses no classical rule. The irrationality of log2⁡9 is a negative statement, “no fraction equals log2⁡9”, and its box closes by ¬I, like the proof of Theorem 2.2.1. The witness (2,log2⁡9) is written down. So Proposition 8.7.2 gives a second proof of Theorem 8.7.1, and this one names its witness. (The exponent laws for real exponents are school facts here.)

Quick check. Does the proof of Proposition 8.7.2 use a classical rule? Does it name its witness?

Solution. No classical rule: its box assumes log2⁡9=p/q and concludes the negation, by ¬I. And yes, the witness (2,log2⁡9) is written down.

8.7.2. What a proof provides

Chapter 4 gave the connectives a meaning through truth values: every letter is true or false, and a table computes the rest. That semantics already takes a side, since a row decides every letter. The intuitionist explains a statement instead by saying what counts as a proof of it. The resulting clauses are called the BHK reading, after Brouwer, Heyting, and Kolmogorov.

To know a statement is to have a proof of it, and a proof of a compound statement is built from proofs of its parts.

  • There is no proof of ⊥.
  • A proof of P∧Q is a pair: a proof of P and a proof of Q.
  • A proof of P∨Q is a proof of P or a proof of Q, with a label saying which.
  • A proof of P→Q is a method that takes any proof of P and returns a proof of Q.
  • A proof of ¬P is a method that takes any proof of P and returns a proof of ⊥. So ¬P is read as P→⊥, exactly the reading that the table in Section 2.2, after Definition 2.2.1, found row by row.
  • A proof of ∃xφ is an object t together with a proof of φ[t/x].
  • A proof of ∀xφ is a method that produces, for every object t, a proof of φ[t/x].

In words: a disjunction must say which side, an existence claim must name the object, and an implication or a universal statement must come with a method that works on every input. A proof of a negation provides no object at all; it is a guarantee that no proof of the negated statement will ever arrive.

Each rule other than RAA builds exactly what its clause asks for. ∨I writes P∨Q from a line P, so it knows which side it used. ∃I writes ∃xφ from an instance φ[t/x], so the witness t is on the page. A →I box is a method: given the assumption, it produces the conclusion. RAA is the exception. Its box shows that ¬φ leads to ⊥, which is a proof of ¬¬φ, and then it writes φ. Under the BHK reading a method for refuting every refutation of φ is not a proof of φ.

Excluded middle under BHK. A proof of P∨¬P for every statement P would be a single method that decides each statement, says which side holds, and proves that side. Nobody has such a method. Take Goldbach’s conjecture, that every even integer greater than 2 is a sum of two primes: nobody has proved it and nobody has refuted it, so nobody can produce a labeled proof of “Goldbach’s conjecture or its negation”. The proof of Theorem 8.7.1 is of this kind: it splits on “s is rational or not” by LEM, and so never says which pair works.

Not every, and some not. Chapter 6 derived ¬∀xφ⊢∃x¬φ (Proposition 6.2.1 (a)) with two RAA boxes, and noted that the derivation never says which x fails φ. The BHK reading says why that matters. A proof of the premise is a method that refutes every proof of ∀xφ; a proof of the conclusion must name an object. Nothing in the first produces the second, and the derivation does not try: its conclusion comes from RAA, not from an ∃I applied to an available instance. Classical mathematics accepts the step. A constructive mathematician accepts that conclusion only with an object in hand.

Quick check. Describe a BHK proof of ¬(P∧¬P). Does it decide whether P holds?

Solution. We need a method that takes any proof of P∧¬P and returns a proof of ⊥. A proof of P∧¬P is a pair: a proof p of P and a method f turning proofs of P into proofs of ⊥. Apply f to p. That is the whole method, and it never decides P: it only uses a pair that someone else supplies. (It is the BHK reading of a ¬I box on P∧¬P whose clash is ¬E on its two halves.)

8.7.3. One extra rule

Chapter 2 found that one rule stands apart. RAA belongs to no connective, and over the other primitive rules it derives ¬¬E and LEM and is derived by each of them (Theorem 2.5.7). Chapter 4’s optional Theorem 4.7.4 showed that the other propositional rules cannot derive it.

Recall what Section 2.5 established. Over the primitive rules other than RAA, the three rules RAA, ¬¬E, and LEM each derive the other two, so adding any one of them gives the same derivable sequents. There is one classical principle, and it has three faces. Nothing needs re-proving here; what is new is the decision to leave the principle out and see what remains.

Call a derivation intuitionistic if no line is justified by RAA and no line uses a derived rule or a cited result whose own derivation uses RAA. Intuitionistic logic is the system of these derivations. Its rules are the introduction and elimination rules for ∧, →, and ∨ (Chapter 1); ¬I, ¬E, ⊥E, ↔I, and ↔E (Chapter 2); the rules for ∀, ∃, and = (Chapter 5); and repetition. Classical logic, the logic of Chapters 1–7, is the same list with RAA added. TAUT (Section 4.8) is not among these rules: it is licensed by Corollary 4.8.5, that is, by the completeness theorem, whose proof splits on atoms by LEM (Lemma 4.8.3), so a line justified by TAUT, on a formula or on a propositional skeleton (Section 6.3), uses RAA in the sense just defined.

This is why Chapter 2 kept ⊥E as a primitive rule although RAA and repetition derive it (the remark in the second-reading fold after Theorem 2.5.7). Had ⊥E been dropped, striking RAA would have taken it away too, and the remaining system would be weaker than the one intuitionists actually use: it could no longer derive ⊥⊢P. That is a claim about all derivations, and we state it without proof; nothing depends on it. With ⊥E kept, the rules without RAA are exactly intuitionistic logic.

System Rules ⊢P∨¬P?
intuitionistic the primitive rules other than RAA not derivable (Theorem 4.7.4 for the propositional rules, and the optional Section 8.8 again, by stages of knowledge; the rules for ∀, ∃ and = do not change this, stated without proof)
classical the same rules and RAA derivable (Proposition 2.5.3)

Every intuitionistic derivation is a derivation, so everything intuitionistic logic derives, classical logic derives too, and soundness applies to it: Theorem 4.7.2, and, for derivations whose lines contain quantifiers or equations, the soundness statement for predicate logic in Section 7.2. The difference is a list of sequents that only the classical system reaches. Chapters 2–7 flag the derivations that use RAA as they go, so the results on that list can be read off the text; Exercise B4 asks for them.

Quick check. Is the derivation of Proposition 2.5.1 (a), P⊢¬¬P, intuitionistic? Is that of (b)?

Solution.

  • (a) Yes: it is one ¬I box on ¬P. (b) No: it is derived with RAA.

8.7.4. Excluded middle cannot be refuted

If intuitionists do not assert P∨¬P, perhaps they deny it. They do not, and they could not: its double negation needs no classical rule at all.

Proposition 8.7.3 (Excluded Middle Cannot Be Refuted) Optional: nothing later depends on this.

For every statement P, ⊢¬¬(P∨¬P), by a derivation that uses neither RAA nor ⊥E.

Try it first (ten minutes): the goal is a negation. Open the box that the goal asks for, hunt for a clash, and note which rule closes each box you open.

Idea. An attempt that starts from P∨¬P stalls at once: ∨I needs one side, and neither side is in hand. But the goal is not P∨¬P. It is ¬(¬(P∨¬P)), a negation, so the goal-directed move of Section 2.2 opens a ¬I box on ¬(P∨¬P) with the goal ⊥. That assumption can clash only with P∨¬P, which ∨I builds from either side, so we manufacture a side as in Example (Manufacturing the other half of a clash). A box on P gives P∨¬P, a clash, and ¬I closes it with ¬P. Then ∨I builds P∨¬P from ¬P, and that is the second clash. Every box ends in ⊥ and closes by ¬I.

Before reading on: self-explain lines 5 and 6.

Proof. Exercise B1 (a) of Section 3.2 (Exercise (B1: Three theorems)) derived this already, inside a conjunction; here is that derivation again, read for what it does not use. The derivation is:

1¬(P∨¬P)asm, for ¬I
2Pasm, for ¬I
3P∨¬P∨I 2
4⊥¬E 3, 1
5¬P¬I 2–4
6P∨¬P∨I 5
7⊥¬E 6, 1
8¬¬(P∨¬P)¬I 1–7

The two boxes close by ¬I, at lines 5 and 8. The other rules used are ∨I (lines 3 and 6) and ¬E (lines 4 and 7). The derivation has no premises. This proves the proposition.

Set it beside the derivation of Proposition 2.5.3. Lines 1–7 are the same, line for line. Only line 8 differs: there RAA closed the outer box and wrote P∨¬P; here ¬I closes it and writes the negation of its assumption. So the whole classical content of Proposition 2.5.3 sits in its last line.

↺Second readingNon-example by minimal change

Keep lines 1–7, and write P∨¬P at line 8 with the justification ¬I 1–7. The box still checks. What fails is the conclusion of ¬I, which must be the negation of the box’s assumption, ¬¬(P∨¬P). Writing P∨¬P there needs RAA.

What the proposition says is this: nobody can refute excluded middle. A refutation of P∨¬P, put beside this derivation, would prove ⊥ from no premises, and no derivation does that. (That last fact is soundness: Theorem 4.7.2, or the soundness statement for predicate logic in Section 7.2 when P or the derivation involves quantifiers or equality, since no valuation, and no structure and assignment, makes ⊥ true.) The intuitionist does not claim that excluded middle fails. The intuitionist declines to assert it, because asserting it would mean having a proof of one side, and for many statements P, an open conjecture for instance, nobody has one.

The proposition under BHK. The derivation of Proposition 8.7.3 is such a method, and it decides nothing. Its conclusion asks for a method that takes any f, a method sending proofs of P∨¬P to proofs of ⊥, and returns a proof of ⊥. Here is one. Given a proof p of P, label it “left” and apply f: that turns proofs of P into proofs of ⊥, which is a proof g of ¬P (lines 2–5). Now label g “right” and apply f once more (lines 6–7). At no point did the method need to know whether P holds.

Remark. The proposition is one instance of a general fact, due to Glivenko (1929) and stated here without proof: if a statement built from letters by the connectives is derivable with all the rules, then its double negation is derivable without RAA. Classical propositional logic survives inside intuitionistic logic, behind two negations. The fact fails once quantifiers are allowed; ¬¬∀x(φ∨¬φ) is in general not derivable without RAA (also stated without proof).

Warning. Intuitionistic logic does not say that excluded middle is false. No instance P∨¬P can be refuted, since ¬¬(P∨¬P) is derivable without RAA; dropping a rule is not adding its negation. (Brouwer’s own analysis adds axioms about the real numbers under which the universal statement “every real number is 0 or not 0” is refuted, but no single instance is.)

Quick check. Does Proposition 8.7.3 show that P∨¬P is derivable without RAA?

Solution. No. It derives ¬¬(P∨¬P); writing P∨¬P after the same box needs RAA, and Chapter 4’s optional theorem in Section 4.7 shows that for a letter p no derivation without RAA reaches p∨¬p, as does the optional Section 8.8.

Quick check. An intuitionist has proved ¬Q→¬P. May she conclude P→Q?

Solution. Not in general: that step is Proposition 2.5.2, in the right column of the table. She may when Q is itself a negation, by Section 2.5’s fold “RAA is never needed to finish a negation”, which Section 8.1 applies to the contrapositive.

8.7.6. A short history

The dispute has dates.

In 1908 L. E. J. Brouwer published “De onbetrouwbaarheid der logische principes” (“The unreliability of the logical principles”) in the Tijdschrift voor Wijsbegeerte. His point was that excluded middle had been abstracted from reasoning about finite collections, where every case can in principle be checked, and then applied without justification to infinite ones, where it cannot. Over the next two decades he rebuilt parts of analysis on this view, and the movement took the name intuitionism.

In 1925 A. N. Kolmogorov showed that classical reasoning can be imitated inside a logic without excluded middle by putting a double negation in front of every subformula: the first double-negation translation (Exercise C2 below checks one step of it).

In July 1927 David Hilbert lectured in Hamburg on “Die Grundlagen der Mathematik”, printed in 1928. He defended excluded middle in a much-repeated comparison: to take it from the mathematician would be like forbidding the astronomer the telescope or the boxer the use of his fists. The quarrel between his program and Brouwer’s became known as the Grundlagenstreit, the dispute over foundations. It turned personal in 1928, when Hilbert had Brouwer removed from the editorial board of the Mathematische Annalen; the board was then dissolved and re-formed without him.

In 1929 V. Glivenko proved the theorem of the remark above: classical propositional logic sits inside intuitionistic logic behind a double negation. In 1930 Arend Heyting published “Die formalen Regeln der intuitionistischen Logik” in the Sitzungsberichte der Preußischen Akademie der Wissenschaften. It wrote down rules for intuitionistic logic, in a format different from ours, and from then on the logic could be studied as a formal system like any other.

In 1933 Kurt Gödel published a double-negation translation of arithmetic, which shows that classical arithmetic is consistent if intuitionistic arithmetic is. Gerhard Gentzen had found an equivalent translation independently and withdrew his paper of 1933 when Gödel’s appeared.

In 1967 Errett Bishop published Foundations of Constructive Analysis. It developed a large part of analysis without excluded middle, and also without Brouwer’s philosophy and his special axioms about the real numbers. Most of what is now called constructive analysis descends from it.

↺Second readingAnother dispute: the conditional

Excluded middle is not the only rule that has been questioned. Chapter 1 read P→Q as a promise that is broken only when P holds and Q fails, so a true Q makes P→Q true whatever P says, and ⊢P→(Q→P). Some logicians find that too generous: an “if” should connect its two parts. Logics built on that view, called relevance logics, reject P→(Q→P). This book keeps Chapter 1’s reading, which is the one mathematics uses.

8.7.7. What analysis would lose

What does analysis look like without the rule? Here are four previews, stated informally and without proof. Classical analysis takes the first as an axiom and proves the other three; each is a place where classical reasoning, by the rule or by an axiom, does work that a constructive proof cannot.

  • The supremum. Classical analysis takes as an axiom that every non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a supremum. Constructively, a non-empty set A bounded above has a supremum if and only if it is order-located: for all real numbers x<y, either y is an upper bound of A or some element of A exceeds x (Bishop’s form of the least upper bound principle). The classical axiom asserts the supremum without requiring anyone to make these decisions.
  • Monotone sequences. E. Specker (1949) gave an increasing, bounded sequence of rational numbers that a computer program can list term by term, but whose limit is not a computable real number: no program can print its decimal digits. So the convergence of bounded monotone sequences, which classical analysis proves from that axiom, cannot come with a method for computing the limit.
  • The intermediate value theorem. Let f be continuous on [a,b] with f(a)<0<f(b). Classically, one proves that f(x)=0 for some x∈[a,b]. Constructively, what holds in general is the approximate version: for every ε>0 some x∈[a,b] has |f(x)|<ε. The exact version holds under extra hypotheses, for example when f is strictly increasing. A bisection needs the sign of f at a midpoint m, and finitely many digits of f(m) cannot always tell whether f(m) is negative, zero, or positive: the digits of a value equal to 0 begin like those of a tiny positive or negative one.
  • Bolzano–Weierstrass. A bisection proof of the theorem says: one of the two halves of an interval contains infinitely many terms of the sequence; continue with that half. That step is LEM on “the left half contains infinitely many terms”, a statement that nobody can decide in general, and the proof does not say which half.

8.7.8. Proofs as programs

The BHK clauses read like the description of a programming language. A proof of P∧Q is a pair; a proof of P∨Q is a value tagged “left” or “right”; a proof of P→Q is a function; a proof of ∃xφ is a pair of an object and a proof; a proof of ∀xφ is a function that accepts any object. This match between propositions and types, and between proofs and programs, is the Curry–Howard correspondence. It is what a proof assistant such as Lean is built on: checking a proof is checking that a program has the right type.

↺Second readingProofs as programs, in Lean

Intuitionistic logic fits this picture exactly. LEM does not, since no program decides every proposition. Lean adds classical logic by an axiom, Classical.choice, a form of the axiom of choice. Excluded middle, Classical.em, is then a theorem, derived from choice together with two extensionality principles by Diaconescu’s theorem. Lean draws one line with care. A definition that uses classical logic to produce data, for instance a number chosen by deciding an arbitrary proposition, must be marked noncomputable, since no program computes it. A proof that uses Classical.em needs no mark, because proofs carry no data.

-- excluded middle is a theorem, proved from the axiom of choice
example (p : Prop) : Or p (Not p) := Classical.em p

-- a number defined by deciding an arbitrary proposition
open Classical in
noncomputable def indicator (p : Prop) : Nat := if p then 1 else 0

Warning. “Constructive” does not mean “computable in practice”. A constructive proof provides a method, and the method may be hopeless. Deciding whether a whole number is prime by trying every smaller divisor is constructive, and for a number with a thousand digits no computer could finish it. Constructive mathematics asks whether a method exists, not whether it is fast.

8.7.9. The book’s position

The main line of this book stays classical. The decision is made once, in the axioms for the real numbers, which include trichotomy: for real numbers a and b, exactly one of a<b, a=b, b<a holds. Constructive analysis does not accept trichotomy for real numbers, because it would decide the sign of every real number. So a case split such as “x≥0 or x<0” rests on an axiom, and the book does not flag it.

What the book does flag is a genuinely non-constructive step of the kind discussed above, such as the bisection step of Bolzano–Weierstrass, and every use of the axiom of choice. One clause says so, and nothing more. Such a step is never silent anyway: by Definition 8.3.2, a ¬¬E or LEM step is written out as an explicit case split or an explicit removal of a double negation. A reader who wants to know where the classical rule enters a proof can always find the place.

Remark (Looking ahead). Analysis on the Real Line takes trichotomy as one of its axioms in Chapter 1, and flags in one clause each genuinely non-constructive step it makes.

Solution. The tool is proof by cases on the remainder of n on division by 3: every integer n is 3k, 3k+1, or 3k+2 for some integer k. This is a school fact, of the same kind as “every integer is even or odd” in Chapter 2. It is ∨E with three cases (Definition 1.6.1, used twice), and in each case ∃I (Definition 5.5.1) with an explicit witness. First factor: n3−n=(n−1)n(n+1). Let n be an integer, and let k be an integer with n=3k, n=3k+1, or n=3k+2.

Case n=3k. Then n3−n=3⋅((3k−1)k(3k+1)).

Case n=3k+1. Then n3−n=(3k)(3k+1)(3k+2)=3⋅(k(3k+1)(3k+2)).

Case n=3k+2. Then n3−n=(3k+1)(3k+2)(3k+3)=3⋅((3k+1)(3k+2)(k+1)).

In each case n3−n=3j for the integer j in brackets, so 3∣n3−n. Since n was arbitrary, this holds for every integer n.

Why the look-alikes are worse. A proof by contradiction (“suppose 3∤n3−n …”) would have to reach exactly this case analysis and then clash with its assumption at the last line: a direct proof in costume (Example (A direct proof in costume)). Induction is closer, but it fits the statement badly: with base case P(0) and the step from n to n+1 (Axiom 4.2.1) it proves the claim only for n∈N, and the negative integers n=−m, m∈Z>0, need a second argument, for instance (−m)3−(−m)=−(m3−m). The case split covers all integers at once.
