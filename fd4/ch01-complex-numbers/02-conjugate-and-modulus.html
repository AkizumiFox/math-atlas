1.2  Conjugate, Modulus, and Division

Section 1.1 built C and showed that it is a commutative ring with 1≠0 (Proposition 1.1.2). One field axiom is still missing: (F8), which asks for an inverse of every non-zero element. To divide by 3+4i, we want a number to multiply by that lands in the real numbers, where division is familiar. The product (a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2 of Example (First Computations in C) shows which number does the job. This section gives its two ingredients names, the conjugate and the modulus, proves the rules they obey, and concludes that C is a field.

1.2.1. Conjugate and modulus

A complex number z=a+bi is a point (a,b) of the plane. Two pieces of plane geometry turn out to be algebra in disguise: the mirror image of z in the horizontal axis, and the distance of z from 0. The mirror image changes the sign of b. The distance, by Pythagoras, is the square root of a2+b2, the very number that appeared in Example (First Computations in C). Square roots of non-negative real numbers exist and are unique in every complete ordered field (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4), so we may use them in R: for x∈R with x≥0, x is the unique y∈R with y≥0 and y2=x.

Conjugating reflects a point in the real axis; the modulus is its distance from the origin.

Definition 1.2.1 (Conjugate and Modulus)

Let z=a+bi∈C with a,b∈R. The complex conjugate of z is z―:=a−bi. The modulus (or absolute value) of z is the real number |z|:=a2+b2, the non-negative square root of Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4 in R.

In words: the conjugate keeps the real part and flips the sign of the imaginary part, Rez―=Rez and Imz―=−Imz. The modulus is a real number, never negative, and its square is a2+b2=(Rez)2+(Imz)2.

Well-definedness. Since z determines a and b (compare real and imaginary parts, Section 1.1), the right-hand sides depend only on z. The square root exists because its argument is non-negative: a2≥0 and b2≥0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e), with 02=0 when the number is 0), so a2+b2≥0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (b)). And R is complete (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 5.6.3), which is the hypothesis of Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4. This is where the completeness of R enters the chapter. Over Q alone the definition would break down at once: |1+i| would have to be a rational number whose square is 2, and there is none (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 2.6.1).

Examples.

A point with integer distance. For z=3+4i: z―=3−4i, and |z|=9+16=25=5. The last step is the uniqueness clause of Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4: 5≥0 and 52=25, so 5 is the non-negative square root of 25.

The unit i. i―=0+1i―=0−1i=−i, and |i|=0+1=1, since 1≥0 and 12=1.

The degenerate case z=0. 0―=0 and |0|=0=0. We show below that 0 is the only complex number with modulus 0; that one fact is what makes division possible.

A real number. For a∈R, a―=a and |a|=a2. The symbol |a| already had a meaning for real a: the absolute value of an ordered field (Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 4.1.1). The next proposition shows the two meanings agree, so the notation does not clash.

Proposition 1.2.1 (The Modulus Extends the Absolute Value)

Let a∈R⊆C. Then a―=a, and the modulus a2+02 of a equals the absolute value of a in the ordered field R (Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 4.1.1).

Proof. Write a=a+0i. Then a―=a−0i=a, since 0⋅i=i⋅0=0 by (F6) and Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (b), and −0=0 because 0+0=0 and negatives are unique (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a)), all in the commutative ring C. The modulus of a is a2+02=a2, the unique non-negative real number whose square is a2. The absolute value |a| of the ordered field R is non-negative and satisfies |a|2=a2 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 4.1.3 (a), (b)). By the uniqueness clause of Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4, the two are equal.

So the modulus extends the absolute value: on R⊆C it is the old |⋅|, and every result about |x| for real x stays true with the new reading. For instance |−3|=3, whichever definition we use.

Quick check. Find z― and |z| for z=−2i and for z=1−i. For which of the two is z―=z?

Solution. For z=−2i=0+(−2)i: z―=0−(−2)i=2i and |z|=0+4=2, since 2≥0 and 22=4. For z=1−i: z―=1+i and |z|=1+1=2. In neither case is z―=z: comparing imaginary parts, z―=z needs Imz=−Imz, that is Imz=0, and both numbers have non-zero imaginary part.

Why this definition. The square root is what makes the modulus a length. Without it, the simpler quantity N(z):=a2+b2 would do some of the same jobs: it is zz―, and it vanishes only at 0. But on real numbers it is N(a)=a2, not |a|, so it does not extend the absolute value: N(2)=4. And it is not a sensible distance: N(1+1)=4, while N(1)+N(1)=2, so the “side” from 0 to 2 would be longer than the two sides through 1. The modulus keeps the convenient algebra of N, since |z|2=N(z), and adds the right size. Section 1.3 exploits exactly this square.

Warning. The modulus squared is zz―, not zz. In general |z|2≠z2: for z=i, |i|2=1 but i2=−1. The two agree only when z is real. For z=a+bi, z2=(a2−b2)+2abi and |z|2=a2+b2; if they are equal, comparing real parts gives a2−b2=a2+b2, so 2b2=0. Since 2>0 in R (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (h)), 2≠0, so b2=0 by part (f), and b=0 by part (e). Conversely, for real z=a, |a|2=a2+02=a2.

1.2.2. Rules for conjugate and modulus

The following rules are used constantly, in this chapter and much later for complex inner products. They say that conjugation respects both operations, and that the modulus respects products.

Theorem 1.2.2 (Properties of Conjugation and Modulus)

For all z,w∈C:

  • (a) z+w―=z―+w― and z――=z;
  • (b) zw―=z―w―;
  • (c) zz―=|z|2 and z+z―=2Rez;
  • (d) |zw|=|z||w| and |z―|=|z|;
  • (e) |z|=0 if and only if z=0.

Idea. Parts (a)–(c) and (e) are direct computations with real and imaginary parts. For (d), expanding |zw| directly is legitimate but messy, since a square root of a long sum appears. It is cleaner to square: by (c) a squared modulus is a product with a conjugate, and (b) lets the conjugate split across the product. Two non-negative real numbers with the same square are equal, and that brings us back from squares to moduli.

Proof. Let z=a+bi and w=c+di with a,b,c,d∈R.

  • (a) We have z+w―=(a+c)−(b+d)i=(a−bi)+(c−di)=z―+w―, since −(b+d)=(−b)+(−d): (b+d)+((−b)+(−d))=0, and negatives are unique (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a) in R). And z―=a+(−b)i, so z――=a−(−b)i=a+bi=z, by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a) in R.

  • (b) Since zw=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i, we get zw―=(ac−bd)−(ad+bc)i. On the other hand, by Definition 1.1.1 and Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (c) in R, z―w―=(a+(−b)i)(c+(−d)i)=(ac−(−b)(−d))+(a(−d)+(−b)c)i=(ac−bd)−(ad+bc)i.

  • (c) By Example (First Computations in C), zz―=a2+b2, and a2+b2=|z|2 because |z| is a square root of a2+b2. Also z+z―=(a+a)+(b−b)i=2a=2Rez.

  • (d) By (c), then (b), then Proposition 1.1.2 to regroup and reorder the four factors, |zw|2=(zw)zw―=zwz―w―=(zz―)(ww―)=|z|2|w|2. In R, |z|2|w|2=(|z||w|)2 (Proposition 1.1.3 (c)), and |z||w|≥0: it is 0 if one factor is 0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (b)), and positive otherwise, since a product of two positive elements of an ordered field is positive ((O4) of Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 2.3.2). So |zw| and |z||w| are both non-negative real numbers whose square is |z|2|w|2. By the uniqueness clause of Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4 they are equal. Finally |z―|=a2+(−b)2=a2+b2=|z|, since (−b)2=b2 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (c)).

  • (e) If z=0, then |z|=0=0. Conversely, let |z|=0, so a2+b2=|z|2=0. If a≠0, then a2>0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e)) and b2≥0, so a2+b2>0 (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (b)), a contradiction; hence a=0. Then b2=0, and the same rule gives b=0. So z=0. This proves the theorem.

The proof of (d) is our first use of a move that runs through the whole chapter: a statement about a modulus |u| becomes a statement about |u|2=uu―, which is algebra; we compute there, and come back by taking non-negative square roots. Section 1.3 names it, square the modulus, and uses it to prove the triangle inequality.

Two remarks on what the theorem says. First, (a) and (b) say that conjugation respects sums and products, and it fixes 1. Not every reflection does: the reflection in the vertical axis, z↦−z―, respects sums, but it sends 1 to −1, and 1⋅1=1 to −1, while the product of the images is (−1)(−1)=1. Second, (d) says that the size of a product is the product of the sizes. In the plane picture, multiplying by w stretches every length by |w|; Section 1.5 completes this picture with angles.

1.2.3. Division

The first payoff is division. If z≠0, then |z|≠0 by (e), so |z|2 is a strictly positive real number, and we may divide by it in R.

Proposition 1.2.3 (Inverse of a Complex Number)

Let z∈C with z≠0. Then w:=1|z|2z― satisfies zw=1. We write z−1 or 1z for this w.

Proof. Since z≠0, |z|≠0 by Theorem 1.2.2 (e), so |z|2>0 by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e). In particular |z|2≠0, so it has an inverse 1/|z|2 in the field R. By Proposition 1.1.2 and Theorem 1.2.2 (c), zw=1|z|2(zz―)=1|z|2|z|2=1, where the last product is computed in R, which gives the same result as in C (Proposition 1.1.1). This proves the proposition.

The hypothesis z≠0 is used exactly once, to make |z|2 non-zero, so that 1/|z|2 exists. For z=0 there is nothing to rescue: 0⋅w=0≠1 for every w (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (b)). The proposition gives one inverse; that it is the only one is a fact about all fields, which we can cite as soon as we know that C is one.

Theorem 1.2.4 (The Complex Numbers Form a Field)

The set C, with the operations of Definition 1.1.1, is a field in the sense of Number Systems: From N to R, Definition 2.3.1. Moreover R⊆C (Proposition 1.1.1) is closed under the field operations of C: for a,c∈R, the sum a+c, the product ac, the negative −a and, if a≠0, the inverse a−1, computed in C, are the real numbers computed in R.

Proof. By Proposition 1.1.2 and the remark after it, C satisfies (F1)–(F7) and (F9), and 1≠0 in C. By Proposition 1.2.3, every non-zero z∈C has an element z−1 with zz−1=1, which is (F8). So C is a field.

For a,c∈R, the sum and product are those of R by Proposition 1.1.1. The real negative −a satisfies a+(−a)=0 in R, hence in C, so it is the negative of a in C, by uniqueness of negatives (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (a)). If a≠0, the real inverse a−1 satisfies aa−1=1 in R, hence in C; and a≠0 in C, since ι is injective. So a−1 is the inverse of a in C, by uniqueness of inverses in the field C (Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1 (a)).

Everything proved for an arbitrary field now holds in C, without a new proof. From Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1: the inverse z−1 is the only y with zy=1, and (zw)−1=z−1w−1 for non-zero z,w; fractions z/w:=zw−1 add and multiply by the usual rules; and C has no zero divisors: zw=0 forces z=0 or w=0. By Proposition 1.1.3 (d), zn≠0 whenever z≠0. These are the facts that the rest of the chapter uses when it cancels a non-zero factor.

Remark. Chapter 2 collects fields as a notion with many examples, finite and infinite. This theorem is the one it cites for C, and R⊆C, with the closure just proved, is one of its examples of one field inside another.

In practice, a quotient is computed by “multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate”. For w≠0, Proposition 1.2.3 gives zw=zw−1=zw―|w|2, which is the fraction z/w multiplied by w―/w―=1 (here w―≠0, since |w―|=|w|≠0 by Theorem 1.2.2 (d), (e)) and simplified with the product rule for fractions (Number Systems: From N to R, Lemma 4.1.1 (b)): the new denominator ww―=|w|2 is real.

Example (Dividing Complex Numbers)

Write 13+4i and 5+i1−i in the form a+bi with a,b∈R.

Solution. For the first, |3+4i|2=25 and 3+4i―=3−4i, so by Proposition 1.2.3 13+4i=3−4i25=325−425i. Check: (3+4i)(3−4i)=9+16=25, so the product with 3+4i is 25/25=1.

For the second, |1−i|2=2 and 1−i―=1+i, so 5+i1−i=(5+i)(1+i)2=5+5i+i+i22=4+6i2=2+3i. This agrees with (2+3i)(1−i)=5+i from Example (First Computations in C): since 1−i≠0, the quotient is the only u with u(1−i)=5+i. Indeed, if u(1−i)=5+i, then u=u⋅1=u((1−i)(1−i)−1)=(u(1−i))(1−i)−1=(5+i)(1−i)−1, by (F7), (F8) and (F5) in the field C (Theorem 1.2.4).

Quick check. Compute |(1+i)(1−i)| in two ways: by multiplying first, and by Theorem 1.2.2 (d). Then compute 1/i.

Solution. Multiplying first, (1+i)(1−i)=1+1=2, so the modulus is |2|=2. By (d), it is |1+i||1−i|=2⋅2=2, since 22=(2)2=2. And 1/i=i―/|i|2=−i/1=−i; check: i(−i)=−i2=1.

1.2.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Define the complex conjugate z― and the modulus |z| of z=a+bi, where a,b∈R. Which earlier result guarantees that |z| exists?
  • (b) True or false, with a reason: zz― is a non-negative real number for every z∈C.
  • (c) Where exactly is the hypothesis z≠0 used in the proof of Proposition 1.2.3?
  • (d) Write 1/(1+2i) in the form a+bi with a,b∈R.

Solution.

  • (a) z―:=a−bi and |z|:=a2+b2, the non-negative square root. It exists by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4, since R is a complete ordered field and a2+b2≥0.

  • (b) True. By Theorem 1.2.2 (c), zz―=|z|2, the square of a real number, and squares of real numbers are non-negative (Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.5 (e) when |z|≠0, and 02=0 by Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 2.3.2 (b) when |z|=0).

  • (c) It gives |z|≠0 by Theorem 1.2.2 (e), hence |z|2>0, so that 1/|z|2 exists in R. Nowhere else.

  • (d) |1+2i|2=1+4=5 and 1+2i―=1−2i, so 1/(1+2i)=(1−2i)/5=15−25i. Check: (1+2i)(1−2i)=5.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Arithmetic)

Write each of the following in the form a+bi with a,b∈R, or as a real number where indicated.

  • (a) (3−2i)(4+i).
  • (b) 1+2i2−i.
  • (c) |(3+4i)(5−12i)|, without expanding the product.
  • (d) Re11+i and Im11+i.

Solution.

  • (a) (3−2i)(4+i)=12+3i−8i−2i2=14−5i.

  • (b) By Proposition 1.2.3, multiply by 2−i―=2+i and divide by |2−i|2=5: 1+2i2−i=(1+2i)(2+i)5=2+i+4i+2i25=5i5=i. Check: i(2−i)=2i−i2=1+2i.

  • (c) By Theorem 1.2.2 (d), |(3+4i)(5−12i)|=|3+4i||5−12i|=5⋅13=65, since |5−12i|=25+144=169=13 (13≥0 and 132=169).

  • (d) 11+i=1−i2=12−12i, so the real part is 1/2 and the imaginary part is −1/2.

Exercise (B2)

Let z∈C. Prove:

  • (a) Rez=z+z―2 and Imz=z−z―2i;
  • (b) z∈R if and only if z―=z;
  • (c) Rez=0 if and only if z―=−z.

Solution. Write z=a+bi with a,b∈R.

  • (a) By Theorem 1.2.2 (c), z+z―=2a, and 2≠0, so (z+z―)/2=a=Rez. Also z−z―=(a−a)+(b+b)i=2bi, and 2i≠0, so (z−z―)/(2i)=(b(2i))(2i)−1=b((2i)(2i)−1)=b=Imz, using 2bi=b⋅(2i) and (F5), (F8), (F7) in the field C (Theorem 1.2.4).

  • (b) z―=z means a−bi=a+bi, which by comparing imaginary parts holds if and only if −b=b, that is 2b=0, that is b=0 (as 2≠0 in R). And b=0 means z=a∈R.

  • (c) z―=−z means a−bi=−a−bi, which by comparing parts holds if and only if a=−a (the imaginary parts agree), that is 2a=0, that is a=0 (as in (b)).

Exercise (B3)

Let z,w∈C with w≠0. Prove that z/w―=z―/w― and |z/w|=|z|/|w|.

Hint: start from w⋅(z/w)=z.

Solution. Let q:=z/w=zw−1, so wq=z. By Theorem 1.2.2 (b), w―q―=z―. Here w―≠0: |w―|=|w|≠0 by Theorem 1.2.2 (d), (e). Multiplying by w―−1 gives q―=z―/w―.

Likewise, by Theorem 1.2.2 (d), |w||q|=|z|, with |w|≠0 by (e). Dividing by |w| in R gives |q|=|z|/|w|.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Exit check: the complex numbers form a field)

Prove Theorem 1.2.2 (b) and (d) and Proposition 1.2.3, and conclude that C satisfies (F8). You may use Definition 1.1.1, Proposition 1.1.2, Proposition 1.1.1, Theorem 1.2.2 (c) and (e), and the rules of the ordered field R, including Number Systems: From N to R, Proposition 6.1.4.

Solution. (b). Let z=a+bi, w=c+di. Then zw=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i, so zw―=(ac−bd)−(ad+bc)i. And z―w―=(a−bi)(c−di)=(ac−(−b)(−d))+(−ad−bc)i=(ac−bd)−(ad+bc)i. The two agree.

(d). By (c), (b), and commutativity and associativity of multiplication (Proposition 1.1.2 (a), (b)), |zw|2=(zw)zw―=(zw)(z―w―)=(zz―)(ww―)=|z|2|w|2=(|z||w|)2. Exercise (C2: Gaussian rationals)

Let Q(i):={a+bi:a,b∈Q}⊆C, where Q⊆R as in Number Systems: From N to R, Chapter 5 (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 5.6.3). Prove that Q(i) is closed under addition, negatives, multiplication, inverses of non-zero elements, and conjugation.

Solution. Under the identification of q with κ(q), κ sends sums, products, negatives and inverses of non-zero rationals to the same operations in R (Number Systems: From N to R, Theorem 5.4.1 (a), (c)), so the copy of Q in R is closed under +, ⋅, negatives and inverses of non-zero elements.

Let z=a+bi and w=c+di with a,b,c,d∈Q. Then z+w=(a+c)+(b+d)i, −z=(−a)+(−b)i and zw=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i have rational parts, and so does z―=a+(−b)i. If z≠0, then a2+b2=|z|2≠0 by Theorem 1.2.2 (e), and a2+b2∈Q. By Proposition 1.2.3, z−1=aa2+b2+−ba2+b2i, whose parts are quotients of rational numbers by a non-zero rational number, hence rational. So all five operations stay in Q(i). For example, (1+2i)−1=15−25i∈Q(i).

Chapter 2 calls a subset of a field a subfield when it contains 0 and 1 and has these closure properties. Q(i) contains 0=0+0i and 1=1+0i, and it is one of the examples there.
