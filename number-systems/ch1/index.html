The ZF Fragment and the von Neumann Natural Numbers

Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, and Sets and Functions, Chapter 2 took the natural numbers as a set N inside the familiar real line, with induction as an axiom (Logic and Proof, Axiom 4.2.1) and with their arithmetic and order borrowed from the real numbers. This volume builds the real numbers, so it cannot borrow from them: where, then, do the natural numbers come from? This chapter starts again from sets alone. Six axioms say how sets are made and when two are equal; with them we build ordered pairs, and so functions, out of sets. Then we build the natural numbers out of nothing but the empty set. Zero is ∅, and “one further” is the step x↦x∪{x}, so that 1={0}, 2={0,1}, 3={0,1,2}: each number is the set of the numbers before it. A seventh axiom, Infinity, says that the steps can be completed into a set, and the least such set is ω. From there, everything that Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, and Sets and Functions, Chapter 2 assumed about N becomes a theorem about ω: Peano’s axioms, induction, the recursion theorem, the laws of + and ⋅, and the order with its well-ordering. A final theorem says that any other system satisfying Peano’s axioms is a relabeling of this one, by exactly one matching, and the chapter closes with the rule by which the later chapters of this volume write N for ω and cite Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, and Sets and Functions, Chapters 2 and 3.

Two pictures. Sets and Functions, Chapter 1 adopted two working conventions: numbers are not sets (Sets and Functions, Section 1.2), and a pair is not a number, nor a triple a pair (the remark after Sets and Functions, Definition 1.4.1). They belong to the picture of Sets and Functions, Chapters 1 to 6, in which numbers are given objects. In this chapter every object is a set, so 0∈5 and 2⊆3 are true statements, and the pair (0,1) is the set {1,2}. The two pictures do not conflict. Sets and Functions, Section 1.2 said that nothing in the main line depends on how numbers are made, and Sets and Functions, Chapter 1 used nothing about pairs beyond their characteristic property and that convention; Section 1.2 proves the characteristic property for pairs built from sets. What survives of the conventions becomes a theorem: no pair is a natural number (Section 1.4), and no n-tuple with n≥3 is a pair or a natural number (Section 1.6). Statements such as 0∈5 are facts about this one construction; the matching of Section 1.7 carries 0, successor, +, ⋅ and <, and nothing else.

Roadmap

  • Axioms for Sets. Six axioms, (Z1)–(Z6), for making sets and telling them apart, why Separation needs a set to cut from, and the sets they name: the empty set, unordered pairs, unions, intersections and differences, each unique by Extensionality.
  • Ordered Pairs Built from Sets. Kuratowski’s pair {{a},{a,b}}, three shorter encodings that fail, the proof of the characteristic property with the case a=b on its own, the product A×B as a set, and the working rules (W1)–(W5) of Sets and Functions, Section 1.2 recovered from the axioms.
  • Successors, Inductive Sets and the Natural Numbers. The successor S(x)=x∪{x} and the numerals 0=∅, 1={0}, 2={0,1}, inductive sets and the axiom of infinity (Z7), ω as the least inductive set, and induction on ω proved as a theorem.
  • The Natural Numbers Satisfy Peano’s Axioms. Transitive sets, the facts that no natural number is an element of itself and that every non-zero one is a successor, Peano’s axioms (P1)–(P5) for ω, Peano systems with a structure for each of (P3)–(P5) that fails it alone, and the theorem that no pair is a natural number.
  • The Recursion Theorem; Addition and Multiplication. The recursion theorem proved on ω by gluing partial solutions on the sets S(n), with no order needed; addition and multiplication defined by recursion, n+1=S(n), and the laws of arithmetic, each by an induction on the variable the recursion runs on.
  • Order and Well-Ordering of the Natural Numbers. “Less than” defined as “element of” and proved a strict total order, each number the set of the numbers below it; well-ordering, by an induction on the numbers that contain no element of the set; order tied to arithmetic, with subtraction and induction from any base; powers and parity; and n-tuples and lists of variable length as sets.
  • All Peano Systems Are Alike. Isomorphisms of Peano systems, the categoricity theorem (every Peano system is a copy of ω by exactly one isomorphism), addition, multiplication and order carried along it, the identification of ω with Sets and Functions, Chapter 2’s N, and the dictionary by which the later chapters cite Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, and Sets and Functions, Chapters 2 and 3 on ω; the review exercises reach back to Logic and Proof, Chapter 4, and Sets and Functions, Chapters 1 and 2.

Named moves

  • Show the set is inductive. To prove P(n) for every n∈ω, form {n∈ω:P(n)} and show that it contains 0 and is closed under S. When the claim fails at 0, carry a disjunction “n=0 or …” instead.
  • Fix the others, induct on the recursion variable. In m+n and m⋅n the recursion runs on the second argument n; fix m (and any third variable) and induct on n. A fact about the first argument, such as 0+n=n, is a lemma to prove first, not a defining equation.
  • Build by recursion, compare by induction. A map out of ω comes from the recursion theorem; facts about it (one-to-one, onto, preserves +) are proved by induction, or by (P5) of the system it maps into.
