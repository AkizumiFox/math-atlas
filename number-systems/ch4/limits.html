4.3  Sequences and Limits in an Ordered Field

In Q the fractions 1/2,2/3,3/4,… “tend to 1”. Sections 4.1 and 4.2 supplied the tools to say what that means without leaving Q: an absolute value to measure distance, and the Archimedean property to make 1/(n+1) small. This section defines the limit of a sequence in an arbitrary ordered field, with tolerances taken from that field. It then proves that a sequence has at most one limit, and that a convergent sequence is bounded. Chapter 5 applies these results with F=Q, at a stage when no other number system is available. On the way it isolates the method behind the limit proofs of the chapter: estimate the distance, find a threshold, then write the proof forwards.

Throughout, F is an ordered field (Definition 2.4.1), and F>0={x∈F:x>0} is its set of positive elements, as in Section 4.1; for F=Q this is Q>0. As agreed after Proposition 4.2.1, we write n for n⋅1F, so 1/(n+1):=1/((n+1)⋅1F) exists and is positive (Proposition 4.2.1 (b), Proposition 3.2.2 (a)).

4.3.1. Sequences

A list 1,1/2,1/3,… is a rule that assigns a term to each position, and a rule of that kind already has a name: a function on N.

Definition 4.3.1 (Sequence; Eventually)

A sequence in F is a function a:N→F (Sets and Functions, Definition 2.1.1). Its value at n is written an and called the n-th term; the sequence is written (an)n∈N, or (an), and {an:n∈N} is its set of values.

A property P(n) of natural numbers holds eventually if there is N0∈N such that P(n) holds for every n∈N with n≥N0.

Examples.

  • In Q: an:=1/(n+1), with terms 1,1/2,1/3,….
  • In any F: an:=n, that is, n⋅1F, with terms 0,1,2,…; and an:=(−1)n, with the powers of Definition 4.2.1, with terms 1,−1,1,…. The second sequence has infinitely many terms but only two values: its set of values is {1,−1}. A sequence is ordered and may repeat; its set of values forgets both.
  • The degenerate case: a constant sequence an:=c for a fixed c∈F. It matters because every definition below must give the expected answer on it.
  • A sequence may be defined by recursion (Sets and Functions, Theorem 3.3.1, read on N=ω as Theorem 1.2.1 through Corollary 1.4.3 (b)). The Newton sequence of Example (A Rational Sequence Aimed at the Gap), x0=2 and xn+1=(xn2+2)/(2xn), is a sequence in Q; Section 4.6 studies it.

Non-example by minimal change. The formula n↦1/n defines no sequence: at n=0 it asks for 1/0, and 0 has no inverse. Everything else about the first example survives the change, and only “a function on all of N” fails. This is why the book’s sequences start at n=0 and why we write 1/(n+1).

“Eventually” absorbs any finite beginning. In Q, “1/(n+1)<1/10” holds eventually, from N0=10 on, although it fails for n≤9; “(−1)n=1” does not hold eventually, since it fails at every odd n. Two eventual properties hold together eventually: if P(n) holds for n≥N0 and Q(n) for n≥N1, then both hold for n≥max{N0,N1}, the larger of the two numbers, which exists by trichotomy in N (Theorem 1.3.2 (a)). The same goes for any finite number of properties, taking the larger number one comparison at a time.

↺Second readingInfinitely many properties

For infinitely many properties it fails: “1/(n+1)<1/(k+1)” holds eventually for each k∈N, from N0=k+1 on, but no single N0 serves every k, since k=N0 fails at n=N0.

4.3.2. The limit of a sequence

We want a precise sense in which 1/2,2/3,3/4,… “tends to 1” in Q. The first idea is “the terms get closer and closer to 1”. Here is why it fails. With an:=(n+1)/(n+2), 1−an=1n+2,2−an=n+3n+2=1+1n+2. Both distances shrink at every step, so the terms get “closer and closer” to 2 as well. What singles out 1 is that the distance to 1 falls below any tolerance we name, and stays below it, while the distance to 2 never falls below 1. The tolerances must come from F itself, since in Q there is nothing else to measure with.

Every tolerance in F is eventually met.

Definition 4.3.2 (Limit of a Sequence in an Ordered Field)

Let (an) be a sequence in F and a∈F. The sequence converges to a in F, written an→a, if for every ε∈F>0 there exists N∈N such that |an−a|<ε for every n∈N with n≥N. In symbols, ∀ε∈F>0  ∃N∈N  ∀n∈N  (n≥N⇒|an−a|<ε). Then a is a limit of (an). The sequence is convergent if it converges to some a∈F, and divergent otherwise.

In words (we say a limit until Theorem 4.3.4): the first clause hands us an arbitrary tolerance ε, a positive element of F. The second says that some index N can be found, and it may depend on ε. The third says that from N on, every term lies within ε of a: the inequality holds eventually. The absolute value is that of Definition 4.1.1, and by Proposition 4.1.3 (d) the condition on each term reads |an−a|<ε⟺a−ε<an<a+ε.

The domain of ε is part of the definition. For F=Q, ε is a rational number, and “an→a in Q” mentions nothing outside Q.

Negating the definition, quantifier by quantifier (Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.3 for the bounded quantifiers, Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.2 for the implication), and using that ¬(x<ε) means x≥ε in the totally ordered F (Proposition 4.1.2 (a)): an↛a means ∃ε∈F>0  ∀N∈N  ∃n∈N  (n≥N∧|an−a|≥ε). One bad tolerance, and beyond every threshold a term that misses it. A sequence is divergent when this holds for every a∈F.

Example (First Limits)

Decide, from Definition 4.3.2, which of these converge, and to what.

  • (a) A constant sequence an=c in any ordered field F.
  • (b) an=1/(n+1) in Q.
  • (c) an=(n+1)/(n+2) in Q.
  • (d) an=(−1)n in any ordered field F.

Solution.

  • (a) an→c. Let ε∈F>0 and take N:=0. For every n, |an−c|=|0|=0<ε. Here N does not depend on ε at all: the degenerate case.

  • (b) an→0. The field Q is Archimedean (Definition 4.2.2, by Theorem 3.2.3). Let ε∈Q>0. By Proposition 4.2.3 there is N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε for every m≥N. For n≥N also n+1≥N, so |an−0|=1/(n+1)<ε, where the absolute value drops because 1/(n+1)>0. The N depends on ε, and Archimedes is what supplies it.

  • (c) an→1. By the computation above the definition, |an−1|=1/(n+2). Given ε∈Q>0, take the N of (b). For n≥N, n+2>n+1≥N, so 1/(n+2)<ε.

  • (d) Divergent, in every ordered field. By the recursion for powers, (−1)n+1=(−1)n(−1)=−(−1)n (Proposition 2.3.2 (c)), and |(−1)n|=1 for every n, by induction with Proposition 4.1.3 (b). Hence |an+1−an|=|−2an|=2|an|=2 for every n. Suppose an→a for some a∈F. Take ε:=1 and its N, and let n:=N, so that n and n+1 are both ≥N. The three-point triangle inequality (Proposition 4.1.3 (e)) gives 2=|an+1−an|≤|an+1−a|+|a−an|<1+1=2, so 2<2, against (O1). So no a∈F is a limit. The bad tolerance is ε=1: two consecutive terms are 2 apart and cannot both lie within 1 of one point.

Quick check. For an=1/(n+1) in Q and ε=1/100, which N∈N satisfy “|an|<ε for every n≥N”?

Solution. Exactly the N≥100. By the comparison formula (Theorem 3.2.1 (a)), 1/(n+1)<1/100 if and only if 100<n+1, that is, n≥100. So every N≥100 works, and an N≤99 fails at n=N itself, where 1/(N+1)≥1/100. The definition asks only for some working N; any larger one works too.

Each clause of the definition is needed; the fold below shows what goes wrong when one is changed.

Warning. an→a does not say that some term equals a: 1/(n+1)→0 in Q (Example (First Limits) (b)), and no term is 0. Nor does it say that |an−a| decreases: an:=(1+(−1)n)/(n+1) in Q has 0≤an≤2/(n+1), so |an|<ε as soon as 1/(n+1)<ε/2, and it tends to 0; yet its terms run 2,0,2/3,0,2/5,…, up and down, hitting 0 at n=1,3,5,….

↺Second readingWhy this definition, clause by clause

Non-example by minimal change. Keep the sequence an=(n+1)/(n+2) and change the candidate limit from 1 to 2. The distance |an−2|=1+1/(n+2) still shrinks at every step, and still the definition fails, at its first clause: for ε:=1, every term has |an−2|>1, so no N exists. This is the negation displayed above, with ε=1 and n=N for every N.

  • Strict or not. Replacing |an−a|<ε by |an−a|≤ε gives the same notion: given ε, apply the weak form to ε/2, and ε/2<ε (Proposition 4.1.2 (b)).
  • Every n≥N, not some. With “some n≥N” the sequence (−1)n would “converge” both to 1 and to −1: beyond any N there are terms equal to each.
  • The order of the quantifiers. With ∃N before ∀ε, the condition says that an=a for every n≥N: for each such n, |an−a| lies below every element of F>0, so it is not itself positive, and it is 0 by Proposition 4.1.3 (a). (Exercise A1 (b) asks for the details.) Then 1/(n+1) would have no limit in Q.
  • Tolerances from F. In a field with more elements than Q, “every rational tolerance is eventually met” can be strictly weaker than “every tolerance in F is eventually met”. The fold after Proposition 4.3.1 (“Rational tolerances are not enough in Q(t)”) shows this happen.

Two kinds of sequence get their own names, because they recur throughout the rest of the chapter.

Definition 4.3.3 (Null Sequence)

A sequence (an) in F is null if an→0.

Definition 4.3.4 (Bounded Sequence)

A sequence (an) in F is bounded if there exists M∈F>0 with |an|≤M for every n∈N. Such an M is a bound for (an).

Examples. In Q, (1/(n+1)) is null and bounded by 1; in every F, ((−1)n) is bounded by 1 and not null (Example (First Limits) (d)); a constant sequence c is bounded by |c|+1, which is positive even when c=0. Non-example. In Q the sequence (n) is not bounded: for any M∈Q>0, Archimedes (Theorem 3.2.3) gives n∈N with M<n=|n|. The bound is required to be positive only for convenience, so that we may divide by it; any M≥0 that works can be replaced by M+1.

4.3.3. Proving a limit from the definition

In Example (First Limits) the distance |an−a| was 1/(n+1) or 1/(n+2), and Archimedes supplied N at once. Usually the distance is messier, and the threshold has to be found before the proof can be written.

Example (A limit found by working backwards)

Prove from Definition 4.3.2 that an:=n2+12n2+3→12 in Q.

Idea (Scratch work). The goal has the shape ∀ε∃N∀n≥N. So the proof starts “Let ε∈Q>0”, names an N, and checks every n≥N. Finding N is the work, done backwards from the distance. One fraction: n2+12n2+3−12=−12(2n2+3), so the distance is 1/(2(2n2+3)). Solving 1/(2(2n2+3))<ε for n exactly would need a square root. But no proof needs the least N, only some N; so we replace the distance by something larger and simpler. Since 2(2n2+3)−(n+1)=4n2−n+5>0 for every n, the distance is at most 1/(n+1), and “1/(n+1)<ε eventually” is Archimedes.

Givens Goal
ε∈Q>0 N with |an−12|<ε for every n≥N
|an−12|=12(2n2+3) N with 12(2n2+3)<ε for n≥N
12(2n2+3)≤1n+1 for every n N with 1n+1<ε for n≥N
Proposition 4.2.3: N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε for m≥N n≥N gives n+1≥N: the gap is closed

The proof runs the table upwards: given ε, choose N, check n≥N.

Solution. Let ε∈Q>0. By Proposition 4.2.3 (Q is Archimedean, Theorem 3.2.3) choose N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε for every m≥N. Bring the difference to one fraction: an−12=2(n2+1)−(2n2+3)2(2n2+3)=−12(2n2+3), so the distance is 1/(2(2n2+3)). We bound it by 1/(n+1). For n≥1, n2=n⋅n≥n⋅1=n (Proposition 4.1.2 (d), applied to 0≤1≤n and 0≤n≤n), so 2(2n2+3)−(n+1)=4n2−n+5≥3n+5>0; for n=0 the left side is 5>0. So 0<n+1<2(2n2+3), and Proposition 4.1.2 (c) gives 1/(2(2n2+3))≤1/(n+1). For n≥N, n+1≥N, so |an−12|≤1/(n+1)<ε.

Archimedes entered only in choosing N; the algebra of the distance holds in every ordered field.

The proof has a shape worth fixing as a method.

Example (Method: proving a limit from the definition)

Template. (1) Bring the distance |an−a| to one fraction. (2) Bound it, for all n≥n0, by C/(n+1) with C∈F>0, enlarging numerators and shrinking denominators. (3) If F is Archimedean, take N′ from Proposition 4.2.3 with 1/m<ε/C for m≥N′, and let N:=max{N′,n0}. (4) Write forwards: let ε∈F>0; let N be as in (3); let n≥N; then |an−a|≤C/(n+1)<ε.

When to use it. The candidate limit is known, the distance has a formula, and F is Archimedean (as Q is, Theorem 3.2.3); in a general F, step (3) needs another source of thresholds (Section 4.4).

The usual failure. Writing the backward computation as the proof: that proves the converse, entry (N5) of Logic and Proof, Example (What is not allowed). Choosing N before ε, or letting it depend on n.

Example (A limit with an oscillating numerator)

Prove from the definition that an=2n+(−1)nn+1→2 in Q.

Solution. The steps are numbered as in Example (Method: proving a limit from the definition).

  1. an−2=(−1)n−2n+1.

  2. (−1)n is 1 or −1 (Example (First Limits) (d)), so |(−1)n−2| is 1 or 3, and |an−2|≤3/(n+1) for every n (n0=0, C=3).

  3. Let ε∈Q>0. Then ε/3>0; choose N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε/3 for every m≥N (Proposition 4.2.3).

  4. For n≥N, n+1≥N, so |an−2|≤3/(n+1)<3⋅ε/3=ε.

The distance itself goes up and down: 1,3/2,1/3,3/4,1/5,…. Step (2) replaced it by 3/(n+1), which decreases, and only the bound had to be pushed below ε.

Quick check. In the proof just given, take ε=1/10. Which N does step (3) produce if N is taken least? What is the least N that works in the definition itself?

Solution. 1/m<1/30 exactly when m≥31, so step (3) gives N=31. In the definition: at even n the distance 1/(n+1) is below 1/10 once n≥10; at odd n the distance 3/(n+1) is below 1/10 exactly when n+1>30. At n=29 the distance is 3/30=1/10, not below it; from n=30 on every term qualifies. So the least N is 30, and the proof’s larger N costs nothing.

4.3.4. Archimedes and the sequence 1/(n + 1)

In Q, 1/(n+1)→0 came straight from Archimedes. In a general ordered field, the two statements turn out to be the same.

Proposition 4.3.1 (Archimedes as a Null Sequence)

The sequence (1/((n+1)⋅1F))n∈N is null in F if and only if F is Archimedean.

Idea. One direction is Proposition 4.2.3 read as a limit. For the other, a field that is not Archimedean has an element x above every n⋅1F; then every 1/(n+1) is at least 1/x, and 1/x is a tolerance that is never met.

Proof. (⇐) Let F be Archimedean and ε∈F>0. By Proposition 4.2.3 there is N∈Z>0 (depending on ε) with 1/(m⋅1F)<ε for every m≥N. For n≥N also n+1≥N, so |1(n+1)⋅1F−0|=1(n+1)⋅1F<ε, where the absolute value drops because the term is positive. So the sequence is null.

(⇒) We prove the contrapositive. Suppose F is not Archimedean. Negating Definition 4.2.2 (Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.3, and Proposition 4.1.2 (a) for ¬(x<n⋅1F)⟺x≥n⋅1F), there is x∈F with x≥n⋅1F for every n∈N. In particular x≥1⋅1F=1>0, so ε:=1/x∈F>0 (Proposition 3.2.2 (a)). For every n∈N, 0<(n+1)⋅1F≤x (Proposition 4.2.1 (b)), so Proposition 4.1.2 (c) gives |1(n+1)⋅1F−0|=1(n+1)⋅1F≥1x=ε. So for this ε no N works, even with n=N, and the sequence is not null.

So in Q(t), the field of Example (A Non-Archimedean Ordered Field), which is not Archimedean, 1/(n+1) does not tend to 0: the tolerance 1/t is never met.

↺Second readingRational tolerances are not enough in Q(t)

The same example shows why tolerances must come from F. In Q(t), every rational tolerance is eventually met by 1/(n+1). Write ι for the embedding ιF of Theorem 4.2.2. Since ι respects products, ι(1)=1 and ι(n+1)=(n+1)⋅1, we have ((n+1)⋅1)⋅ι(1/(n+1))=ι(1)=1, so the term 1/((n+1)⋅1) is ι(1/(n+1)). Given q∈Q>0, Example (First Limits) (b) gives N with 1/(n+1)<q in Q for every n≥N, and ι, being strictly increasing, carries this to ι(1/(n+1))<ι(q). Yet the sequence is not null in Q(t). For F=Q the two readings coincide, because there every tolerance is rational.

4.3.5. Changing finitely many terms

Whether a sequence converges, and to what, depends only on its terms from some point on. Two forms of this are used constantly: altering finitely many terms, and dropping the first k.

Proposition 4.3.2 (Only the Tail Matters)

Let (an) and (bn) be sequences in F, and a∈F.

  • (a) If there is N0∈N with an=bn for every n≥N0, then an→a if and only if bn→a; in particular (an) is null if and only if (bn) is; and (an) is bounded if and only if (bn) is.
  • (b) For k∈N, the shifted sequence (an+k)n∈N converges to a if and only if (an) does.

Idea. For limits, a threshold for one sequence becomes a threshold for the other once it is pushed past N0, or moved by k. For bounds, the finitely many terms before N0 are absorbed by a finite maximum.

Proof.

  • (a) Limits. Suppose an→a, and let ε∈F>0. Choose N with |an−a|<ε for every n≥N, and let N′:=max{N,N0}. For n≥N′, bn=an and |bn−a|=|an−a|<ε. So bn→a. The hypothesis is symmetric in the two sequences, so the converse follows by exchanging them. The case a=0 is the statement about null sequences.
    ↺Second readingThe bound part of (a)

    Bounds. Suppose |an|≤M for every n, with M∈F>0. If N0=0, the two sequences are equal. Otherwise let M′:=max{|b0|,…,|bN0−1|,M}, the maximum of finitely many elements of F, which exists by Sets and Functions, Proposition 3.3.8 (its proof works in any ordered field, as Sets and Functions, Section 5.3 remarks). Then M′≥M>0. For n<N0, |bn|≤M′ because |bn| is one of the listed elements; for n≥N0, |bn|=|an|≤M≤M′. So M′ bounds (bn). The converse is the same with the sequences exchanged.

  • (b) Suppose an→a, and let ε∈F>0, with N as in the definition. For n≥N, also n+k≥N, so |an+k−a|<ε. Hence (an+k)n converges to a, with the same N.

Conversely, suppose an+k→a, and let ε∈F>0, with N such that |am+k−a|<ε for every m≥N. Let n≥N+k. Then n=m+k for the natural number m:=n−k, and m≥N (Proposition 1.3.1 (a), (c)). So |an−a|=|am+k−a|<ε, and N+k is a threshold for (an). This proves the proposition.

The shift clause is about convergence only; it is used to start a sequence later, for instance to read a formula such as 1/n, which needs n≥1, as the shift by 1 of a sequence indexed from 0.

One more reformulation moves every limit to 0.

Proposition 4.3.3 (Limits Through Null Sequences)

For a sequence (an) in F and a∈F: an→a if and only if the sequence (an−a) is null.

Proof. For every n, |(an−a)−0|=|an−a|. So the two conditions of Definition 4.3.2, for (an) with limit a and for (an−a) with limit 0, say word for word the same thing, with the same N for each ε.

4.3.6. A sequence has at most one limit

If “the terms are eventually within every tolerance of a” is to name the number the sequence tends to, it must single out one a. It does.

Theorem 4.3.4 (Uniqueness of Limits)

Let (an) be a sequence in F and a,b∈F. If an→a and an→b, then a=b.

Try it first (ten minutes): suppose an→a and an→b with a≠b. Which ε makes the two tails collide?

Idea. The natural first choice is ε:=|a−b|, positive because a≠b; it is the choice that worked in Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.5. Beyond both thresholds a term an is within |a−b| of a and of b, and the triangle inequality through an yields only |a−b|<2|a−b|: true, and no contradiction. The two tolerances add. So give each half: with ε:=|a−b|/2, the same chain ends in |a−b|<|a−b|.

Proof. Let an→a and an→b, and suppose, for a contradiction, that a≠b. Then a−b≠0, so |a−b|>0 (Proposition 4.1.3 (a)), and ε:=|a−b|2∈F>0 by Proposition 4.1.2 (b); it depends only on a and b. Since an→a, there is N1 with |an−a|<ε for every n≥N1; since an→b, there is N2 with |an−b|<ε for every n≥N2. Let n:=max{N1,N2}. Then both inequalities hold at this n, and the three-point triangle inequality (Proposition 4.1.3 (e)), with |a−an|=|an−a| by part (b) of the same proposition, gives |a−b|≤|a−an|+|an−b|<ε+ε=|a−b|. So |a−b|<|a−b|, against (O1). This contradiction shows a=b.

Since a convergent sequence has exactly one limit, we may speak of the limit and write it liman, or limn→∞an. So liman=a means the same as an→a. In Q, Example (First Limits) gives lim1/(n+1)=0 and lim(n+1)/(n+2)=1, while lim(−1)n names nothing.

4.3.7. Convergent sequences are bounded

A convergent sequence cannot run off: from some index on, its terms are trapped near the limit, and only finitely many terms come before that index.

Proposition 4.3.5 (Convergent Sequences Are Bounded)

Every convergent sequence in F is bounded.

Idea. Use the definition once, with the tolerance 1: from some N on, |an|≤|an−a|+|a|<1+|a|. The terms before N are finitely many, so one of them, or |a|+1, is the largest absolute value in sight.

Proof. Let an→a. Applying Definition 4.3.2 with ε:=1∈F>0 gives N∈N with |an−a|<1 for every n≥N. For such n, the triangle inequality (Proposition 4.1.3 (e), with x=an−a and y=a) gives |an|=|(an−a)+a|≤|an−a|+|a|<1+|a|. If N=0, let M:=|a|+1. Otherwise let M:=max{|a0|,…,|aN−1|,|a|+1}, which exists by Sets and Functions, Proposition 3.3.8 in the totally ordered F (as in the proof of Proposition 4.3.2). In both cases M≥|a|+1≥1>0, by Proposition 4.1.3 (a), so M∈F>0. For n<N, |an|≤M because |an| is one of the listed elements; for n≥N, |an|<|a|+1≤M. So M bounds (an).

The bound M depends on the whole sequence, through a, N and the first N terms, but on no tolerance: it is one element of F, fixed once. Section 4.4 uses exactly that, in the product of two limits. The converse fails: ((−1)n) is bounded by 1 and divergent (Example (First Limits) (d)).

4.3.8. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Write “an↛a” in symbols, and name the law used at each step.
  • (b) What does ∃N∈N ∀ε∈F>0 ∀n∈N (n≥N⇒|an−a|<ε) say about (an)? Does (1/(n+1)) satisfy it in Q, with a=0?
  • (c) For an=(2n+1)/(n+1) in Q, find the least N that works in Definition 4.3.2 for the limit 2 and ε=1/50.

Solution.

  • (a) Start from ¬∀ε∈F>0 ∃N∈N ∀n∈N (n≥N⇒|an−a|<ε). The bounded negation laws (Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.3 (a), then (b), then (a)) move ¬ past the three quantifiers, turning each ∀ into ∃ and the ∃ into ∀. That leaves ¬(n≥N⇒|an−a|<ε), which is n≥N∧¬(|an−a|<ε) by Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.2. Finally ¬(|an−a|<ε) is |an−a|≥ε, by trichotomy in F (Proposition 4.1.2 (a)). The result: ∃ε∈F>0  ∀N∈N  ∃n∈N  (n≥N∧|an−a|≥ε).

  • (b) It says that an=a for every n≥N: the sequence is eventually constant, equal to a. Indeed, fix n≥N. Then |an−a|<ε for every ε∈F>0. If an≠a, then ε:=|an−a| is in F>0 (Proposition 4.1.3 (a)) and gives |an−a|<|an−a|, against (O1); so an=a. Conversely, a sequence with an=a for every n≥N satisfies the formula, since |an−a|=|0|=0<ε. The sequence (1/(n+1)) does not satisfy it: no term equals 0. It converges to 0, but its N must depend on ε. The quantifier order is the whole difference.

  • (c) an−2=(2n+1−2(n+1))/(n+1)=−1/(n+1), so |an−2|=1/(n+1). By the comparison formula, 1/(n+1)<1/50 if and only if n+1>50, that is, n≥50. So N=50 works, and every N≤49 fails at n=49 (which is ≥N), where |a49−2|=1/50. The least N is 50.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Limits from the definition)

Prove from Definition 4.3.2, following Example (Method: proving a limit from the definition), that in Q:

  • (a) 3n+2n+4→3;
  • (b) n+5n2+1→0.

Say where the Archimedean property is used.

Solution.

  • (a)
    1. 3n+2n+4−3=−10n+4, so the distance is 10/(n+4). (2) 0<n+1<n+4, so 1/(n+4)<1/(n+1) (Proposition 3.2.2 (b)), and the distance is <10/(n+1). (3) Given ε∈Q>0, choose N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε/10 for m≥N (Proposition 4.2.3). (4) For n≥N, n+1≥N, so the distance is <10/(n+1)<ε.
  • (b)
    1. The distance is (n+5)/(n2+1). (2) For n≥1: n+5≤6n; n2<n2+1 gives 1/(n2+1)<1/n2; so the distance is <6n/n2=6/n, and 6/n≤12/(n+1) because n+1≤2n. So the distance is <12/(n+1) for n≥1 (n0=1; at n=0 the term is 5). (3) Choose N∈Z>0 with 1/m<ε/12 for m≥N; then N≥1=n0. (4) For n≥N: n≥1 and n+1≥N, so the distance is <12/(n+1)<ε.

Where Archimedes is used: in step (3) only, both times.

Exercise (B2)

Prove that if an→a in F, then |an|→|a|. Show by an example that the converse fails, and prove that it holds when a=0.

Solution. Let ε∈F>0, and choose N with |an−a|<ε for n≥N. By the reverse triangle inequality (Proposition 4.1.3 (f)), for n≥N, ||an|−|a||≤|an−a|<ε. So |an|→|a|, with the same N.

The converse fails for an=(−1)n and a=1: |an|=1 for every n, a constant sequence, so |an|→1=|1| (Example (First Limits) (a)); but (an) converges to nothing (part (d) of that example).

For a=0 the converse holds: ||an|−|0||=||an||=|an|=|an−0|, by Proposition 4.1.3 (a), since |an|≥0. So the two conditions coincide: a sequence is null if and only if its absolute values form a null sequence.

Exercise (B3: Bounded but not convergent, in Q(t))

In the field Q(t) of Example (A Non-Archimedean Ordered Field), show that the sequence (n⋅1)n∈N is bounded and does not converge. Is (n) bounded in Q?

Solution. Bounded. By Example (A Non-Archimedean Ordered Field), t>n⋅1 for every n∈N. Also n⋅1≥0 (Proposition 4.2.1 (b), and 0⋅1=0), so |n⋅1|=n⋅1<t. And t>1⋅1=1>0, so M:=t∈Q(t)>0 is a bound.

Not convergent. Suppose n⋅1→a for some a∈Q(t). Take ε:=1/2, which is positive (Proposition 4.1.2 (b)), and its N. With n:=N, both n and n+1 are ≥N, and (n+1)⋅1−n⋅1=1 by Definition 4.2.1 (a). The three-point triangle inequality gives 1=|(n+1)⋅1−n⋅1|≤|(n+1)⋅1−a|+|a−n⋅1|<12+12=1, against (O1). So the sequence diverges.

In Q. No: by the non-example after Definition 4.3.4, Archimedes puts some n above any proposed bound. The same sequence is bounded in one field and unbounded in another. Boundedness is a property of the sequence in F, like convergence.

Exercise (B4: Prove or disprove)

Let F be an ordered field and (an), (bn) sequences in F. Prove each true statement from Definition 4.3.2 and refute each false one with a counterexample.

  • (a) If an→a, then (a2n)n∈N converges to a.
  • (b) If (a2n)n and (a2n+1)n both converge, then (an) converges.
  • (c) If (an) is null and |bn|≤|an| for every n, then (bn) is null.
  • (d) If an→a, then (an+1−an) is null.
  • (e) If (anbn) is null, then (an) or (bn) is null.
  • (f) In F=Q: if an→a and every an is an integer, then there is N with an=aN for every n≥N.

Solution.

  • (a) True. Given ε, take the N of an→a. For n≥N, 2n=n+n≥n≥N, so |a2n−a|<ε.

  • (b) False. Take an=(−1)n in Q. Then (−1)2n=1 and (−1)2n+1=−1 for every n (induction, from (−1)k+2=(−1)k), so the two subsequences are constant, with limits 1 and −1; and (an) diverges (Example (First Limits) (d)).

  • (c) True. Given ε, take N with |an|<ε for n≥N; then |bn−0|≤|an|<ε for n≥N.

  • (d) True. Given ε, take N with |an−a|<ε/2 for n≥N. For n≥N also n+1≥N, and |an+1−an|≤|an+1−a|+|a−an|<ε.

  • (e) False. In Q take an=(1+(−1)n)/2 and bn=(1−(−1)n)/2. Since (−1)n is 1 or −1 (Example (First Limits) (d)), their values are 1,0,1,… and 0,1,0,…, and anbn=0 for every n: the constant 0, which is null. Neither factor is null: for ε=1 and any N, one of N,N+1 is even and the other odd, and there |an|=1, resp. |bn|=1.

  • (f) True. Take N with |an−a|<1/2 for n≥N. For n≥N, k:=an−aN is an integer with |k|≤|an−a|+|a−aN|<1. If k>0, then k≥1 by discreteness (Theorem 2.4.2 (c); the order of Q agrees with that of Z on integers, by the comparison formula of Theorem 3.2.1 (a) with denominators 1), against |k|<1; if k<0, the same with −k. So k=0, that is, an=aN.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: A positive limit keeps the terms positive)
  • (a) Prove that if an→a in F and a>0, then an>a/2 eventually; in particular an>0 eventually.
  • (b) Give a sequence in Q with an>0 for every n whose limit is not positive. What does this say about the converse of (a)?

Solution.

  • (a) Let ε:=a/2, which lies in F>0 by Proposition 4.1.2 (b), since a>0. Choose N with |an−a|<a/2 for every n≥N. By Proposition 4.1.3 (d), for such n, an>a−a2=a2>0, where a−a/2=a/2 because a/2+a/2=a (Proposition 4.1.2 (b)). So an>a/2 for every n≥N.

  • (b) an=1/(n+1): every term is positive, and the limit is 0 (Example (First Limits) (b)). So “every term positive” does not give “limit positive”: the strict inequality is lost in the limit. What survives is the weak inequality a≥0, proved in Section 4.4.

Exercise (C2: Find the error)

Here is an argument for Proposition 4.3.5. Is the conclusion true? Is the argument correct? If not, name the line that fails, say what makes it illegal, and give a counterexample to the step. Then repair the argument.

  1. Let an→a. (2) Take ε:=1 in the definition of the limit, and let N be the index it provides. (3) Then for every n∈N, |an|≤|an−a|+|a|<1+|a|. (4) So M:=1+|a| is a bound for (an).

Solution. The conclusion is true: it is Proposition 4.3.5. The argument is not correct.

The failing line is (3). The first inequality is the triangle inequality and is fine. The second uses |an−a|<1 “for every n∈N”. Line (2) supplies it only for n≥N: it is the bounded universal ∀n∈N(n≥N⇒|an−a|<1). Instantiating it at n needs the line n≥N (the bounded universal applies only to n≥N), and for n<N no such line is available. Line (3) cites a line that is not there: entry (N9) of Logic and Proof, Example (What is not allowed).

Counterexample to the step. In Q, let a0:=5 and an:=0 for n≥1, with a=0. Then an→0, and N=1 is a legitimate choice in line (2) for ε=1, since |an−0|=0<1 for n≥1. At n=0, line (3) claims |a0|<1+|0|=1, but |a0|=5; and the M=1 of line (4) is not a bound. No counterexample to the conclusion exists: 5 is a bound.

Repair. Keep line (3) for n≥N only, and take M:=max{|a0|,…,|aN−1|,|a|+1}, the finite maximum of the proof of Proposition 4.3.5.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 2: says that the conclusion is true and the argument is not correct. - 3: names line (3) and the use of |an−a|<1 for n<N, where the hypothesis does not apply. - 3: gives a convergent sequence and a legitimate N for which the step’s inequality fails at some n<N, and checks it. - 2: repairs the argument by a bound that also covers the finitely many terms before N (a finite maximum, or a finite sum of |a0|,…,|aN−1| and |a|+1). Fatal errors (0 for the step): declaring the conclusion false; offering a counterexample to the conclusion; blaming the triangle inequality.
