2.6  The Gaps in the Rationals

The rationals now have everything Section 2.5 asked of them: they form an ordered field (Theorem 2.5.1), every rational lies below a natural number (Theorem 2.5.3), and no two rationals are neighbors, since their midpoint lies between them. Is anything missing? School geometry suggests an answer. The diagonal of a square with side 1 has a length whose square is 2, and no fraction seems to measure it. That picture is motivation only. This section proves the two facts behind it from the construction: no rational squares to 2, and the set of rationals whose square is below 2 has no least upper bound in Q. We also build a sequence of rationals that closes in on the gap from above without ever landing in it. A later chapter of this volume fills the gap.

2.6.1. No rational squares to two

Throughout, x2:=x⋅x and 2:=1+1, with 4=2⋅2 and so on; by the identifications of Sections 2.2 and 2.4 these are the natural numbers of the same names. Their sums and products may be computed in N, since the maps ι and j preserve + and ⋅ (Proposition 2.2.3, Proposition 2.4.4), and so may their comparisons (Theorem 2.3.4 (a), Theorem 2.5.1 (b)).

Logic and Proof, Chapter 2 proved that “2 is irrational” (Logic and Proof, Theorem 2.2.1), about the school rationals, by parity: an argument about even and odd integers. Parity is available here too, from Proposition 1.6.4 (d) for positive integers or from the integer part (Proposition 2.5.5; see Exercise R4). We prove the statement for the constructed Q by a different route, one that needs only the order and the least-element property of Z, and that adapts to other non-squares (Exercise C2).

Theorem 2.6.1 (No Rational Squares to Two)

There is no x∈Q with x2=2.

Idea. Work backwards from a supposed square root x=p/q. From x2=2 we get 2−x=x2−x=x(x−1), so x=2−xx−1=2−p/qp/q−1=2q−pp−q. Since 1<x<2 (as 12<2<22), the new denominator p−q is positive and smaller than q. So no fraction for x can have a least positive denominator, while Proposition 2.3.6 says some fraction must. The written proof checks the descent with integers only: it never divides, and it verifies (2q−p)2=2(p−q)2 by expanding. Before reading on: using only integers, why is the new denominator q′:=p−q positive, and why is q′<q?

Proof. Suppose, for a contradiction, that x∈Q and x2=2. Then x≠0, since 02=0 (Proposition 2.3.2 (b)) and 2>0. By (O1), x>0 or x<0; let y:=x in the first case and y:=−x in the second. Then y>0 (Proposition 2.3.5 (a)) and y2=x2=2, because (−x)(−x)=xx (Proposition 2.3.2 (c)).

By Proposition 2.4.4 (b), y=a/b with a∈Z and b∈Z>0, and a>0 by Theorem 2.5.1 (a). By Definition 2.4.2, y2=a2/b2, and 2=2/1, so y2=2 says a2⋅1=2b2 (Proposition 2.4.4 (b)). Hence the set D:={q∈Z>0:p2=2q2 for some p∈Z>0} contains b, so it is non-empty, and 0 is a lower bound of it. By Proposition 2.3.6, D has a least element q. Choose p∈Z>0 with p2=2q2.

q<p. Suppose p≤q. Since p,q≥0, Proposition 2.3.5 (g) gives p2<q2 if p<q, and p2=q2 if p=q; so p2≤q2, that is, q2+q2≤q2, and adding −q2 (O3) gives q2≤0. But q≠0, so q2>0 by Proposition 2.3.5 (e). This contradicts (O1), so p≤q fails, and q<p by totality (Proposition 2.3.5 (i)).

p<2q. Suppose 2q≤p. Since 2q,p≥0, (g) (or equality, if 2q=p) gives (2q)2≤p2, that is, 2q2+2q2=4q2≤2q2, so 2q2≤0. But 2q2>0 by (O4). As before, p<2q.

Descent. Let p′:=2q−p and q′:=p−q, integers. By Proposition 2.3.5 (a), p<2q gives p′>0, and q<p gives q′>0; so p′,q′∈Z>0 (Theorem 2.3.4 (b)). Adding −q to p<2q (O3) gives q′=p−q<q. Expanding with Proposition 2.3.2 (c) and (d), p′2−2q′2=(4q2−4pq+p2)−(2p2−4pq+2q2)=2q2−p2=0. So p′2=2q′2 with p′∈Z>0, that is, q′∈D. But q′<q, and q is the least element of D. This contradiction shows that no x∈Q has x2=2.

The answer to the prompt: q′=p−q>0 because q<p, and q′<q because p<2q. These are the two inequalities “1<x<2” of the Idea, written for integers. The hypothesis p2=2q2 is used in each of the three steps, and the minimality of q only once, in the final contradiction.

Remark. The proof assumes x2=2, reaches a contradiction, and concludes ¬(∃x∈Qx2=2): its last step is ¬I (Logic and Proof, Definition 2.2.1), not RAA (Logic and Proof, Definition 2.5.1), since what is refuted is the assumption and the conclusion is its negation. The steps “q<p” and “p<2q” refute p≤q and 2q≤p and then read the remaining case off trichotomy.

2.6.2. The set below the gap

Sets and Functions, Section 5.3 stated, without proof, that the set of positive rationals with square below 2 has no least upper bound in Q, and pointed out that the ambient set decides whether a supremum exists. We now prove it. By Proposition 2.3.5 (i) and Theorem 2.5.1, ≤ is a total order on Q, so (Q,≤) is a partially ordered set and the words of Sets and Functions, Section 5.3 apply: upper bounds (Sets and Functions, Definition 5.3.2) and a supremum, an upper bound s with (S1) x≤s for every x in the set and (S2) s≤u for every upper bound u (Sets and Functions, Definition 5.3.5).

Theorem 2.6.2 (A Bounded Set of Rationals with No Rational Supremum)

Let A:={x∈Q:x2<2},A+:={x∈A:x>0}={x∈Q:x>0, x2<2}.

  • (a) A is non-empty and bounded above in (Q,≤), but A has no supremum in Q.
  • (b) A and A+ have the same upper bounds in Q. Hence A+ is also non-empty and bounded above, and has no supremum in Q.

Try it first (ten minutes): a rational s>0 is offered as supA. Find a rational that proves the offer wrong, once in the case s2<2 and once in the case s2>2.

Idea. If s2<2, we need an element of A above s. The obvious try is s+h with h>0 small, but (s+h)2=s2+2sh+h2 has an h2 in it, and taming that needs a separate size estimate on h. If s2>2, we need a smaller upper bound, and a guess s−h runs into the same algebra.

A cleaner idea finds the new rational in one formula. If s2>2, then 2/s<s and (2/s)2=4/s2<2: the gap lies between 2/s and s. Their average t(s):=12(s+2s)=s2+22s is smaller than s, and its square is still above 2; that is the smaller upper bound. If s2<2, apply the same trick from the other side: t(s) overshoots, so u(s):=2/t(s)=4s/(s2+2) undershoots, and it lands above s. Each claim is an identity in the field Q, checked by clearing denominators. The proof uses Theorem 2.6.1 exactly once, to rule out the third case s2=2.

Proof.

  • (a) Non-empty: 12=1<2, so 1∈A. Bounded above: 2 is an upper bound. Indeed, let x∈A, and suppose x>2. Then 0≤2<x, so 4=22<x2 by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), and 2<4, so 2<x2, against x2<2 by (O1). So x≤2, by totality.

    Two maps. For s∈Q>0, we have 2s>0 by (O4), and s2+2>0 by Proposition 2.3.5 (e) and (b); both are non-zero. Let t(s):=s2+22s,u(s):=4ss2+2. Both are rationals, and both are positive, by Proposition 2.5.2 (a) and (O4). They satisfy

    t(s)2−2=(s2−2)24s2,s−t(s)=s2−22s,u(s)−s=s(2−s2)s2+2,2−u(s)2=2(s2−2)2(s2+2)2.
    (2.6.1)

    Each identity is checked by multiplying both sides by its denominator, which is non-zero, so that the multiplication can be undone with the inverse. For the first, 2s⋅t(s)=s2+2, so 4s2(t(s)2−2)=(s2+2)2−8s2=s4−4s2+4=(s2−2)2. For the second, 2s(s−t(s))=2s2−(s2+2)=s2−2. For the third, (s2+2)(u(s)−s)=4s−s3−2s=s(2−s2). For the fourth, (s2+2)2(2−u(s)2)=2(s2+2)2−16s2=2(s2−2)2.

    No supremum. Suppose, for a contradiction, that s∈Q is a supremum of A. By (S1) and 1∈A, s≥1>0, so s∈Q>0. By Theorem 2.6.1, s2≠2, so by (O1), s2<2 or s2>2. In both cases s2−2≠0, so (s2−2)2>0 by Proposition 2.3.5 (e).

    Case 1: s2<2. Then 2−s2>0, and the third identity of Eq. 2.6.1 exhibits u(s)−s as a product of positive elements (with Proposition 2.5.2 (a) for the reciprocal), so u(s)>s. The fourth gives 2−u(s)2>0, so u(s)2<2 and u(s)∈A. So A has an element u(s)>s, and s is not an upper bound of A, against (S1).

    Case 2: s2>2. The first identity gives t(s)2−2>0, so t(s)2>2. Then t(s) is an upper bound of A: if some x∈A had x>t(s), then 0<t(s)<x would give 2<t(s)2<x2 by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), against x2<2; so x≤t(s) for every x∈A. By (S2), s≤t(s). But the second identity, with s2−2>0 and 2s>0, gives s−t(s)>0, that is, t(s)<s. Then t(s)<s≤t(s), so t(s)<t(s), against (O1).

    Both cases are impossible, so A has no supremum in Q.

  • (b) Since A+⊆A, every upper bound of A is an upper bound of A+. Conversely, let u be an upper bound of A+. Since 1>0 and 12<2, 1∈A+, so u≥1>0. Let x∈A. If x>0, then x∈A+ and x≤u. Otherwise x≤0<u (Proposition 2.3.5 (i)). So u is an upper bound of A.

Conditions (S1) and (S2) for a set mention only the set of its upper bounds: (S1) says that s is one, and (S2) that it lies below all of them. So s is a supremum of A+ if and only if it is a supremum of A, and by (a), A+ has none. It is non-empty, since 1∈A+, and bounded above, by 2.

The set A+ is the set of Sets and Functions, Section 5.3, so clause (b) is the statement left unproved there. The two descriptions differ only by the non-positive elements of A, which lie below every upper bound anyway.

Remark. Analysis on the Real Line, Chapter 1 proves the same conclusion for A+ in Q, as part of its motivation for the axioms of the real numbers, and then shows that A+ does have a supremum in the real numbers. That is the sense of “gap”: a bounded set of rationals whose least upper bound, if it is anywhere, is not a rational.

Warning. “A has no supremum in Q, so A is unbounded” mixes up the two conditions a supremum needs. A is bounded above, by 2 for instance; what fails is (S2): among the rational upper bounds there is no least one, because every upper bound s is beaten by the smaller upper bound t(s).

2.6.3. A rational sequence aimed at the gap

The map t does more than refute a candidate. Started at an upper bound, it produces a smaller one, and it can be applied again. Iterating it gives a sequence of rationals that approaches the gap from above.

Example (A Rational Sequence Aimed at the Gap)

For s∈Q>0 let t(s):=(s2+2)/(2s). Define x0:=2 and xn+1:=t(xn) for n∈N, and let en:=xn2−2. Show that this defines a sequence with xn∈Q>0 for every n∈N, and that for every n∈N:

  • (i) en>0, that is, xn2>2;
  • (ii) en+1=en2/(4xn2)≤en2/8;
  • (iii) xn−xn+1=en/(2xn)>0, so xn+1<xn.

Compute x0,…,x3 and e0,…,e3.

Solution. The sequence. For s∈Q>0, s2+2>0 and 2s>0, so t(s)∈Q>0 by (O4) and Proposition 2.5.2 (a): t maps Q>0 to Q>0. The recursion theorem (Sets and Functions, Theorem 3.3.1, read on N=ω as Theorem 1.5.1 by Corollary 1.7.3 (b)), with X=Q>0, starting value 2 and g(n,s):=t(s), gives exactly one function n↦xn from N to Q>0 with x0=2 and xn+1=t(xn). So every xn is a positive rational, with no further argument.

Two identities. For s∈Q>0, 2s⋅t(s)=s2+2. Hence 4s2(t(s)2−2)=(s2+2)2−8s2=(s2−2)2 and 2s(s−t(s))=2s2−s2−2=s2−2. Multiplying by the inverses of 4s2≠0 and 2s≠0,

t(s)2−2=(s2−2)24s2,s−t(s)=s2−22s.
(2.6.2)

(i). We use Logic and Proof, Axiom 4.2.1 (read on ω as Theorem 1.3.3 (b), by Corollary 1.7.3 (b)) for P(n): en>0. Base P(0): e0=22−2=2>0. Step: suppose en>0. By Eq. 2.6.2 with s=xn, en+1=t(xn)2−2=en24xn2. Here en2>0 by Proposition 2.3.5 (e), since en≠0, and 1/(4xn2)>0 by Proposition 2.5.2 (a); so en+1>0 by (O4). This proves (i) for every n.

(ii). The equation was just shown, for every n. By (i), xn2>2, so 4xn2>4⋅2=8 by Proposition 2.3.5 (c). From 0<8<4xn2, Proposition 2.5.2 (b) gives 1/(4xn2)<1/8, and multiplying by en2>0 (Proposition 2.3.5 (c)) gives en+1<en2/8; in particular en+1≤en2/8.

(iii). By Eq. 2.6.2 with s=xn, xn−xn+1=en/(2xn), a product of the positive elements en and 1/(2xn); so it is positive, and xn+1<xn by Proposition 2.3.5 (a).

The first terms. By the definition of t, x1=4+24=32,x2=9/4+23=1712,x3=289/144+217/6=577408. The errors are e0=2, e1=9/4−2=1/4, e2=289/144−2=1/144 and e3=5772/4082−2=1/166464, since 5772=332929=2⋅166464+1 and 4082=166464. As a check on (ii): e1=22/(4⋅4)=1/4, e2=(1/16)/9=1/144, and e3=(1/144)2/(4⋅289/144)=1/(144⋅1156)=1/166464.

For orientation only (decimals are not part of this volume’s toolkit), here are the terms in school decimals:

n xn en=xn2−2 xn, roughly
0 2 2 2
1 3/2 1/4 1.5
2 17/12 1/144 1.41667
3 577/408 1/166464 1.414216

The terms decrease, every one of them is an upper bound of A (a positive rational whose square exceeds 2, as in Case 2 of the proof above), and by (ii) the error is roughly squared at each step: after three steps, e3=1/166464 is already below 1/100000. The terms crowd together. Yet no rational L can be “the number they approach”, for if they approached L, their squares would approach L2, and the errors en force L2=2, which Theorem 2.6.1 forbids. This paragraph is a picture, not a proof: limits in Q have not been defined. A later chapter of this volume defines them, with ε∈Q>0, and proves that this sequence is Cauchy in Q yet has no limit in Q, citing (i)–(iii) from this example.

Quick check. Is 3/2 an upper bound of A={x∈Q:x2<2}? Is 17/12? Is 4/3?

Solution. The first two are. Each is positive, and (3/2)2=9/4>2 because 9>8, while (17/12)2=289/144>2 because 289>288 (the comparison formula, Theorem 2.5.1 (a)). A positive rational b with b2>2 bounds A: if x∈A had x>b, then x2>b2>2 by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), against x2<2. (These are x1 and x2 of Example (A Rational Sequence Aimed at the Gap), and 17/12=t(3/2) is the smaller upper bound that Case 2 of the proof of Theorem 2.6.2 produces from s=3/2.)

But 4/3 is not, although it is positive: (4/3)2=16/9<2 because 16<18, so the hypothesis b2>2 fails, and indeed 7/5∈A, as (7/5)2=49/25<2 (49<50), while 7/5>4/3 (21>20).

This chapter built two number systems by one method. From N to Z: pairs (a,b), identified when a+d=b+c, so that subtraction becomes possible. From Z to Q: pairs (p,q) with q>0, identified when ps=rq, so that division becomes possible. Each time an operation was defined on representatives and checked to be well defined (Theorem 2.2.1), and each axiom was reduced to the level below. The third step, from Q to the real numbers, takes the same route in a later chapter of this volume: the representatives are sequences of rationals like the one above, identified when their difference tends to 0, so that the gap found in this section, and every other gap of Q, is filled.

2.6.4. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)

These questions are about the proof of Theorem 2.6.2.

  • (a) Where is Theorem 2.6.1 used, and what would go wrong without it?
  • (b) Run the proof on s=3/2 and on s=7/5: which case applies, which rational does the proof produce, and why does it refute s?
  • (c) Let F be an ordered field containing an element r>0 with r2=2, and let AF:={x∈F:x2<2}. Which step of the proof fails for AF, and what happens to t(r) and u(r)?
  • (d) State the idea of the proof in two sentences.

Solution.

  • (a) Once, right after a supposed supremum s is fixed: it excludes s2=2, so that trichotomy leaves the two cases s2<2 and s2>2. Without it there is a third case, in which neither map helps: (s2−2)2=0, so the identities give u(s)=s and t(s)=s.

  • (b) For s=3/2: s2=9/4>2 (as 9>8), so Case 2. The proof produces t(3/2)=(9/4+2)/3=17/12, an upper bound of A, since (17/12)2=289/144>2; and 17/12<3/2, since 17⋅2=34<36=3⋅12. So 3/2 is not the least upper bound. For s=7/5: s2=49/25<2 (as 49<50), so Case 1. The proof produces u(7/5)=28/549/25+2=28/599/25=14099, which lies in A, since 1402=19600<19602=2⋅992, and exceeds 7/5, since 140⋅5=700>693=7⋅99. So 7/5 is not an upper bound.

  • (c) The step “s2≠2”: in F the candidate s=r satisfies r2=2, and it falls in neither case. Indeed r is the supremum of AF in F: it is an upper bound (if x>r>0, then x2>2), and an upper bound v<r would have v>0 (as 1∈AF) and v2<2, and then u(v)∈AF with u(v)>v, as in Case 1. The maps fix r: t(r)=(2+2)/(2r)=2/r=r, since r⋅r=2, and u(r)=4r/4=r.

  • (d) Suppose a rational s were the supremum; since no rational squares to 2, either s2<2 or s2>2. The explicit rationals u(s) and t(s) then give, respectively, an element of the set above s or an upper bound below s, so s fails (S1) or (S2).

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Put the proof in order)

Here are lines of a proof of Theorem 2.6.1, shuffled, together with two lines that do not belong. Write the lines of a correct proof in a correct order, and say what is wrong with each line you leave out.

  • (i) Set p′:=2q−p and q′:=p−q; both are positive integers, by the two inequalities between p and q.

  • (ii) Write x=a/b with a,b∈Z>0; then a2=2b2.

  • (iii) Since p2=2q2, we have p<q.

  • (iv) Since q′=p−q<q, this contradicts the choice of q; so no rational squares to 2.

  • (v) Suppose x∈Q has x2=2; replacing x by −x if necessary, take x>0.

  • (vi) p<2q, since p≥2q would give p2≥4q2>2q2.

  • (vii) Choose such an x with the least positive value.

  • (viii) Among all pairs (p,q) of positive integers with p2=2q2, choose one with q least (Proposition 2.3.6; b is such a q).

  • (ix) Expanding, p′2−2q′2=2q2−p2=0, so p′2=2q′2.

  • (x) q<p, since p≤q would give 2q2=p2≤q2, that is, q2≤0.

Solution. An admissible order is (v), (ii), (viii), (x), (vi), (i), (ix), (iv). Lines (x) and (vi) may be exchanged, since neither uses the other. Otherwise the order is forced by what each line uses: (ii) needs the x>0 of (v); (viii) needs the pair (a,b) of (ii), which makes the set of such q non-empty; (x) and (vi) are about the pair fixed in (viii), and if they came before it, the pair might change afterwards; (i) needs both inequalities; (ix) needs the definitions in (i); and (iv) needs q′∈Z>0 with p′2=2q′2, from (i) and (ix), and q′<q, which comes from (vi).

Line (vii) is legal but does no work. There is only one positive rational x with x2=2: if 0<x<y had x2=y2=2, then Proposition 2.3.5 (g) would give 2=x2<y2=2, against (O1). So “the least” just picks out the x of (v) and gives nothing new. The descent does not produce a smaller x: it keeps the number and shrinks its denominator, p′/q′=p/q, so choosing x least never leads to a contradiction. What must be least is the denominator q, as in (viii).

Line (iii) fails. Its conclusion is false: from p2=2q2 and q>0 we get p2>q2, hence q<p by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), the opposite of (iii). It also clashes with (x).

Exercise (B2)

Let A:={x∈Q:x2<2} and A+:={x∈Q:x>0, x2<2}. Without looking back, prove that A and A+ have the same upper bounds in Q, and deduce from Theorem 2.6.2 (a) that A+ has no supremum in Q.

Solution. (⊆) for upper bounds: if u bounds A, it bounds the subset A+⊆A. Conversely, let u bound A+. Since 1>0 and 12=1<2, 1∈A+, so 1≤u and u>0. Let x∈A. If x>0, then x∈A+, so x≤u. If x≤0, then x≤0<u. So u bounds A.

Now suppose s were a supremum of A+. Then s is an upper bound of A+, hence of A (S1), and s≤u for every upper bound u of A+, which are exactly the upper bounds of A (S2). So s would be a supremum of A, which Theorem 2.6.2 (a) excludes. Hence A+ has no supremum in Q.

Compute x4 of Example (A Rational Sequence Aimed at the Gap), as a fraction of integers, and e4=x42−2. Check your value of e4 against (ii) of the example. Hence x4 is an upper bound of {x∈Q:x2<2} below x3.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Exit check: the gap at 2)

Prove that no rational number squares to 2, and that the set {x∈Q:x2<2} has no supremum in Q.

Solution. No rational squares to 2. Suppose x∈Q with x2=2. Then x≠0, and replacing x by −x if needed ((−x)2=x2), x>0. Write x=a/b with a,b∈Z>0 (Proposition 2.4.4 (b), Theorem 2.5.1 (a)); then a2=2b2. Among all q∈Z>0 for which some p∈Z>0 has p2=2q2, take the least (Proposition 2.3.6; the set contains b and is bounded below by 0), with its p. Then q<p (else p2≤q2 by Proposition 2.3.5 (g), so q2≤0, against q2>0) and p<2q (else 4q2≤p2=2q2, so 2q2≤0). So p′:=2q−p and q′:=p−q are positive integers with q′<q, and p′2−2q′2=2q2−p2=0 by expanding. This contradicts the choice of q.

Bounded above. 2 bounds the set A: x>2 would give x2>4>2 (Proposition 2.3.5 (g)). And 1∈A.

No supremum. Suppose s∈Q is a supremum of A. As 1∈A, s≥1>0, and s2≠2 by the first part.

If s2<2, let u:=4s/(s2+2)∈Q>0. Clearing denominators, u−s=s(2−s2)/(s2+2)>0 and 2−u2=2(s2−2)2/(s2+2)2>0. So u∈A and u>s: s is not an upper bound.

If s2>2, let t:=(s2+2)/(2s)∈Q>0. Clearing denominators, t2−2=(s2−2)2/(4s2)>0 and s−t=(s2−2)/(2s)>0. So t2>2, and t bounds A (an x∈A with x>t>0 would have x2>t2>2); and t<s. So s is not the least upper bound.

By trichotomy one of the two cases holds, and each contradicts the choice of s. So A has no supremum in Q.

Marking scheme. (10 points) - 4: no rational squares to 2: sets up a least denominator, or any other correct route (1); proves the key inequalities q<p<2q, or the parity step of another route (2); reaches the contradiction (1). - 1: shows that A is bounded above, with an explicit upper bound, and non-empty. - 2: the case s2<2: an explicit element of A larger than s, with both properties verified. - 2: the case s2>2: an explicit smaller rational, verified to be an upper bound of A. - 1: concludes by trichotomy, with s2=2 excluded by the first part. Fatal errors (0 for the step): assuming that 2 exists (in Q or in a larger field) and comparing s with it; “s2<2, so s+ε∈A for small ε” with no ε exhibited and checked. A second route for the first part, by parity (every integer is 2m or 2m+1, with m=⌊a/2⌋ from Proposition 2.5.5, and not both), earns the same 4 points: 1 for the least denominator, 2 for “p even, then q even”, 1 for the contradiction.

Exercise (C2: Which integers have a rational square root?)

For which n∈Z>0 does the set An:={x∈Q:x2<n} have a supremum in Q? Prove your answer.

Solution (Hint 1). First find k∈N with k2≤n<(k+1)2, and treat n=k2 on its own. For k2<n, adapt both proofs of this section: the descent, and then the two maps.

Solution (Hint 2). Let T:={m∈Z:m≥0, n<m2}. It contains n+1, it is bounded below by 0, and 0∉T; so its least element is k+1 for some k∈N. In the descent for k2<n, try q′:=p−kq.

Solution. Answer: exactly when n=k2 for some k∈N, and then the supremum is k.

Finding k. Let T:={m∈Z:m≥0, n<m2}. Since n+1≥1, (n+1)2≥n+1>n (Proposition 2.3.5 (c)), so n+1∈T; 0 is a lower bound of T; and 0∉T, as 02=0<n. By Proposition 2.3.6, T has a least element l. Since 0∉T and l≥0, we have l>0, so l≥1 by discreteness (Theorem 2.3.4 (c)); then l−1≥0 by (O3), so k:=l−1∈N by Theorem 2.3.4 (b). Then n<(k+1)2, and k∉T with k≥0 gives k2≤n. So either n=k2 or k2<n<(k+1)2.

Case n=k2. Here k≥1, since n≥1. We show k=supAn. (S1): if x∈An had x>k, then x>k≥0 would give x2>k2=n (Proposition 2.3.5 (g)), against x2<n; so x≤k. (S2): let v be an upper bound of An, and suppose v<k. Let w:=v if v≥0 and w:=0 otherwise; then v≤w and 0≤w<k. Let c be the midpoint of w and k (Section 2.5): w<c<k. Then c>0 and c2<k2=n by (g), so c∈An, and c>w≥v, so v is not an upper bound. Hence k≤v for every upper bound v, and k is the supremum.

Case k2<n<(k+1)2. Then n≥2, since n=1=12 is the other case; so 1∈An.

  • (i) No rational squares to n. Suppose x2=n with x∈Q; as in Theorem 2.6.1, we may take x>0, and then p2=nq2 for some p,q∈Z>0, with q least among all such denominators (Proposition 2.3.6). Multiplying k2<n<(k+1)2 by q2>0 gives (kq)2<p2<((k+1)q)2, so kq<p<(k+1)q by (g). Let p′:=nq−kp,q′:=p−kq. Then 0<q′<q. For p′, expand: q⋅p′=nq2−kpq=p2−kpq=p(p−kq)>0 by (O4); since q>0, p′≤0 would give qp′≤0 (Proposition 2.3.5 (c)), so p′>0. (The bounds alone do not give this: for n=5, k=2, q=10, p=28, we have kq<p<(k+1)q, but nq−kp=−6. It is p2=nq2 that forces the sign.) Finally, expanding, p′2−nq′2=(k2−n)(p2−nq2)=0, so q′ is a smaller admissible denominator, a contradiction.

  • (ii) No supremum. The proof of Theorem 2.6.2 (a) goes through with n in place of 2, using (i) where it used Theorem 2.6.1. The set An contains 1 and is bounded above by n (if x>n≥1, then x2>n2≥n). For s∈Q>0, let t(s):=s2+n2s,u(s):=2nss2+n. Clearing denominators as before, t(s)2−n=(s2−n)24s2,s−t(s)=s2−n2s,u(s)−s=s(n−s2)s2+n,n−u(s)2=n(s2−n)2(s2+n)2. A supremum s would satisfy s≥1>0 and s2≠n by (i). If s2<n, then u(s)∈An and u(s)>s; if s2>n, then t(s) is an upper bound of An with t(s)<s. Either way s is not the supremum.

So An has a supremum in Q exactly when n is the square of a natural number.

(10 points) - 2: produces k∈N with k2≤n<(k+1)2, from a least element. - 2: the case n=k2: checks (S1) and (S2) for k. - 3: the case k2<n: no rational squares to n, by a descent with a least denominator (or another correct route), including a proof that the new numerator is positive. - 2: the two maps (or any explicit rationals) and the two cases, as in the proof for 2. - 1: states the answer: exactly the squares of natural numbers. R. Review

Exercise (R1)

Prove, by induction on m, that for all m,n∈N: if m+n=m, then n=0. Use only the equations of addition (m+0=m, m+(n+1)=(m+n)+1, 0+n=n, (m+1)+n=(m+n)+1) and the fact that k+1=l+1 implies k=l. Do not use cancellation or any fact about the order: cancellation gives the claim in one line (m+n=m+0), and it is itself proved from the facts this exercise asks you to use.

Solution. We use Theorem 1.3.3 (b) (the form of Logic and Proof, Axiom 4.2.1 on ω=N, by Corollary 1.7.3 (b)) on m, for the property P(m): “for every n∈N, if m+n=m, then n=0”. Keeping “for every n” inside P(m) lets the step use the hypothesis for the same n.

Base P(0). Let 0+n=0. Since 0+n=n (Theorem 1.5.3 (a)), n=0.

Step. Assume P(m), and let (m+1)+n=m+1. By Theorem 1.5.3 (a), (m+1)+n=(m+n)+1, so (m+n)+1=m+1. By Corollary 1.5.2, m+n=m. By P(m), n=0. This proves P(m+1).

By induction, P(m) holds for every m∈N.

Cancellation (Proposition 1.6.3 (b)) and the order facts are deliberately not used: each gives a one-line proof, and cancellation is itself proved by peeling off one successor at a time with Corollary 1.5.2, the move of the step above.

Exercise (R2)

Show that the set Q, as constructed in this chapter, is countable.

Solution. The tools are the results on countable sets of Sets and Functions, Chapter 4, read on N=ω through the dictionary of Section 1.7 (Corollary 1.7.3 and the table after it).

Z is countable. N×N is countable (Sets and Functions, Theorem 4.2.6). The map a↦[a], from N×N to Z=(N×N)/∼, is surjective, since every class is the class of each of its elements (Sets and Functions, Definition 5.2.4). By Sets and Functions, Proposition 4.2.4, Z is countable.

Z×Z>0 is countable. Z>0⊆Z is countable by Sets and Functions, Proposition 4.2.3, and the product of two countable sets is countable (Sets and Functions, Corollary 4.2.7 (a)).

Q is countable. The map (p,q)↦[(p,q)], from Z×Z>0 to Q, is surjective (Sets and Functions, Definition 5.2.4), so Q is countable by Sets and Functions, Proposition 4.2.4.

(Section 1.7 lets a review exercise cite Sets and Functions, Theorem 4.2.6 and Sets and Functions, Corollary 4.2.7 (a), which run on ω through Proposition 1.6.4.) The conclusion is Sets and Functions, Theorem 4.2.8 for the constructed Q; that theorem, about the school rationals, is not used.

Exercise (R3)

Let A:={x∈Q:x2<2} and s∈Q. Write “s is a supremum of A in Q” in symbols, with bounded quantifiers. Then write its negation, moving ¬ inward as far as it goes and naming the law used at each step. The proof that A has no supremum in Q has two cases; which half of the negation does each case establish, and with which witness?

Solution. By Sets and Functions, Definition 5.3.5, “s is a supremum of A” is the conjunction of (S1) and (S2): (∀x∈A  x≤s) ∧ ∀v∈Q ((∀x∈A  x≤v)→s≤v). Its negation is, by Logic and Proof, Proposition 2.5.5 (d), ¬(∀x∈A  x≤s) ∨ ¬∀v∈Q ((∀x∈A  x≤v)→s≤v). By Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.3 (a), the first disjunct becomes ∃x∈A ¬(x≤s), that is, ∃x∈A  s<x (totality of ≤, Proposition 2.3.5 (i)). By Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.3 (a) and then Logic and Proof, Proposition 6.2.2, the second becomes ∃v∈Q ((∀x∈A  x≤v)∧¬(s≤v)), that is, there is an upper bound v of A with v<s. In words: s fails to be the supremum when some element of A lies above s, or some upper bound lies below s.

In the proof of Theorem 2.6.2, Case 1 (s2<2) establishes the first disjunct, with witness x=u(s)=4s/(s2+2). Case 2 (s2>2) establishes the second, with witness v=t(s)=(s2+2)/(2s). (The proof itself needs no negation law: it derives a contradiction from (S1) in Case 1 and from (S2) in Case 2.)

Exercise (R4)

Is there a rational number x with x3=2, where x3:=x2⋅x? Prove your answer.

Solution. No. The tool is descent on the denominator, as in Theorem 2.6.1, but run with parity, which Proposition 2.5.5 supplies.

Parity. For every a∈Z there is m∈Z with a=2m or a=2m+1, and not both forms occur. Indeed, let m:=⌊a/2⌋ (Proposition 2.5.5): m≤a/2<m+1, so 2m≤a<2m+2 (Proposition 2.3.5 (c)). The integer r:=a−2m has 0≤r<2; if r>0, then r≥1, and if r>1, then r≥2, by discreteness (Theorem 2.3.4 (c)); so r=0 or r=1. If 2m=2m′+1, then d:=m−m′ has 2d=1; but d≤0 gives 2d≤0, and d≥1 gives 2d≥2, and by discreteness one of these holds. Moreover an odd cube is odd: (2m+1)3=2(4m3+6m2+3m)+1.

Descent. Suppose x∈Q and x3=2. Then x>0: x=0 gives x3=0, and x<0 gives x2>0 and x3=x2⋅x<0 (Proposition 2.3.5 (e), (c)). So x=p/q with p,q∈Z>0 and p3=2q3 (Proposition 2.4.4 (b), Definition 2.4.2). Take q least among such denominators (Proposition 2.3.6), with its p. Since p3=2q3 has the even form, p is not odd (its cube would be odd), so p=2m with m∈Z, and m>0 because p>0 (m≤0 would give 2m≤0). Then 2q3=8m3, and canceling the non-zero factor 2 (Proposition 2.3.5 (f)), q3=2(2m3). So q is not odd either: q=2l with l∈Z>0. Then 8m3=2⋅8l3, so m3=2l3, and l<l+l=q. So l is a smaller admissible denominator, with m∈Z>0: a contradiction.

Why the look-alike fails. The substitution p′:=2q−p, q′:=p−q of the square-root proof came from x=(2−x)/(x−1), which used x2=2. For cubes the same algebra fails: expanding, (2q−p)3−2(p−q)3=−3p3+12p2q−18pq2+10q3, which is not a constant multiple of p3−2q3 (it has a p2q term), so this identity gives no way to derive p′3=2q′3 from p3=2q3, and the descent has no smaller pair to move to. Parity, by contrast, works for every power: “even cube, so even base” is the same argument for squares and cubes.
